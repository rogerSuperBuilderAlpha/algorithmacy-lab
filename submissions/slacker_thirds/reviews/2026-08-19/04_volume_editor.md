## 2. Step 0 — register and bar

The register is a first-person film-studies collection chapter in the Hansen/Bordwell manner — "I" steers, scenes precede theses, contractions and pointed concessions are licensed — and the chapter holds that register throughout. I take it as given and flag nothing inside it.

What I judge hard: whether the editors can recognize their commission in this text; whether the treatment of a co-editor's published position is collegial, fair, and right; whether a reader who bought a cult-film book can follow the theory; and the mechanics Bloomsbury will enforce. I do not judge prose rhythm, the defensibility of the Simmel or Royce readings, or citation page-accuracy beyond what I verified myself.

## 3. Part 1 — findings, ranked by damage

### F1. The chapter delivered is not the chapter described in the accepted abstract, and nobody has told the editors

The abstract we accepted promised, in its own words: "*Slacker* contains two distinct kinds of third: 'hosting' and 'engineering'"; Austin hosting "through the walkable downtown, subcultural density, and conventions of civil inattention (Lofland, 1973)"; a camera–algorithm parallel that is "formal and specific"; "I'll argue that cinema has trained viewers to read engineering thirds for a century"; streaming's encroachment on indie infrastructure; Möhlmann on interpretive skills; and closing questions about political forms. The chapter contains none of that vocabulary and none of those claims. Its own thesis sentence reads: "the arrangement divides into two jobs and a third act. Austin holds strangers in reach and introduces nobody; the camera selects which stranger the viewer sees next."

Why this damages my lens: the abstract is the working document on our desk. We sequence the volume from it, we draft the introduction from it, and Bloomsbury's acceptance was "pending editorial review." An editor who skims the abstract in October to place this chapter between two neighbors will describe a chapter that does not exist, and an editor who discovers the divergence unassisted — at the feedback draft, or worse at the final — will read the silence as either carelessness or management. The chapter can survive the divergence; the relationship cannot survive the silence.

The fix is not textual. It is P3.1 and P3.2: a disclosure paragraph folded into the confirmation reply the editors are already owed, plus a replacement abstract, sent before the October 1 feedback draft. The full ruling on whether this is the same chapter is Part 2(a).

### F2. One body-level stall for the cult-film reader: the translation philology

> "The postponement was already old when Wolff translated it: the chapter's first English serialization, in 1902, carries the same sentence as 'This society-constructing mediation of a third element is, however, to be treated in a later connection.'"

This is the one sentence in the body where the reader who came for *Slacker* is asked to care about the history of a translation. The claim it supports — that Simmel's promise sat unredeemed from the start — survives intact in a note, where the Wolff/1902 comparison already partly lives (notes 16 and 17 do exactly this kind of work). Everything around it is fine: "God and two believers never sit at one table" is the sentence that rescues the deferral argument for a general reader, and it should stay exactly where it is. Fix: P3.5, word-negative.

### F3. The chapter promises a complement "at the end" and never cashes it there

Line 13: "That reading is right about the characters, and this chapter ends by supplying it with a complement." The complement is real — "The characters can afford to be Episodic because something else is doing the joining" — but it is delivered in that same paragraph, and the conclusion never returns to the Episodics. As the person whose reading is being complemented, I notice a promised ending that doesn't arrive. Cheapest fix, P3.3: drop "ends by," so the sentence claims what the paragraph does rather than what the conclusion doesn't.

The body consistently makes the camera the selector — a fix a prior round closed (re-check item 20) — and this note re-names the selector "the cut." ## 4. Part 2 — the seat-specific audit

### (a) The ruling: same chapter, better chapter, or different chapter?

**A better chapter than the one we accepted, wearing about half of it.** Ruled plainly:

What survives is the skeleton, and it is the skeleton that made us accept the abstract. The question is identical — what holds a hundred unacquainted characters together in one legible day. The two-function anatomy is identical under new names: "Austin hosts while the camera engineers" became "Austin holds strangers in reach and introduces nobody; the camera selects which stranger the viewer sees next." Simmel is still the point of departure, Uber is still the destination, Stark and Pais still supply the co-optation sentence, and the present-day political payoff the abstract promised as open questions arrives in more concrete form than it promised — deactivation policy quoted from Uber's own pages, the RDU survey, the EU's legislated human being, Viljoen's collective remedy "in embryo."

What does not survive is the payload. Gone: hosting/engineering as terms; Lofland's civil inattention; Möhlmann's interpretive skills; the century-of-viewer-training claim; streaming's enclosure of indie infrastructure; the closing questions about political forms. Added: Simmel's postponed passage read as a distinct figure (the deferred third — a genuine textual find, and the chapter's real contribution); Royce's membership test; Reich's largess at the club door; the law of platform deactivation.

Is the trade favorable? Yes, and one substitution decides it. The abstract's weakest joint was the sentence "The parallel between Linklater's camera and Uber's algorithm is formal and specific" — an aesthetic selector and an economic broker differ in kind, and a referee would have opened the chapter there. The delivered chapter quietly refuses that analogy. It runs the platform comparison through Steve's door instead — "the four acts at Steve's door recur, act for act, in the documents of a platform he could not have named" — and the door, unlike the camera, actually is a grant-administering institution. That is not vocabulary drift; it is the author finding the argument the abstract was reaching for. The century-of-training claim, which the abstract could assert but 5,000 words could never have defended, is well lost.

So: **the answer to the panel's question 6 is "the skeleton yes, the promises no."** We would recognize this chapter as descended from the abstract; we could not honestly present it as delivery of the abstract. Would we have accepted the new chapter's abstract had it been the one submitted? Yes — it is film-first, it engages our contributors and one of us, and its platform politics are more concrete than what we accepted.

**What the author owes us, and in what form: both a note and a revised abstract, folded into the confirmation reply — not nothing, and not a resubmission.** The confirmation reply is already owed (terms, and the Bloomsbury AI policy). The disclosure paragraph rides in it; the replacement abstract rides as an attachment, because the introduction and the marketing copy get drafted from abstracts and the old one now describes vaporware. Timing matters more than form: before October 1, so the feedback draft lands as expected rather than as a surprise. P3.1 and P3.2 are paste-ready.

One sentence on the chapter's most attackable line, since an editor should know where the incoming fire will land: "I argue that this refusal and the platform's withheld reason are the same gesture seen at forty years' distance." Strictly, the film's refusal is aesthetic and the platform's is administrative, and "the same gesture" claims identity where the chapter has earned "the same shape." But the sentence announces itself as argument, the door supplies the middle term, and a cult-film collection wants exactly one sentence this bold. It stands. The author should be ready to defend it in review, not soften it now.

### (b) The Bizarro paragraph — judged by the person it engages

The paragraph: "On the Strawsonian reading, the film's people are Episodics … That reading is right about the characters, and this chapter ends by supplying it with a complement. The pressure belongs on its formal extension, the claim that the refusal itself organizes the film. One commentator draws the consequence: 'the frustration that viewers experience in the film's refusal to provide a coherent plot *is the point*.'"

**Collegial?** Yes. The reading is conceded flat out — "right about the characters" — the complement is offered rather than a correction, and the hard consequence ("is the point") is quoted from Marlovits, not from her. The concluding move even strengthens her thesis: "The characters can afford to be Episodic because something else is doing the joining" gives the Episodic reading an enabling condition instead of an objection. No co-editor reads this paragraph and feels handled.

**Fair?** Yes, with one complication the author needs to know about. I retrieved the post this session (see VERIFY). Both quotations are verbatim. But the post also contains this: "The structure of the movie is also non-narrative itself, since we follow one character and then another in a seemingly disjointed way with no overarching narrative." So the "formal extension" the chapter presses is not exclusively Marlovits's — Bizarro states a version of it herself. The chapter's carve still holds, because her sentence is descriptive (the structure *is* non-narrative) while the claim the chapter attacks is explanatory (the refusal *organizes* the film), and only Marlovits asserts the explanatory version. But the reader who checks the post — and Sara will — will find her own structural sentence sitting next to the criticized extension. No text change needed; the author should be ready to say the descriptive/explanatory distinction out loud if asked, rather than appear to have routed the criticism away from the co-editor by citation management.

**Right?** Yes. The shuffle argument is sound: a genuine refusal of coordination would be illegible, and "viewers who cannot say what the film is about can always say where they are" is both true of the film and the correct wedge. The refusal is total at the level of plot and cannot be the principle of construction; the complement is real work, not diplomacy.

**One editorial note for our own files:** since the chapter engages Sara's published position, Melissa should hold the editorial pen on this one. That is our housekeeping, not the author's; his disclosure need not mention it.

### (c) Length and shape

4,470 body words against a firm 3,000–5,000: inside the range, with roughly 530 words of panel headroom, of which this seat consumes none and returns 46.

The apparatus is the question. Thirty-nine endnotes and 52 entries is journal scale for a trade-facing collection chapter — but the mandate specifies Chicago with endnotes, notes and bibliography ride outside the word count, and the apparatus divides on inspection into two kinds. The honest kind — the hedges and IOUs in notes 2, 3, 10, 25, and 26, which disclose the subtitle-track debt, the unconfirmed witnessing, the paraphrased Berg, the unconfirmed club, and the chapter's own reading of Reich — is exactly what an editor wants to see in a feedback draft; keep every one. The philological kind — the German quotation and Simmel's subject index in note 19, the Koschorke parade in note 21 — is where the chapter reads most like a journal article. My ruling: invite pruning, don't require it. Specifically, note 19's German quotation can shrink to the locator plus the two examples, and note 21 can lose the parade after the two collection titles. Five to eight notes could merge or shrink without loss; none of this is a condition of acceptance.

The chapter's scene-first construction is its protection: the reader who came for *Slacker* gets the taxi monologue, the hit-and-run, Steve spelling his name at the list, the licked hand stamp, the Madonna Pap smear, the PixelVision camera, Gary, and Mount Bonnell, and the theory always arrives after a scene has posed its problem. The stalls, in order of severity:

It survives because "God and two believers never sit at one table" has already done the teaching two paragraphs earlier, and because the stipulation is load-bearing for everything after. Leave it.

On the mechanical pack's two jargon flags: *tertius gaudens* is glossed in-line ("the laughing third"), and *divide et impera* is glossed by its own predicate ("sets the two against one another in order to rule both"). I decline both flags.

### (e) Fit

The chapter earns its place, and in three ways an editor prices. First, the collection's title comes from the Les Amis hitchhiker, and the chapter stages that scene and gives it work — "the reach comes with no management attached" is a reading of the true call, not a cameo. Second, it cites and engages the literature the volume's other contributors will be working from — Macor, Małecka, Stone, Berg, and both ReFocus chapters — and it engages a co-editor constructively, which makes the volume read as a conversation rather than a stack. Third, it is the volume's likely theory chapter: the essays we would expect around it (fandom, Austin, production history, the sequel-adjacent films) will not duplicate it, and it gives the introduction a concept to point at. Fit is not the problem; the abstract is.
