## 2. Step 0 — register and bar

The register is a first-person Hansen/Bordwell collection chapter: "I" steers, scenes precede theses, contractions and pointed concessions are house materials, and I treat none of that as a defect. What I judge hard: whether every scene on the page is the scene in the film, and whether the chapter reads *Slacker* as well as a *Slacker* collection demands — no dialogue misquoted, no scene relocated, no timing invented, no rival reading shelved that deserved an argument.

## 3. Part 1 — findings, ranked by damage

**F1 (high). The Steve scene's outcome is misreported, and it is the chapter's centerpiece scene.** The body says: "He is charged, checked, and turned away, and arguing leaves him three dollars poorer." The conclusion repeats it: "a doorman who charges him, checks a list, turns him away, gives no further reason." The transcript supports neither verb. The doorman quotes a price ("Three dollars"), checks the list, and refuses the *listing* ("Sorry, Steve. Don't see you… You're not on the list") — but entry itself stays purchasable, and members of Steve's own party go in ("— You wanna go in? — Okay"). No line shows Steve paying; the nearest evidence points the other way — the next voice outside the club says "Couldn't get in," and three bucks goes to "Liquor store, quarts of beer, three bucks." So "turned away" overstates (he is refused the grant, not the door) and "three dollars poorer" asserts a payment the sources nowhere show. This is precisely the failure mode the last panel caught: a rewritten scene description nobody checked. The good news is that the accurate scene is *better* for the argument. Reich's structure is about the grant, and the film's door refuses the grant while leaving the priced entry open — the doorman severs status from access in one breath, which is a sharper miniature of the deactivation regime than an outright ejection would be. Fix in Part 3 (R1); the payment/entry question itself goes to the disc (VERIFY).

**F2 (moderate). Steve's bragging is relocated and its referent changed.** "At the door he claims the club keeps a spot for him because he is 'tight' with them." In the transcript both claims come while he parks the van, before the door, and the "spot" the club supposedly keeps is a parking spot: "This is my spot. They keep this here for me all the time… 'Cause I'm in, like, tight, you know. Tight." At the door he says only "What's up, dude? I'm on the guest list." As written, the chapter converts a parking brag into a claimed door privilege and moves it to the threshold. Corrected (R2), the scene actually serves the chapter better: the gap between the sidewalk brag and the door's ledger is the gap the sentence "Steve's error concerns what being on it would have been worth" needs.

**F3 (moderate). The day-arc of the two joins is neater than the film.** "Early on, the camera does the joining… By night the people on screen close meetings themselves… By dawn the camera is joining scenes on its own again." Two of the four "by night" exhibits are not night scenes: the lift ("How about a lift back into town?") belongs to the daylight junkyard-and-pickup stretch, and the burglars' "The address, man. Take me there" is at latest dusk. The middle of the day is also full of camera-joins (the walkers of the Drag), so "take turns" claims an alternation the film only trends toward. The claim the chapter needs — character-closed joins thicken as the day goes on, and the dawn joins revert wholly to the camera — survives honest phrasing (R3). Relatedly, the earlier universal "At every hand-off the film picks a single passerby from several available" is overstated by the same scenes: the burglars' hand-off follows a stated address, not a chosen passerby. One softening word there would not hurt, though I have not budgeted it.

**F4 (moderate, argument-level). "The monologues do not in fact travel: each dies with its speaker, unanswered by the scene that follows" — the chapter's own opening is the counterexample.** Two pages earlier the chapter says the film "has thus moved the passenger's dream into the street." The dream monologue is answered by the scene that follows, and the chapter built its opening on exactly that. The rebuttal of Price can survive, because no *listener* carries the dream anywhere — the camera does — but as written the two passages contradict each other, and a referee for the collection will see it. The fix (R5) turns the contradiction into support: the one monologue that travels, travels by the camera, which is the chapter's thesis in miniature.

**F5 (low-moderate). "Steve's list failed in public, and the group could renegotiate on the sidewalk."** The group that gets in free by stamp-licking is a different party (the cousin-from-Greece group) at a different venue (the five-dollar door). Steve's group did not renegotiate; it dispersed — some paid and went in, Steve stayed out. The sentence's real point — a sidewalk refusal happens face to face, a deactivation notice arrives after the fact — doesn't need the renegotiation claim, and is two words shorter without it (R8).

**F6 (low). The dawn sentence contradicts its own quotation.** "…with 'Wake up, America' from a passing car… and in those joins nothing changes hands and nobody addresses anybody." A loudspeaker shouting "Wake up, America" is nothing *but* address — address without an addressee. The chapter means no one is singled out, which is its own vocabulary of selection; say that (R4) and the dawn sentence rhymes with the thesis instead of tripping over its example.

**F7 (low). The Video Backpacker "once watched a stabbing."** He didn't, by his own account: "I didn't even see the knife impact on the body. I missed that part." He saw a stabbed man fall — and the missed moment is his whole complaint, the thing he cannot rewind to. The correction (R6) is two words and makes the rewind line hit harder.

**F8 (low). "The same gesture seen at forty years' distance."** The film is 1990/91; the Uber documents are 2016–2026 and the platform-work directive is 2024. Every anchor gives thirty-five years or fewer. "Forty" is the one number in the chapter's most attackable sentence, and it's wrong; "thirty-five" costs nothing (R7).

**F9 (hairline, flag only).** The café exchange splices two question-and-answer pairs: "You seen Gary around?" is answered "No"; "I told you. No one's seen him for months" answers the follow-up "Does he still live in the same place?" The sense survives the compression, and "the answer he has evidently gotten before" is licensed by "I told you," so I propose no edit — but the author should know the splice is there before the disc check. Same shelf: "rides in from an overnight coach" — the transcript says bus throughout, Walters says he "rides a bus into town one morning" (verified below), and "overnight" is inference; "bus" for "coach" would be more concrete and costs nothing if the author wants it.

**Clean sections for this lens.** The Simmel section's one film paragraph (the taxi passenger and the woman in the street) is accurate. The Gary sequence is rendered almost perfectly — every quoted line exact, the overhearing, the security check, the wave-along all as sourced — and the Paul hand-off, the true-call interview, the hustle economy, the PixelVision terms, the stamp scene, and the 2:00 eviction all check out word for word against the transcript, trims marked where made. The apparatus quotes (notes 2, 25) match. This is a far more faithful chapter than its predecessors; F1–F2 are the residue, not the pattern.

## 4. Part 2 — seat-specific audit

**(a) Is the three-answer carve-up fair?** Mostly, with two exceptions and one exposure. The relay (Macor via Małecka) and the shared-property answers (Małecka's ethic, Price's talk) are stated fairly and given real concessions before the gap is named. Price is slightly underrated — "conversation… the determining force" can be read as a mechanism claim, talk as the relay's medium, not just a shared property — but the amended F4 rebuttal covers him: talk determines nothing at the joins, because no listener carries it across one. Stone is fairly filed under drift. The exceptions: **Soldani is misfiled.** Her claim is not at bottom a camera claim; it is that a real scene — the Austin music-and-arts scene whose bodies fill the cast — performs itself into coherence. That is a *worldly* answer to "what keeps the next stranger available," the nearest rival the chapter has, and the chapter shelves it inside a drift clause. The concede-then-gap exit exists and is strong: a scene is an acquaintance network, and the film's pairings — the taxi passenger and the struck woman, the asker and the talker — are precisely the pairs no scene contains. One sentence (O2) buys the fairness. **Boom is an exposure.** The chapter's climactic line — "the drift concepts have no name for it" — sits one endnote away from an essay called "On Drifts and Swerves." If Boom's swerve names an authored, non-strolling deviation, a referee quotes the title back at the chapter. I could not retrieve the essay this session (UNVERIFIED), so this is a question, not a finding: the author has the *ReFocus* volume and should either show the swerve is still a walker's figure or trim "no name for it" to something the essay cannot contradict.

**(b) Does the drift critique land?** Where it aims, yes: characters in *Slacker* choose nothing about what we see, and no reading that locates the drifting in the walkers survives that. Two caveats. First, the sophisticated drift reading relocates the flâneur to the camera — a standard move since the city symphonies — and the chapter's grammatical-person argument doesn't touch that version; only its last sentence does ("Whatever performs that choice is doing something other than strolling"), and it asserts rather than shows. The showing is available and cheap: a flâneur can loiter, double back, retrace; this camera never returns — the chapter's own "it never goes back" is the anti-flânerie fact, and it should be aimed at the camera explicitly (O1). Second, "figures… in which the walker chooses where to turn" is attackable on the dérive specifically: Debord's drifter is *drawn* by the terrain as much as choosing. The critique survives because the dérive stays first-person — drawn or choosing, it is the walker's own feet — but the chapter should not rest it on "chooses."

**(c) Does the sociology crowd out the film?** I checked the 214-word run (the Royce definition paragraph) as a reader, not a counter. It holds: the paragraph is short-section philosophy that ends by handing the reader straight to the film ("The film administers the test in a scene built around a man who never appears"), and the Maori canoe keeps it concrete. A *Slacker* reader will not put the chapter down there. The stretch where the film thins most is the platform section's remedies paragraph (Review Center, EU regulations) — but it is one paragraph, the Video Backpacker anchors it mid-way, and the material is load-bearing. I decline to manufacture a finding; the chapter's sociology stays on a leash.

**(d) The owed close reading, and where it pays most.** The chapter already contains its best unexploited shot-level material, in the title scene. The moon-landing talker's exit is not just a comic wave-along: he stops a second stranger, performs a mock door — "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing" — and the film then follows the checked man home into the next scene (the voting-rant house; Gaughen's geography has the walk ending at 704 W 22nd, with the house at 18:30). If the disc confirms the continuity, the film has staged, at a hand-off, a man with no authority administering a gate — price zero, record imaginary, reason withheld, person present — and the camera ratifying his selection. That single reading fuses the chapter's two hard cases (the character who selects, the door that grants) inside the scene the chapter is named for, at near-zero setup cost because the scene is already on the page. Second choice, cheaper still: the film plants the guest list as a *working* institution in the morning — Stephanie's friend offers "If you wanna come, I can put you on the list," and the offer is accepted ("So, yeah, Friday night. Guest list?") — before the night door cashes the same institution as failure. One sentence turns the chapter's "controlled comparison" of two doors into the film's own before-and-after of one grant. Both need only the disc; neither needs a new section.

## 6. Verdict

**Minor revisions.** The single most important fix is R1: the chapter's jurisprudential centerpiece currently reports an ejection and a payment the sources do not show, and the corrected scene — grant refused, entry still priced and open — is both true and stronger. The biggest genuine strength is the Gary sequence and the two-doors comparison: the sidewalk pairing of asker and talker is read with a precision the *Slacker* literature has not managed, every line as spoken, and the argument grows out of the scene instead of landing on it. The one thing only the author can supply: the disc — whether Steve pays or stays out and who says "Couldn't get in," and whether the security-checked stranger is the resident of the voting-rant house, which decides whether the chapter's best available close reading is there to be written.
