# Review — urban sociology lens

**Verdict:** minor revisions
**Target:** chapter_v3.md (post-followup; panel 3)

## Step 0 — register

The first person, the scene-led argument, the blunt concessions, and the film-forward rhythm all belong to
this chapter. The review treats them as settled features. The current section is stronger than the prior
version: Lofland has returned, "cheap" has mostly become "slack," and the *Papachristou* holding is no
longer misstated. The remaining problems are smaller than the old ones, but they are still load-bearing for
section 3.

## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and structure

### 1. Austin is still too large a unit for the work "setting" does

Current text:

> What makes these scenes play as oddly plausible is the city, and Austin stages neither of them.

and later:

> Austin put these people into the same day; the walk between two of its scenes is not a walk anyone could
> take.

The chapter now says in section 2 that a city is made of foci: "the coffee shop, the Drag, the campus." That
is right. Section 3 sometimes forgets it and lets Austin as a whole supply encounter conditions. The census
and permit figures establish slack in the city. They do not establish the encounter surface. The encounter
surface is a smaller public realm: coffee shops, 22nd Street, 2nd Street, Guadalupe, campus-adjacent rooms,
club doors, sidewalks with enough shared subcultural code for a stranger to try a pitch.

Klinenberg would call some of this social infrastructure: the physical places that shape whether people
come together. Anderson's cosmopolitan canopy would raise a harder question: whether a place hosts
encounters across social difference or only among people already legible to one another. The chapter does
not need either term in the body. It does need the distinction they force. *Slacker* shows a narrow public,
not the city as such.

**Fix:** move the force from Austin-as-metropolis to Austin-as-foci. This is the section's own theory and it
costs almost nothing.

Paste-ready:

> What makes these scenes play as oddly plausible is the city, but not the city as a whole. They happen in
> foci: coffee shops, campus streets, downtown sidewalks, empty rooms within walking distance. Austin
> supplies the slack. These places supply the encounter surface, and none of them arranges a particular
> meeting.

### 2. The moon-landing scene is not an unbroken monologue

Current text:

> A man leaves a coffee shop and a stranger falls into step beside him, delivering an unbroken monologue
> about the moon landings that he cannot easily shake.

Source status: **unverified against the film in this session; checked against the local subtitle-derived
transcript only.** The transcript has the captive answering enough to matter: "Mm-mmm," "Yeah," "Yeah,
that's my birthday," "Sounds like most people I know," and then "All right, look. I gotta go see this
friend of mine, okay?" The chapter should confirm this against the disc before changing the sentence, but
the current wording is probably too clean for the interaction it uses.

This matters because the Goffman/Schegloff paragraph has the right ingredients even though it still needs
repair. A summons gets an answer; the answer opens a sequence; face-to-face refusal has a cost. The scene
works because the captive gives tiny answers and then tries to exit. Calling the speech "unbroken" makes
the later analysis less exact.

Paste-ready after disc check:

> A man leaves a coffee shop and a stranger falls into step beside him, talking about the moon landings and
> pulling just enough answers from him to keep the encounter alive.

### 3. The pap-smear pitch is not on the Drag, and it is not quite a cold approach

Current text:

> It licenses the white twenty-something pitching Madonna's pap smear on the Drag, and it has always been
> available against somebody else.

Source status: **verified this session for location.** Brendan Gaughen's live page places the Madonna pap
smear scene at "2nd St just west of Guadalupe," with a turned street sign on the 400 block of 2nd Street.
That is downtown, not the Drag in the usual Austin sense. The fix is simple: say "on a downtown sidewalk" or
"on 2nd Street."

Source status: **unverified against the film in this session; checked against the local transcript only**
for the interaction. The transcript also complicates "stranger." The pitch follows "Do you know Stephanie?"
and "Oh, yeah. I thought I heard that you were in 'Timberlawn.'" That is thin recognition, not anonymous
address. The point survives. It gets better if the chapter says what kind of public this is: not civil
inattention broken at random, but a subcultural sidewalk where weak ties and overheard names make a bizarre
sale attempt minimally hearable.

Paste-ready:

> It licenses the white twenty-something trying to sell Madonna's pap smear on a downtown sidewalk, and it
> has always been available against somebody else.

If the author wants the interaction made more exact:

> The pap-smear pitch is stranger than a cold approach. It starts from a name someone half-knows, in a
> sidewalk world where weak recognition is enough to hold the group for a minute.

### 4. *Papachristou* now has the right holding, but the paragraph still misses the selector inside the setting

Current text:

> A code that reads types and not persons is no neutral improver of odds. It licenses the white
> twenty-something pitching Madonna's pap smear on the Drag, and it has always been available against
> somebody else.

Source status: **official PDF retrieved this session; text extraction unavailable in this VM. The pin-level
quotation check comes from the project's prior verified research.** The current paragraph fixed the
two-ground vagueness holding. It still keeps the weaker theoretical claim. The ordinance reads types, but
the officer enforces it on persons: these bodies, in this car, tonight. That is exactly the chapter's
apparatus: a selector operating inside a setting.

This is the best urban-sociological complication in the section. It lets the chapter keep the definition of
setting and show why settings are never innocent. A setting supplies conventions and reach. Street-level
officers, bouncers, clerks, guards, and other human agents turn those conventions into selective action.
May then becomes more than a coda. His nightclub-door evidence shows the same nested form in commercial
space: a public-facing setting with a selector at the threshold.

Paste-ready:

> The ordinance read types. The officer read persons: these four, in this car, tonight. That is a selector
> inside a setting, standing at the edge of the public realm and deciding who gets to keep moving through
> it. What outlived the ordinance is the discretion. It licenses the white twenty-something trying to sell
> Madonna's pap smear on a downtown sidewalk, and it stays available against somebody else.

Add a note limit if May remains:

> May's evidence is an exploratory audit-pair study of nightlife entry in Austin, Dallas, and Houston. It
> supports the discretion claim; it should not be made to carry a frequency claim.

### 5. The slack paragraph is much improved, but "overbuilt" and "same fact" still outrun the evidence

Current text:

> Austin overbuilt through the mid-eighties and was still absorbing the surplus at the end of the decade:
> housing stock rose 48.0 percent between 1980 and 1990 while population rose 34.6.

and:

> Empty rooms and unstructured time are the same fact from two sides, and unstructured time is what this
> film is about.

Source status: **not re-retrieved this session; the project's prior verification checked the census and
permit tables.** The numbers support slack. They do not, by themselves, prove overbuilding. The chapter's
own note concedes changed city boundaries, and household size can shift stock faster than population
without proving surplus construction. The December 1989 multifamily-permit zero is useful but narrow; the
note rightly says Dallas and Houston fell to eight permits by May 1990.

The "same fact" sentence is the only sentence in section 3 still making more sociology than the evidence
gives it. Empty rooms make unstructured time plausible. They do not equal it. The missing bridge is rent
against wages, family support, student status, informal work, or subcultural tolerance for underemployment.
The chapter does not need to build that bridge in full. It only needs to stop saying the bridge is already
there.

Paste-ready:

> The material base was slack. Austin's housing stock rose 48.0 percent between 1980 and 1990 while
> population rose 34.6, though the city's boundaries changed over the decade.[^27] In December 1989 the
> Austin metropolitan area issued no multifamily building permits at all, against 632 in Houston and 260 in
> Dallas.[^28] Austin was not cheap: median contract rent on census day 1990 was $346, above Houston and San
> Antonio and below only Dallas among the state's big cities. What it had was room. The rental vacancy rate
> was 11.6 percent, roughly one unit in nine standing empty,[^29] in a city with 50,245 students at the
> university in the middle of it.[^30] Empty rooms make unstructured time plausible, and unstructured time
> is what this film is about.

### 6. Goffman and Schegloff are still treated as opposites, and they should be consecutive

Current text:

> The mechanism is not politeness. Erving Goffman's civil inattention — the glance and withdrawal by which
> strangers in public leave each other alone — explains why most people on that sidewalk do not speak. What
> licenses the monologue is the opposite rule, and Emanuel Schegloff describes it...

Source status: **not re-retrieved this session; the project's prior verification checked Goffman pp. 83-88
and Schegloff through the Gumperz and Hymes reprint.** The current paragraph still has the older level slip.
Civil inattention and summons-answer are not opposite rules. They are consecutive rules. Goffman explains
how strangers show recognition and withdraw from it. Schegloff explains what happens after a summons has
opened an encounter.

"Nonterminal, offering no natural exit" also overstates Schegloff. Nonterminality means the summons-answer
pair projects further talk. It does not mean an encounter has no exit. The strongest current sentence is
the next one, and it should stay:

> On a telephone you can let it ring and be taken for out; on a sidewalk you cannot, because the other
> person can see that you heard, so silence stops reading as absence and starts reading as a snub.

That sentence does the translation work a reader needs.

Paste-ready:

> Two rules run one after the other here, and the monologue lives in the seam. Erving Goffman's civil
> inattention — the glance given and withdrawn, by which strangers show they have seen each other and
> decline to make anything of it — explains why most people on that sidewalk do not speak.[^25] Emanuel
> Schegloff explains what happens once a summons gets an answer: the answer opens a sequence and commits
> the answerer to listening further.[^26] The moon-landing man exploits that. Schegloff closes the escape
> route at the other end, too. On a telephone you can let it ring and be taken for out; on a sidewalk you
> cannot, because the other person can see that you heard, so silence stops reading as absence and starts
> reading as a snub. Both sides of an opening cost something. That the cost falls harder on the person who
> wants out is my claim, not his.

## Part 2 — The four bans, and AI-slop audit

**Ban 1 — metaphor doing argumentative work.** One live hit: "Empty rooms and unstructured time are the
same fact from two sides." It is not only figurative. It carries an unproven causal bridge. R5 above fixes
it. "American law of the street" is acceptable because the next sentences cash it out with *Papachristou*.

**Ban 2 — throat-clearing.** Section 3 is clean. It opens with scenes and keeps moving.

**Ban 3 — meta-commentary / forward-backward reference.** Clean. "That the cost falls harder on the person
who wants out is my claim, not his" is attribution discipline, not meta-commentary.

**Ban 4 — unexplained jargon.** Much improved. Civil inattention is glossed. Assortative matching is
glossed. The telephone/sidewalk sentence translates the old "excusing inference" problem into ordinary
language. "Nonterminal" is glossed, but the gloss is too strong; that is a source-use problem more than a
jargon problem.

**AI-slop audit.** The earlier metronome has mostly been broken. The remaining antitheses in section 3 do
work: "not cheap," "types and not persons," "one documented instance and not a pattern." Keep them if the
sentences around them are revised. The only decorative one is "no neutral improver of odds," which R5
replaces with a positive claim about discretion.
