## Part 1 - Theoretical rigor and structure

### 1. Benjamin is still too cleanly converted into competence

**Verified this session.** I retrieved the Second Version through the Monoskop PDF text and checked the
same passage against the Stanford Encyclopedia quotation. The chapter quotes the training sentence
correctly:

> The function of film, he wrote, "is to train human beings in the apperceptions and reactions needed to
> deal with a vast apparatus whose role in their lives is expanding almost daily."

The next sentence in the chapter is the problem:

> His bet was hopeful; the training was to produce a public equal to the machinery.

That gloss is close, but it makes Benjamin sound like a theorist of skill acquisition. The sentence after
the one the chapter quotes gives the hope and the condition: dealing with the apparatus teaches that
technology will release people from enslavement to the apparatus "only when humanity's whole constitution
has adapted itself to the new productive forces." Benjamin's word-world is release, enslavement,
apparatus, productive forces. The chapter's "public equal to the machinery" shifts it toward fluency.

Paste-ready fix:

> His bet was hopeful, and the hope arrived with its condition attached. Dealing with the apparatus, the
> next sentence says, "also teaches them that technology will release them from their enslavement to the
> powers of the apparatus only when humanity's whole constitution has adapted itself to the new productive
> forces."

The note can keep p. 108 and add that the quoted sentence follows immediately after the training claim.

### 2. The eye-tracking paragraph still overstates what the studies show

**Verified this session.** I retrieved Loschky, Larson, Magliano and Smith through PLOS and Hutson,
Chandran, Magliano, Smith and Loschky through PubMed. The current Loschky quotation is supported: the
filmmakers influenced when and where viewers attended, and the *Moonraker* stimulus is a twelve-second,
six-shot clip. Hutson et al.'s abstract matters because the same line of work later asks what drives the
"tyranny of film" and reports that when the scene was shown as a slideshow, the context manipulation
attenuated attentional synchrony. Motion is doing some of the work.

The current paragraph ends:

> Taking a stranger's run of choices for a world went invisible as convention.

Neither Smith and Henderson nor Loschky et al. quite show that. One shows edit blindness. The other shows
gaze control despite different comprehension. Those results support a narrower and better sentence:
viewers often stop seeing the choosing. They do not prove that viewers take a run of choices for a world,
and the later motion result weakens the bridge from moving images to landing pages or dispatch systems.

Paste-ready fix after the Loschky sentence:

> Their own team later asked what drives that effect. Shown as a slideshow instead of a film, the same
> scene let context pull viewers' attention apart, which puts motion near the center of the result. A
> landing page has no motion in it.

Then replace:

> Taking a stranger's run of choices for a world went invisible as convention.

with:

> The choosing itself often stopped being visible.

New note if used:

> John P. Hutson, Prasanth Chandran, Joseph P. Magliano, Tim J. Smith, and Lester C. Loschky, "Narrative
> Comprehension Guides Eye Movements in the Absence of Motion," *Cognitive Science* 46, no. 5 (2022):
> e13131. I checked the abstract only.

### 3. Hansen is accurate but underused

**Verified this session.** I retrieved Hansen's "The Mass Production of the Senses" through a public PDF
mirror and checked the p. 59 period claim and the p. 69 "single most inclusive cultural horizon"
sentence. The chapter states her bounds correctly:

> though she bounds herself to 1920s-to-1950s mainstream Hollywood, and *negotiated* is the last of four
> verbs in a list that opens with rejected.

The sentence is not wrong. It spends Hansen mostly on a caution. Her useful term for this chapter is
reflexivity: cinema gave modernity a mass form in which its effects were reflected, rejected, disavowed,
transmuted or negotiated. That lets the chapter distinguish being trained from having a public scene in
which the training can be noticed.

Paste-ready replacement:

> Miriam Hansen gives that training a public scene. Cinema was, she argues, "the single most inclusive
> cultural horizon in which the traumatic effects of modernity were reflected, rejected or disavowed,
> transmuted or negotiated" - four verbs, and only the last is agreement. Her period runs from the 1920s
> through the 1950s and her cinema is mainstream Hollywood, so the century below is mine and not hers.

### 4. Beller is the missing expected interlocutor

**Verified this session at the abstract / publisher-page level.** I checked Project MUSE's summary of
*The Cinematic Mode of Production* and Beller's article "The Cinematic Mode of Production: Towards a
Political Economy of the Postmodern." Beller argues that cinema turns looking into value-producing labor
and brings the industrial revolution to the eye.

The chapter does not need Beller's apparatus. In fact, using him as the main witness would pull the
section away from its narrower claim about learned reception of selectors. But a critical-theory reader
will expect him once §6 says cinema trains spectators and §7 moves to platforms. A one-sentence bracket
would prevent a predictable margin note.

Paste-ready addition after Hansen or before the cognition studies:

> Jonathan Beller gives the stronger political-economy version: cinema turns looking into labor. I am
> not using that claim here, because my problem is narrower than value production by spectators; it is
> the learned habit of receiving an arrangement as if it were a world.

If this stays, add Beller to the bibliography. If space is too tight, put the bracket in a note instead.

## Part 2 - The four bans, and AI-slop audit

**Ban 1, metaphor doing argumentative work.** One live hit in §6: "Taking a stranger's run of choices
for a world" is doing argumentative work the cited studies do not do. The literal replacement above fixes
it.

**Ban 2, throat-clearing.** §6 passes. "Münsterberg, Benjamin and Hansen between them measured nothing"
is not throat-clearing; it is the hinge from theory to experiment.

**Ban 3, meta-commentary / forward-backward reference.** §6 passes. The transfer paragraph states the
absence and then names the closest available evidence.

**Ban 4, unexplained jargon.** The earlier "diegetic sound" and "180-degree rule" problem is fixed in the
current text. The section now says "sound that belonged to the world on screen" and "cuts that jump
across a line the convention says a camera should not cross." Good.

**AI-slop audit.** The section is much cleaner than the prior version. Two small tics remain but do not
need a rewrite on their own: "rather than" appears in the learned/unlearned contrast and in the closing
"resource ... rather than part" sentence, and both contrasts are doing work. The paragraph-openers vary.
No action required.
