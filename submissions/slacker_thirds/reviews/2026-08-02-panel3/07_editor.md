# Review — collection editor

**Verdict:** major revisions
**Target:** chapter_v3.md (post-followup; panel 3)

## Step 0 — register

The first person belongs here. The chapter is written as a film essay in which the author looks, follows
a problem, and changes scale. That register is an asset. The collection-editor question is narrower:
whether the chapter now fits the accepted abstract, keeps *Slacker* in view, earns a place in a Linklater
volume, and gives the press a defensible reason to accept a chapter that runs about 6,100 body words
against a 5,000-word ceiling.

## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and structure

1. **The chapter belongs in the collection, but it still has to behave like a chapter about
   *Slacker*.**

   The core argument is strong and collection-worthy. The chapter sees something real in the film: Austin
   puts strangers within reach, and the camera chooses which stranger reaches the viewer next. This is
   exactly the kind of claim a Linklater collection should want, because it makes *Slacker* central to a
   problem larger than *Slacker* without treating the film as an example tacked onto a theory.

   The chapter states the film claim cleanly:

   > Austin in 1990 is a setting. Linklater's camera is a selector.

   And §4 earns it:

   > In presentation it is an ordered walk: scene one, then scene two, in that sequence, chosen.

   This should stay. The revision problem is proportion. The current chapter repeatedly leaves the film
   for long runs of disciplinary settlement. In a Linklater volume, the theory has to keep returning to
   what Linklater made the camera do.

   **Editorial direction:** keep the argument, reduce the apparatus, and spend any remaining over-ceiling
   words on the film's movement, bodies, sound, and transfers.

2. **The terminology change from the accepted abstract is approved, but it must be flagged to the
   editor.**

   The accepted abstract used "hosting" and "engineering." The chapter now uses "setting" and
   "selector." That change is sound. "Setting" is plainer than "host," and "selector" is more exact than
   "engineering" for the camera, Uber, and the streaming landing page. The new pair also prevents a
   metaphor from carrying the distinction.

   The chapter's definitions are strong:

   > A setting puts people within reach of one another and supplies the conventions that let one approach
   > another. It arranges no particular meeting.

   > Against it, a selector chooses: which two parties come together, or which one an audience meets next.

   Do not revert to the abstract's terms. Do send the editor_query note before submission, because the
   accepted abstract circulated one vocabulary and the manuscript's title now announces another.

   **Paste-ready note to the collection editor:**

   > The accepted abstract distinguished "hosting" thirds from "engineering" thirds. The chapter now calls
   > these a setting and a selector. The argument is unchanged, but the new terms are plainer and more
   > precise: Austin supplies a setting, while the camera and later platforms select.

3. **The word overage cannot be defended in its current allocation.**

   The brief puts the body near 6,090 words against a 5,000-word ceiling. A simple current-file count in
   this session came out at 6,122, depending on counting rules. Either way, the chapter is about 1,100
   words over. That is too much to treat as a rounding error in an edited collection.

   The overage is defensible only if the excess is visibly film work. At present, much of it is not. The
   most expensive stretches for a collection reader are:

   - §2 from "What I am claiming is narrower than it first looks" through the *PNAS* paragraph.
   - §3's agglomeration-economics paragraph.
   - §5's surge-pricing caveat and deactivation-law detail.
   - §6's algorithmic-literacy search inventory.

   The Sundance paragraph in §1 should stay. It protects the premise from an obvious objection. The Cameron
   paragraph in §5 should also stay, because it answers the strongest objection to the platform comparison.
   The cut ledger is right about those two. The rest of the cut should come from literature audit and
   platform detail.

   **Cut target:** get the body below 5,300 before asking for an exception. The collection can defend a
   5,250-word chapter that spends its extra words on *Slacker*. It cannot defend a 6,100-word chapter whose
   extra words explain the sociology of brokerage to readers who opened a Linklater book.

4. **§2 is the main pacing problem.**

   §2 needs Simmel, Feld, Lofland, and the diagnostic questions. It does not need to keep the full novelty
   audit in the body. The chapter currently says:

   > What I am claiming is narrower than it first looks, and it has prior art I should name.

   That sentence begins a defensive passage addressed to a disciplinary referee. The reader has already
   accepted the useful distinction by then. Move the audit to a note and let the body return sooner to
   Austin and the camera.

   **Paste-ready body replacement for the audit block:**

   > The broker and the setting usually sit in separate literatures. Rivera, Soderstrom, and Uzzi bring
   > proximity and brokerage onto one map, but they ask why ties form. This chapter asks what a third knows
   > about the parties it coordinates. The note gives the disciplinary check.

   Move the Small-Gose, Finkel, three-empty-sets, and Rosenfeld material to a long note. Preserve it there.
   It is good scholarship; it is bad pacing in the body.

5. **§4 is the chapter's reason for existing, but it still leans too much on others describing the
   handoff.**

   The first handoff is the film's proof of concept. The chapter describes the narrative choice well:

   > A conventional film lives there for ninety minutes; this one gives it about two, then leaves the son
   > mid-arrest for a bystander who happened to watch.

   Then Chris Walters supplies the visual description:

   > "The camera eye slowly pulls back until it sees another nameless guy ... and follows him upstairs into
   > another story already in progress."

   Walters is useful evidence, but he should not be the only person in the chapter who looks at the
   camera's action closely. A chapter whose thesis is that the camera selects needs one sentence sequence
   from the author's own viewing: where the camera stands, when the old figure stops mattering, when the
   new figure becomes the route, and whether sound helps carry the transfer.

   **Author-only insertion after the son/bystander sentence:** add 90-120 words from the disc, with
   timecode if the collection permits it. The needed content is simple: position, movement, duration,
   sound, and the instant of commitment to the new body.

6. **§5 proves more than the chapter needs to prove.**

   The accepted abstract promises that Uber does not merely host coordination; it brokers matches and
   enforces terms. The current §5 proves that claim, then keeps proving adjacent claims. The result is a
   strong platform-studies mini-essay inside a film chapter.

   Keep the three powers:

   > it prices each match

   > it reads the parties

   > it can remove a party

   But compress the support. The reader needs enough detail to see why the camera is a weak selector and
   Uber is a powerful one. The reader does not need the full surge-pricing counterexample and both
   deactivation-law directions in the body.

   **Paste-ready replacement for the opening frame of §5:**

   > Uber shows what a selector can do once selection becomes infrastructure. Linklater's camera can pick
   > a body from the street and leave another body behind. Uber can price the match, read the parties, and
   > remove one of them from the road. The difference is not that one selects and the other does not. The
   > difference is what selection lets the third party do.

   Then move surge pricing and the Seattle injunction detail to notes unless another panel makes them
   load-bearing.

7. **§7 lands the chapter well, but the final sentence overclaims the film's rarity.**

   The restored card line now does real work:

   > "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as apathy."

   That line should stay. The closing paragraph is much better with it. The last sentence is weaker:

   > Both kinds of coordination do their work in the open here, in a film anyone can watch, which is the
   > rarest thing about it.

   "The rarest thing about it" is too large and too abstract for the close of a chapter about a film with
   so many concrete oddities. End on the day and the watchability.

   **Paste-ready close:**

   > Its people withdrew from plot, career, and convergence and stayed coordinated anyway: put within reach
   > by a cheap and legible city, and shown to us one at a time by a camera that took nothing from them
   > except a day, and gave the day back watchable.

## Part 2 — The four bans, and AI-slop audit

**Ban 1 — metaphor doing argumentative work.** Mostly clear. The terminology change helps. "Setting" and
"selector" are literal enough to hold the argument. The one live case is the heading "Enclosure." The
section uses the word as a frame but does not cash it out in the body. Either retitle §7 or add one plain
sentence.

**Paste-ready retitle:** `## 7. Distribution after the selector`

**Ban 2 — throat-clearing.** Mostly clear after the follow-up revisions. One soft instance remains in §5:

> What a decade of subsequent work adds is a precise account of what a selector can do that a camera
> cannot, and it comes to three things.

The sentence announces the list before giving it. Replace it with the concrete list, as suggested above.

**Ban 3 — meta-commentary / forward-backward reference.** Mostly clear. The chapter still has a few
instructional phrases that slow pacing:

> That is the modern counterpart of the Orion vice-president who said no.

This is not a violation by itself, but §7 has enough conceptual explanation around the example that the
sentence starts to feel like a guide rail. Keep only if §7 is otherwise trimmed.

**Ban 4 — unexplained jargon.** Improved. *Dérive*, *tertius gaudens*, civil inattention, nonterminal,
and *divide et impera* are now handled. The remaining collection-reader problem is economics language in
§3:

> assortative matching, the tendency of like to pair with like, is negatively related to local market size.

This is glossed, but still stiff. If the paragraph stays, make the sentence concrete.

**Paste-ready replacement:**

> Larger markets may also match people less neatly by type: in Mion and Naticchioni's account, like pairs
> with like less often as the local market grows.

**AI-slop audit.** The prose is authored and mostly free of generic mush. The antithesis habit is present
but usually useful because the chapter is sorting two forms. The larger cadence issue is recurring
mini-verdicts at paragraph ends:

> Nobody is owed a return.

> He is not a setting.

> That is a ruling on a preliminary injunction and not a final judgment.

> Convergence is uneven, and nobody has a reason to correct the flattering half.

Many are good sentences. Too many arrive in the same position. During compression, remove the verdict
closers that merely restate the paragraph. Keep the ones that turn the film.

right change from the abstract. The manuscript is too long by roughly 1,100 words, and the excess currently
sits in the wrong places. Cut the literature audit and platform-law detail, add one close description of a
handoff, send the terminology query, and resubmit near 5,300 or under 5,000 if the ceiling is firm.
