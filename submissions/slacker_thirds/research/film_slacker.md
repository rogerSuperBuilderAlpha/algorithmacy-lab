# Slacker (Linklater, 1991) — film-studies research base

Compiled 2026-07-02. Every claim below carries the URL at which it was verified by direct fetch.
Claims that could not be confirmed at a fetched source are marked **UNVERIFIED**.
Quotes are kept short and attributed; longer passages are paraphrased.

## 1. Hard facts (verified)

### Character count — the "about a hundred" figure
- Criterion's official synopsis: Linklater and crew "threw out any idea of a traditional plot," creating "a tapestry of over a hundred characters."
  Source (fetched via browser): https://www.criterion.com/films/408-slacker
- John Pierson (book chapter, reprinted by Criterion): the film felt like a twenty-four-hour tour of a college town "with an amusing collection of about a hundred losers and schmoozers," organized as "three dozen different episodes."
  Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off
- Vincent Canby, NYT: "a hundred or so relentlessly eccentric people."
  Source: https://www.nytimes.com/1991/07/05/movies/review-film-slacker-a-collection-of-eccentrics-and-lunacies.html
- Brendan Gaughen (End of Austin): "120+ amateur performers" cast from invite-only calls; 25 documented Austin locations.
  Source: https://endofaustin.com/2014/05/22/the-geography-of-slacker-25-years-later/
- **The "97 characters" figure: UNVERIFIED.** No fetched source gives 97 as a character count. A targeted search for "Slacker" + "97 characters" returned zero results. Likely source of the confusion: 97 is a *runtime* figure — Pierson calls the film a "ninety-seven-minute talkfest" (Criterion "Slacking Off," URL above), while Criterion's film page lists 100 minutes. Recommendation: the chapter should say "roughly one hundred" (Criterion: "over a hundred"; Pierson/Canby: "about a hundred") and avoid 97. (For comparison, Pierson gives an exact count for the *next* film: Dazed and Confused had "seventy-eight characters.")

### Production
- Shot on 16 mm (Arriflex), budget $23,000, filmed in Austin, Texas in 1989. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slacker_(film)
- "Summer 1989," largely without permits; only one location required payment ($20 to park). Source: https://endofaustin.com/2014/05/22/the-geography-of-slacker-25-years-later/
- Pierson dates the shoot "mid-1989" and repeatedly cites the "$23,000 film"; self-financed via credit cards and family money ("film-stock-in-the-refrigerator"), with a crane and a Steadicam deployed in two early sequences. Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off
- Rosenbaum: "Made in 1990 for a reputed $23,000" (his 1990 = completion year). Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1058-slacker-s-oblique-strategy
- Cinematography Lee Daniel; editing Scott Rhodes; production manager/casting Anne Walker-McBay. Source: https://www.criterion.com/films/408-slacker
- Some scenes incorporate 8 mm, Super 8, and PixelVision footage. Source: https://www.slantmagazine.com/dvd/slacker/

### Release history
- Work-in-progress screening at the 1989 Independent Feature Film Market; a $35,000 German television sale followed. Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off
- Austin premiere at the Dobie Theatre, July 1990 — Wikipedia gives July 27, 1990 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slacker_(film)); the Austin Chronicle's 30th-anniversary piece (Richard Whittaker, July 27, 2020) describes the first public screening at the Dobie and the year-long self-booked run (https://www.austinchronicle.com/daily/screens/2020-07-27/now-streaming-in-austin-1990-the-year-richard-linklaters-slacker-broke/). The Chronicle's first Slacker cover story ran July 27, 1990 (per https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/slack-where-we-started-11709590/ search result and the 2001 piece below). Minor date ambiguity (24th vs 27th) — flag for library check if an exact day is needed.
- Sold-out consecutive weeks at the Dobie; Orion Classics bought it on the model of their My Beautiful Laundrette deal: $100,000 advance plus roughly $50,000 for 35 mm blowup/completion. Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off
- Sundance, January 1991 (competition; no prize; juror Gus Van Sant told Linklater he'd had his vote). First screening audience "broke into thirds: one-third loved it, one-third walked out, and one-third fell asleep" (Pierson — a nice accidental echo of the chapter's "structural thirds"). Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off
- New Directors/New Films at MoMA, March 1991; Canby's original review ran March 22, 1991 (excerpted in the July 5 NYT piece, URL above).
- National release by Orion Classics July 5, 1991, opening at New York's Angelika day-and-date with the "official" Austin reopening; played college markets through the fall; final gross ~$1.2 million. Sources: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slacker_(film)
- Michael Barker (then Orion Classics) confirms the acquisition story from the buyer's side, incl. Telluride/Toronto/NYFF rejections and the four Sundance screenings that went from near-empty to fights-to-get-in. Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1060-slacker-looking-back

### The roaming-camera handoff structure (as described by critics)
- Wikipedia: a "relay-race style" handoff — the film follows a character for minutes, then transitions to another. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slacker_(film)
- Roger Ebert (1991): Linklater borrows the surrealists' technique (Buñuel's The Phantom of Liberty) and speeds it up so he can "carom through the slacker community of Austin... like a cue ball with a camera"; Ebert's summary: "We don't get a story, but we do get a feeling." Source (fetched via browser): https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/slacker-1991
- Canby (NYT): "a series of unconnected vignettes that open one into another" in the manner of Phantom of Liberty; famously, "a 14-course meal composed entirely of desserts." Source: https://www.nytimes.com/1991/07/05/movies/review-film-slacker-a-collection-of-eccentrics-and-lunacies.html
- Budd Wilkins (Slant, 2013): after the hit-and-run, Linklater's character fades back, "figuratively passing the baton to another, thereby setting the template"; the "round-robin" structure borrows from Ophüls's La Ronde and Buñuel. Source (fetched via browser): https://www.slantmagazine.com/dvd/slacker/
- John Pierson (2011): "There's not a single jump cut, and the passage from one scene to the next is always in a flow." Source: https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2011-01-21/slack-to-the-future/
- Barker: structure "a string of stories à la Arthur Schnitzler's La ronde" with twists reminiscent of The Phantom of Liberty. Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1060-slacker-looking-back

### The anti-plot opening
- Linklater himself plays the first character, credited as "Should have stayed at the bus station" (Criterion cast list: https://www.criterion.com/films/408-slacker).
- The taxi monologue: he recounts a dream in which "instead of anything... going on, there's nothing going on," then expounds the idea that each un-taken choice "fractions off" into its own reality — Rosenbaum reads this as announcing "the movie's contrarian discourse on the virtues of inaction." Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1058-slacker-s-oblique-strategy
- Ebert paraphrases the same monologue (every possibility imagined becomes "a separate reality on some other level of existence"). Source: https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/slacker-1991
- A fan transcription of the full monologue (incl. the Wizard of Oz crossroads: Dorothy and the Scarecrow's considered directions "became separate realities") exists at http://eatyourcerealwithafork.blogspot.com/2006/06/slacker-monologue-i-was-just-watching.html — **fan transcript; verify wording against the film or the 1992 book before quoting.**
- The handoff into the plot-that-isn't: the monologist witnesses a hit-and-run; the camera leaves him for the driver (who has run over his own mother), then for a witness of the arrest, and so on (Rosenbaum, URL above; also Canby, Ebert URLs above).

### The Madonna pap smear scene
- Teresa Taylor, drummer of Butthole Surfers, credited only as "Pap smear pusher"; the character was Taylor's own invention (Criterion: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/2905-a-slacker-s-story ; cast credit at https://www.criterion.com/films/408-slacker).
- Her sales line, per Pierson: "It's like sort of getting down to the real Madonna." Orion's trailer and one-sheet led with the gag; Pierson relays the (hearsay-flagged: "reportedly") anecdote of Madonna herself bolting from the Nuart when the trailer played. Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off
- Rosenbaum: the scene "may be the most acute comment ever" on celebrity worship. Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1058-slacker-s-oblique-strategy

### Conspiracy / JFK / moon-landing monologists
- Criterion cast list character names: Jerry Delony as "Been on the moon since the '50s"; John Slate as "Conspiracy A-Go-Go author"; Louis Mackey as "Old anarchist." Source: https://www.criterion.com/films/408-slacker
- Ebert: a man who "knows" a moon astronaut saw an alien craft before NASA cut the transmission. Source: https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/slacker-1991
- Canby quotes the soothsayer: "We've been on Mars since 1962." Source: https://www.nytimes.com/1991/07/05/movies/review-film-slacker-a-collection-of-eccentrics-and-lunacies.html
- The old anarchist's Spanish Civil War/Orwell past is fabricated — a talker of a good game (Rosenbaum: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1058-slacker-s-oblique-strategy ; Slant: https://www.slantmagazine.com/dvd/slacker/).
- The Oblique Strategies scene (~two-thirds in, per Rosenbaum; "near film's end" per Slant): a woman (D. Montgomery per Slant) hands cards to passersby; cards read "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as apathy," "It's not building a wall but making a brick," and "There is no structure. The underlying order is chaos." Sources: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1058-slacker-s-oblique-strategy ; https://www.slantmagazine.com/dvd/slacker/

### Criterion edition (for citation of materials)
- Spine #247; 100 min; three audio commentaries (Linklater solo; cast group; Linklater/Daniel/Walker technical); booklet with essays by Pierson and Barker, reviews by Rosenbaum and Chris Walters, and **production notes by Linklater**; includes It's Impossible to Learn to Plow by Reading Books (1988). Source: https://www.criterion.com/films/408-slacker

## 2. Linklater's own statements on structure and narrative refusal

### On the relay design and "architecture" (strongest quotes for the thesis)
- Austin Chronicle, Marc Savlov, "Slack to the Future," Jan. 21, 2011 (conversation with Linklater and Pierson): Linklater on the design contract with the viewer — "We're not going to offer a story but at least we'll offer a comprehensive architecture that you can pass through and not feel completely disoriented." Source: https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2011-01-21/slack-to-the-future/
- Same conversation, excerpted by Criterion (verbatim block verified): Linklater says his was the first TV-remote generation, "creating our own narratives by watching five minutes of this and then one minute of that," and that he conceived Slacker as "you're either channel surfing or you're going in and out of different movies." Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1722-slacker-twenty-years-later
- Austin Chronicle, Marjorie Baumgarten, "Slack Where We Started," June 29, 2001: "It's truly a radical narrative. That was the core of the whole experience to me." Source: https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2001-06-29/82235/
- Criterion 2004 commentary (SECONDHAND via Edward Copeland's blog — needs check against the disc): Linklater wanted the film to feel like "one long take"; "It seems spontaneous, but we always knew what was coming next." Source: http://eddieonfilm.blogspot.com/2010/07/longest-tracking-shot.html
- Declared art-film models: Ophüls's La Ronde and Buñuel's The Discreet Charm of the Bourgeoisie (per Pierson's chapter: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off).

### On the film's politics — withdrawal as refusal, not apathy
- 1991 self-interview (Austin Chronicle, Sept. 1991; republished 2020): slackers are "ultimately being responsible to themselves," refusing activity alien to who they are; "No one ever said slackers weren't productive," their products just "fall outside the market economy"; the film is "a 100 percent Austin product"; method = characters' "complete freedom within the confines of a certain structure." Source: https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2020-07-24/from-the-archives-linklater-on-linklater/
- 2001: "It's a film of 100 people, and they don't work... my whole point was: No, they do a lot." Also: he meant the term "slacker" affectionately, not negatively. Source: https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2001-06-29/82235/
- 2011: self-definition through unpaid vocation — "You may have worked a busboy job but really you were in a band, you were a writer, you were an artist." And the film "documented and maybe articulated... a lifestyle" on a "long continuum from the Beats and beyond." Source: https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2011-01-21/slack-to-the-future/
- Wilkins attributes to Linklater the phrase "principled nonparticipation," celebrated and satirized at once (https://www.slantmagazine.com/dvd/slacker/); Criterion's synopsis calls the milieu "an emerging generation of aggressive nonparticipants" (https://www.criterion.com/films/408-slacker). The in-film crystallization is the Oblique Strategies card "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as apathy" (verified at https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1058-slacker-s-oblique-strategy).
- Pierson quotes Linklater characterizing the channel-surfing generation's "total nonbelief in everything" — while noting Linklater is "in it but not of it." Source: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off

## 3. Existing scholarship on Slacker

### Monographs on Linklater
- Rob Stone, *The Cinema of Richard Linklater: Walk, Don't Run*, Directors' Cuts series (London/New York: Wallflower Press / Columbia University Press; 1st ed. 2013, rev. 2nd ed. 2018). Metadata: https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-cinema-of-richard-linklater/9780231165525/ ; TOC and chapter first-pages verified at https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7312/ston17920 (fetched via browser).
  - Ch. 4, "The Form and Content of Slack" (pp. 74–103, 2018 ed.) opens by placing Slacker against "unfilmable" modernist form — Joyce's *Ulysses*, Coupland's *Generation X*, Cortázar's *Hopscotch* (chapters readable in any order).
  - Ch. 1 opens with the film's own self-heckle — the bystander who watches Linklater shooting passersby and proclaims "Ain't no film in that shit!"
  - Ch. 2 "Locating Linklater" (Austin as resistant place); Ch. 5 "Walking and Talking" (dialogue as the determinant of form and as production method).
- David T. Johnson, *Richard Linklater*, Contemporary Film Directors (Urbana: University of Illinois Press, 2012). Publisher Q&A (Apr. 30, 2012) confirms the book's master-theme of time and groups Slacker with Linklater's films confined to "roughly twenty-four hours or less"; closes with an interview with Linklater. Sources: https://www.press.uillinois.edu/wordpress/qa-with-richard-linklater-author-david-t-johnson/ ; review by Mary Harrod, Senses of Cinema no. 64 (Sept. 2012): http://www.sensesofcinema.com/2012/book-reviews/richard-linklater-by-david-t-johnson/ (Harrod notes Johnson quotes Linklater calling time "the most unique element of film as an art form," p. 138).

## 4. What critics/scholars already say holds the film together

The chapter's structural-third thesis (Austin-1990 "hosts"; the camera "engineers") should be positioned against six existing answers:

1. **Hidden formal craft (closest to the "engineering" half).** Ron Rosenbaum: the film "appears to have no structure, to be chaotic... when, in fact, it has a very subtle, extremely well-crafted structure that makes it a portrait of chaos" — reading the in-film card "There is no structure. The underlying order is chaos" as self-reference. (https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1058-slacker-s-oblique-strategy). Pierson: "the absolute brilliance of the structure," the "clever links between the three dozen different episodes" (https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off) and "not a single jump cut... always in a flow" (https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2011-01-21/slack-to-the-future/). Linklater's own "comprehensive architecture that you can pass through" (same URL) is the auteur-side statement of this answer.
2. **The surrealist relay / camera's licensed drift.** Ebert ("cue ball with a camera"; the film "grants itself that freedom" to peel away from anyone: https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/slacker-1991); Canby and Hinson via Buñuel's Phantom of Liberty (NYT and WaPo URLs); Barker via Schnitzler/La Ronde (https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1060-slacker-looking-back); Wilkins's "passing the baton" (https://www.slantmagazine.com/dvd/slacker/). These name the mechanism but treat it as inherited genre device, not as active brokerage.
3. **The city itself.** Gaughen: the film "is less about a place and more about some of the people that make up (and are very much made by) the place" — and shows the spatial continuity is partly illusory (implausible distances between consecutive scenes), which actually *supports* the claim that the camera, not geography, does the joining (https://endofaustin.com/2014/05/22/the-geography-of-slacker-25-years-later/). Stone ch. 2 "Locating Linklater" grounds the film in Austin-as-resistance (https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7312/ston17920). Linklater: "a 100 percent Austin product" (https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2020-07-24/from-the-archives-linklater-on-linklater/).
4. **The day-in-the-life frame.** Canby: "The time, morning, afternoon, night and morning again" (NYT URL); Johnson groups Slacker with Linklater's ~24-hour films (https://www.press.uillinois.edu/wordpress/qa-with-richard-linklater-author-david-t-johnson/); Pierson's "twenty-four-hour tour."
5. **Talk/philosophizing as connective tissue.** Hinson: characters "talk, nonstop, for the sheer pleasure of hearing themselves talk" (https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/1991/08/23/slacker-loaf-on-the-edge/7345af35-7730-4f3d-bd20-6be90d433f7b/); Stone ch. 5 "Walking and Talking" makes dialogue "the determinant factor in the form" (JSTOR URL); Rosenbaum's Austin coffeehouses as "the agora of Athens."
6. **Spectatorship/media logic.** Linklater's channel-surfing self-account (https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1722-slacker-twenty-years-later) and Oake's Gen-X-as-spectatorship (Velvet Light Trap 53) treat coherence as located in a viewing habit rather than in the film's world.

**Novelty check:** no fetched source separates the coherence work into two coordinated mechanisms — a shared reference environment (hosting) plus an active per-handoff broker (engineering). The nearest neighbors are Rosenbaum (hidden craft, but monolithic "structure"), Pierson (seamless links, trade-craft framing), and Linklater's own "architecture you can pass through." The chapter can honestly claim the two-mechanism decomposition as its contribution while crediting these as anticipations. Macor's chapter title "The Least Auteur Film Ever Made" is the strongest existing counter-position to camera-as-engineer (coherence from collective authorship) and should be engaged directly (contents need library check).

## How this serves the argument (hosting/engineering thesis)

- **Engineering has a primary-source anchor.** Linklater's "comprehensive architecture that you can pass through and not feel completely disoriented" (Savlov 2011) is the film's maker describing exactly the brokered-passage function the chapter assigns to the camera; pair with Pierson's "not a single jump cut... always in a flow" as the craft-level confirmation that every handoff is engineered, never elided.
- **Hosting has a primary-source anchor too.** Linklater's "100 percent Austin product" plus the 1991 self-interview's account of a milieu "responsible to themselves" outside the market economy establishes Austin 1990 as the shared reference environment; Gaughen's finding that the film's spatial continuity is partly fictional is the key evidence that the city *hosts* but does not *connect* — the connecting is the camera's job. That division of labor is the chapter's wedge.
- **The relay is documented, not invented.** "Relay-race" (Wikipedia), "passing the baton" (Slant), "cue ball with a camera" (Ebert), La Ronde/Phantom of Liberty lineage (Canby, Hinson, Barker, Pierson) — the chapter can cite a stable critical consensus on the mechanism and then reinterpret it as active brokerage between strangers rather than passive drift.
- **Anti-plot is announced inside the film.** The opening monologue (Linklater as "Should have stayed at the bus station") declares the forking-realities logic the structure then performs — each handoff is a road taken while ~99 others fraction off; Speed's article title ("Roads Not Taken") shows scholarship already reads Linklater through this monologue, so the chapter builds on, not against, the literature.
- **"Withdrawal as refusal" is textually grounded.** The Oblique Strategies card "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as apathy" (verified in Rosenbaum) plus Linklater's "No, they do a lot" gives the politics section verified anchors: nonparticipation in plot mirrors nonparticipation in the market — both still coordinated.
- **False-novelty guard.** Closest existing readings to defuse: Rosenbaum (hidden well-crafted structure), Pierson (seamless links), Macor's "least auteur film ever made" (collective authorship), Stone's form/content of slack (modernist unfilmables). None decompose coherence into host + broker; say so, with these citations.
- **Numbers discipline.** Use "over a hundred characters" (Criterion) not "97" — 97 is a runtime figure (Pierson's "ninety-seven-minute talkfest"); note Sundance's audience "broke into thirds" (Pierson) only as a charming aside, not evidence.
