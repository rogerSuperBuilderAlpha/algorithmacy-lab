# Urban thirds: the sociology of the city and strangers

Research base for the *Slacker* chapter's claim that Austin circa 1990 acts as a **hosting third** — a third party that coordinates strangers by supplying shared reference points (walkable downtown, subcultural density, conventions of civil inattention) without brokering any particular encounter.

Compiled 2026-07-02. Verification convention: every claim carries a fetched source URL. Claims that rest on secondary sources or that could not be page-verified are flagged **[LIBRARY CHECK]** or **UNVERIFIED**.

## 1. The sociology of the third

### 1.1 Simmel's triad (1908; Wolff trans. 1950)

**Textual locus.** "The Triad" is not a freestanding essay: it is part of chapter 2 of Simmel's *Soziologie. Untersuchungen über die Formen der Vergesellschaftung* (1908), "Die quantitative Bestimmtheit der Gruppe" (the quantitative determination of the group). All English headings — including "The Triad" itself — were added by translator-editor Kurt H. Wolff in *The Sociology of Georg Simmel* (Glencoe, IL: Free Press, 1950), which collects excerpts mainly from the 1908 *Soziologie* plus items from 1896–1917.
- Source (fetched): Hollstein, "Georg Simmel's Contribution to Social Network Research" — https://www.socium.uni-bremen.de/lib/download.php?file=0220f95cb5.pdf&filename=Hollstein+2021+Simmels+contribution+to+network+research.pdf (surfaced via search; publication details confirmed there and in the Cambridge review below)
- Book metadata: Cambridge review of the Wolff volume confirms Glencoe, Ill.: The Free Press, 1950, pp. lxiv + 445 — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-economics-and-political-science-revue-canadienne-de-economiques-et-science-politique/article/abs/the-sociology-of-georg-simmel-translated-edited-and-with-an-introduction-by-kurt-h-wolff-glencoe-ill-the-free-press-toronto-burns-maceachern-1950-pp-lxiv-445-625/837E49C7AE887ECBEB096419ACB79A9D
- Standard page range for "The Triad": pp. 145–169 (per Oxford Bibliographies "Brokerage in Networks," surfaced via search — https://www.oxfordbibliographies.com/display/document/obo-9780199846740/obo-9780199846740-0146.xml ). **[LIBRARY CHECK: confirm 145–169 against the physical Wolff edition; the Internet Archive copy is available at https://archive.org/details/sociologyofgeorg0000simm ]**

**Dyad vs. triad.** In Wolff's arrangement the triad section is preceded by "The Isolated Individual and the Dyad." Simmel's core claim: the dyad depends on each member and dies with either; the arrival of a third transforms the sociological situation qualitatively, because relations can now be *mediated* — the group acquires a supra-individual life, and each member can be outvoted, bypassed, or connected indirectly. The triad is thus the smallest structure in which "society" as a form exists independently of any one member. (Summary per AcaWiki and Hollstein; the AcaWiki entry focuses on the triadic roles.)
- Source (fetched): https://acawiki.org/The_triad

**The three roles of the third** (verified via fetched AcaWiki entry, corroborated by the Cambridge treatise article below):
1. **The non-partisan / mediator** — strengthens or repairs the relation of the other two; can stand equally *between* the two positions or *above* them. (This is the closest classical ancestor of the chapter's hosting third.)
2. **Tertius gaudens** — "the third who enjoys": benefits from the other two's separation or conflict; can make them compete for its favor or play them off against each other.
3. **Divide et impera** — the third that actively *produces* conflict between the other two in order to dominate both.
- Source (fetched): https://acawiki.org/The_triad
- Corroboration: "Simmel's Treatise on the Triad (1908)," *Journal of Institutional Economics* — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/abs/simmels-treatise-on-the-triad-1908/949F7B9C95967464B7D2692EAD1AE66C (abstract page; full text paywalled)

**Chapter citation to use:** Georg Simmel, "The Triad," in *The Sociology of Georg Simmel*, trans. and ed. Kurt H. Wolff (Glencoe, IL: Free Press, 1950), 145–69. [orig. *Soziologie*, 1908]

### 1.2 The third's later career: brokerage (brief — already verified in companion project)

- **Burt, structural holes.** Ronald S. Burt, *Structural Holes: The Social Structure of Competition* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1992). The broker spanning a "structural hole" between disconnected others captures information and control benefits — Burt explicitly builds on Simmel's tertius gaudens ("the third who benefits"). Publisher page: https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674843714
- **Obstfeld, tertius iungens.** David Obstfeld, "Social Networks, the Tertius Iungens Orientation, and Involvement in Innovation," *Administrative Science Quarterly* 50, no. 1 (2005): 100–130. The "third who joins": a strategic orientation toward *introducing* disconnected others or facilitating new coordination between connected ones — the explicit contrast to Burt's gaudens broker. Journal page: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2189/asqu.2005.50.1.100
- Positioning note: Simmel's mediator, Burt's gaudens, and Obstfeld's iungens are all *brokering* thirds — they act on particular pairs. The hosting third is different in kind: it coordinates without touching any particular dyad (see §5).

### 1.3 Simmel's "The Stranger" (1908)

Originally an excursus to the chapter on the sociology of space in *Soziologie* (Leipzig: Duncker & Humblot, 1908). Key verified content (via fetched Wikipedia article):
- The stranger is defined against the wanderer: not the one "who comes today and goes tomorrow," but the one **"who comes today and stays tomorrow."**
- The stranger is a **unity of nearness and remoteness**: "close to us, insofar as we feel between him and ourselves common features of a national, social, occupational, or generally human, nature. He is far from us, insofar as these common features extend beyond him or us, and connect us only because they connect a great many people."
- The stranger's distance yields **objectivity**, which Simmel glosses as freedom: "the objective individual is bound by no commitments which could prejudice his perception, understanding, and evaluation of the given." Historically the stranger appears as the **trader** and the **impartial arbitrator** — i.e., the stranger *is* a kind of third.
- Source (fetched): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stranger_(essay)
- Wolff translation locus: pp. 402–408 of the 1950 volume is the standard citation; some sources give 402–406. **[LIBRARY CHECK: pin exact range.]** Search corroboration: https://www.academia.edu/67418307/Excursus_on_the_Stranger_in_the_Context_of_Simmel_s_Sociology_of_Space
- Full text of the essay (public copy): https://www.infoamerica.org/documentos_pdf/simmel01.pdf

**Citation:** Georg Simmel, "The Stranger," in *The Sociology of Georg Simmel*, trans. and ed. Kurt H. Wolff (Glencoe, IL: Free Press, 1950), 402–8.

### 1.4 Simmel's "The Metropolis and Mental Life" (1903)

Lecture, "Die Großstädte und das Geistesleben," Dresden 1903. Verified themes (via search-surfaced full texts and summaries):
- **Punctuality, calculability, exactness** are "forced upon" metropolitan life: the relations of metropolitan residents must be coordinated "into a firmly fixed framework of time" — the metropolis as a machine for coordinating strangers on schedule.
- The **money economy** reduces qualitative values to quantitative ones and objectifies relations among people who never know each other personally — impersonal coordination at scale.
- The **blasé attitude** and reserve are protective adaptations to overload — the psychic ancestor of Goffman's civil inattention (§2.3): metropolitan indifference is itself a *convention* that makes dense co-presence livable.
- Full text (fetched via search results): https://www.germanhistorydocs.org/en/wilhelmine-germany-and-the-first-world-war-1890-1918/georg-simmel-the-metropolis-and-mental-life-1903.pdf ; also https://ondoc.logand.com/d/5362/pdf
- Summary source: https://soztheo.com/sociology/key-works-in-sociology/georg-simmel-the-metropolis-and-mental-life-1903/
- Wolff translation locus verified: pp. 409–424 — https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1298504

**Citation:** Georg Simmel, "The Metropolis and Mental Life," in *The Sociology of Georg Simmel*, trans. and ed. Kurt H. Wolff (Glencoe, IL: Free Press, 1950), 409–24. [orig. 1903]

## 2. Strangers and public space

### 2.1 Lyn Lofland, *A World of Strangers* (1973)

Lyn H. Lofland, *A World of Strangers: Order and Action in Urban Public Space* (New York: Basic Books, 1973), 240 pp. Verified thesis (via *Social Forces* review page and publisher/retail metadata): city life — "a potentially chaotic and meaningless world of strangers" — is made livable by **ordering**. Lofland's historical argument: pre-industrial cities ordered strangers by **appearance** (dress, insignia); the modern city orders them by **location** — knowing *where* someone is (what kind of place) replaces knowing *who* they are. Coordination among strangers is achieved by a legible spatial code, not by acquaintance.
- Review metadata (fetched via search): https://academic.oup.com/sf/article-abstract/53/3/518/2230362
- Full text available: https://archive.org/details/worldofstrangers0000lofl
- **[LIBRARY CHECK: exact wording of the appearential→locational ordering argument, chs. 2–4.]**

This is the single closest fit to the hosting-third claim in urban sociology: the city supplies the *classification scheme* by which strangers read each other, brokering nothing.

### 2.2 Lyn Lofland, *The Public Realm* (1998)

Lyn H. Lofland, *The Public Realm: Exploring the City's Quintessential Social Territory* (Hawthorne, NY: Aldine de Gruyter, 1998), 305 pp. Defines the public realm as the social territory constituted by relations among strangers and codifies its interactional principles (cooperative motility, civil inattention, audience-role prominence, restrained helpfulness, civility toward diversity). A review called it "the unifying brilliance for which the sociology of public places has been waiting."
- Metadata verified via *Social Forces* review page: https://academic.oup.com/sf/article-abstract/78/1/408/2234387 ; WorldCat: https://search.worldcat.org/title/public-realm-exploring-the-citys-quintessential-social-territory/oclc/37755235
- Full text: https://archive.org/details/publicrealmexplo0000lofl
- **[LIBRARY CHECK: the five interactional principles list, ch. 2 — the list above is from standard secondary summaries and should be page-verified.]**

### 2.3 Goffman, civil inattention (1963)

Erving Goffman, *Behavior in Public Places: Notes on the Social Organization of Gatherings* (New York: Free Press, 1963). **Civil inattention**: the ritual by which co-present strangers briefly acknowledge one another (a glance at proper distance) and then withdraw attention, displaying that the other is not a target of curiosity, design, or hostility. Goffman calls it perhaps the slightest of interpersonal rituals yet one that constantly regulates social intercourse.
- Concept locus per secondary sources: the discussion sits in the chapter on **unfocused interaction**, ch. 5 ("Some Rules of Involvement... "/the eye-contact discussion), commonly cited at **pp. 83–88** (the canonical definition around p. 84). Sources: Frontiers in Sociology article citing pp. 83–88 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sociology/articles/10.3389/fsoc.2023.1212090/full ; Springer *Ethics and Information Technology* article — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-020-09575-7
- **[LIBRARY CHECK: exact page for the definitional sentence; confirm chapter number/title in the 1963 Free Press edition.]**
- Argument link: civil inattention is a *convention supplied by the public order itself* — a coordination rule no one negotiates per-encounter. It is exactly the kind of shared reference point the hosting third provides.

### 2.4 Jane Jacobs, the sidewalk ballet (1961)

Jane Jacobs, *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (New York: Random House, 1961). Verified quotes (fetched):
- "Under the seeming disorder of the old city, wherever the old city is working successfully, is a marvelous order for maintaining the safety of the streets and the freedom of the city." The order is likened to "an intricate ballet in which the individual dancers and ensembles all have distinctive parts which miraculously reinforce each other and compose an orderly whole." — fetched at https://didacticdiscourse.wordpress.com/2012/03/12/quote-a-classic-the-sidewalk-ballet-by-jane-jacobs/
- The passage is from Part One, the chapter "The uses of sidewalks: safety" (Jacobs's Hudson Street "sidewalk ballet"); the book also introduces "eyes upon the street." Overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities
- **[LIBRARY CHECK: page number — commonly cited as p. 50 of the 1961 Random House edition, but one secondary source gives p. 117 (likely an anthology reprint). Also pull the "sum of such casual, public contact" trust passage (commonly cited ~p. 56), which was not verifiable from fetched pages.]**
- Argument link: the ballet is *uncoordinated coordination* — order emerging from many strangers using a shared stage. Jacobs's sidewalk is a hosting third avant la lettre: the street supplies the choreography's setting and props; nobody directs the dance.

### 2.5 Milgram, the familiar stranger (1972)

Stanley Milgram, "The Familiar Stranger: An Aspect of Urban Anonymity," *Division 8 Newsletter* (Division of Personality and Social Psychology, American Psychological Association), 1972; based on 1971 studies (CUNY; a commuter-rail platform study). Reprinted in Milgram, *The Individual in a Social World: Essays and Experiments* (1977). A **familiar stranger** is someone repeatedly observed but never interacted with — a real social relationship of mutual, agreed-upon non-interaction, anchored to a shared *place* (the train platform). Milgram noted the relationship is place-bound: the same pair, meeting far from the anchoring location, are far more likely to speak.
- Sources (fetched via search): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Familiar_stranger ; original-publication detail: https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=4163677 ; Paulos & Goodman's Ubicomp paper reviewing Milgram: https://www.kostakos.org/courses/ubicomp10S/papers/social/paulos-04.pdf
- **[LIBRARY CHECK: the newsletter original is near-unobtainable; cite the 1977 reprint.]**
- Argument link: the familiar-stranger relation is *created by the venue, not by the parties* — the purest small-scale demonstration that a place can constitute a relationship between people who never transact. *Slacker*'s Austin is a city-sized field of familiar strangers.

### 2.6 Oldenburg, the third place (1989) — TERMINOLOGICAL COLLISION NOTE

Ray Oldenburg, *The Great Good Place: Cafes, Coffee Shops, Community Centers, Beauty Parlors, General Stores, Bars, Hangouts and How They Get You Through the Day* (New York: Paragon House, 1989), 338 pp. First-edition publisher verified as **Paragon House, 1989** (ISBN 9781557781109) via Biblio/Internet Archive/WorldCat listings: https://www.biblio.com/9781557781109 ; https://archive.org/details/greatgoodplaceca0000olde ; https://search.worldcat.org/title/The-great-good-place-:-cafes-coffee-shops-community-centers-beauty-parlors-general-stores-bars-hangouts-and-how-they-get-you-through-the-day/oclc/19456652 . (Note: the Wikipedia infobox surfaces Berkshire Publishing Group, the *current* rights-holder/reissue publisher — do not cite Berkshire for the 1989 first edition. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Good_Place_(book) )

**What Oldenburg means by "third":** an *ordinal* third — third in a sequence of settings in an individual's life. First place = home; second place = work; third place = "a generic designation for a great variety of public places that host the regular, voluntary, informal, and happily anticipated gatherings of individuals beyond the realms of home and work." Characteristics: neutral ground, leveler (status-transcending), conversation as main activity, accessibility and accommodation, regulars, low profile, playful mood, home away from home.
- Sources (fetched via search): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_place ; https://greatgoodplace.org/ ; https://www.pps.org/article/roldenburg

**The collision, made explicit for the chapter:** Oldenburg's "third" counts *settings in a biography* (home, work, hangout); the chapter's "third" counts *parties in a relation* (you, me, and the coordinating third). They are orthogonal uses of the same word — and yet they meet in the material: *Slacker*'s cafés (Les Amis), record stores, and bars are Oldenburg third places, and it is largely *through* its stock of third places that Austin performs its role as a structural third. Recommended move: exploit the near-collision rather than merely disclaim it — Oldenburg's third places are the *organs*; the hosting third (the city) is the *organism*. Note also that Oldenburg's third place typically has a **host who greets regulars** (a literal host inside the venue), whereas the hosting city hosts without any greeter — a nice contrast for sharpening the "no broker" clause of the chapter's definition.

## 3. Austin circa 1990: the material conditions of the hosting city

### 3.1 The bust that produced slack

- The mid-1980s Texas oil collapse (oil price crash of 1986) triggered a statewide recession and a real-estate and banking collapse; the 1980s boom had built a residential and commercial overhang that then deflated. Sources: Texas Tribune — https://www.texastribune.org/2020/05/18/texas-oil-prices-1980s/ ; Dallas Fed, "Texas Real Estate: From the 1980s' Oil Bust to the Shale Oil Boom" — https://www.dallasfed.org/~/media/documents/research/events/2014/14tengallonduca.pdf
- Austin-specific effect, verified via fetched End of Austin article and its comment thread: Austin in the late 1980s–early 1990s was "significantly cheaper," which "certainly helped make it possible for part-time workers (and full-time dropouts) to pursue esoteric aims." A resident's testimony in the comments: rent at the Del Prado apartments *fell* across the late-80s downturn (from ~$515 to ~$350/month) as owners grew "desperate to keep the apartments rented"; the same units ran ~$1,300 by 2020. Source (fetched): Brendan Gaughen, "Slacker Geography, 25 Years Later," *The End of Austin*, May 22, 2014 — https://endofaustin.com/2014/05/22/the-geography-of-slacker-25-years-later/
- The causal chain for the chapter: **bust → vacancy → cheap rent → slack (unsold time) → a population free to loiter in public → density of available strangers.** The hosting third was, in part, an accident of a regional financial collapse.

### 3.2 The film as artifact of that Austin (verified production facts)

- Shot in Austin in 1989 on 16mm; budget **$23,000**; premiered **July 27, 1990 at Austin's Dobie Theatre** (near the UT campus); released nationally **July 5, 1991 by Orion Classics**; grossed ~$1.2M; added to the **National Film Registry in 2012**. Structure: a relay of handoffs — the camera follows one character until it "picks up someone else in the scene and follows them," across an ensemble of misfit locals in a single day. Source (fetched): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slacker_(film)
- Geography of the film, verified via Gaughen (fetched, above): locations concentrate along **the Drag (Guadalupe Street)** and West Campus, the UT tower area, downtown (Congress Ave., Red River), with excursions to South and East Austin — i.e., the film's relay structure is literally enabled by a compact, walkable corridor where strangers' paths cross.
- Then-vs-now: Gaughen documents locations replaced by student towers, a Starbucks, an urgent care clinic, etc.; companion piece with photo comparisons: Anderson Boyd, "'Slacker' Austin, Then and Now," *The Alcalde* (Texas Exes), May 28, 2014 (fetched) — https://alcalde.texasexes.org/2014/05/slacker-austin-then-and-now ; Gaughen quote from it: "Downtown is nearly unrecognizable today from how it was in the late '80s and early '90s."
- **UNVERIFIED:** the widely repeated Linklater line that he only later realized *Slacker* was "documenting the end of something" (an Austin subculture eroded by the 1990s tech boom and the end of cheap rent). It appears in search-result summaries attributed to a post-release interview, but no fetched page contained it. Candidate source that could not be fetched (HTTP 403): "I Watch 'Slacker' to Read Austin in the Original," *Places Journal* — https://placesjournal.org/article/i-watch-slacker-to-read-austin-in-the-original/ . **Do not quote until located** (try Places Journal directly, or Alison Macor, *Chainsaws, Slackers, and Spy Kids: Thirty Years of Filmmaking in Austin, Texas*, UT Press 2010).

## 4. The city as coordination medium (the crisp anchors)

Two anchors, one humanist and one economic:

1. **Jacobs's sidewalk order** (§2.4): "a marvelous order... composed of movement and change" that no one plans — the street as the medium in which strangers' self-interested trajectories compose into safety and trust. This is coordination *by* the city, not *in spite of* it.

2. **Agglomeration economics: sharing, matching, learning.** Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga, "Micro-Foundations of Urban Agglomeration Economies," in *Handbook of Regional and Urban Economics*, vol. 4, ed. J. Vernon Henderson and Jacques-François Thisse (Amsterdam: North-Holland, 2004), 2063–2117. Verified via fetched author page ( https://diegopuga.org/research.html ) and SSRN listing ( https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=439613 ). The canonical taxonomy of why cities exist economically: they let agents **share** indivisible facilities and risks, **match** better (employer–employee, firm–supplier — thicker markets, better pairings), and **learn** (knowledge spillovers). The **matching** mechanism is the direct bridge to the platform comparison: a city improves match quality *without ever proposing a match* — it just thickens the market — whereas a platform algorithmically brokers each pair. Same function, opposite mechanism.

3. **Glaeser (popular anchor).** Edward Glaeser, *Triumph of the City: How Our Greatest Invention Makes Us Richer, Smarter, Greener, Healthier, and Happier* (New York: Penguin Press, 2011) — cities as engines of proximity-driven connection and learning. Metadata via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumph_of_the_City . **[NOTE: a specific "cities as matchmakers" / marriage-market passage in Glaeser was NOT located in fetched sources — treat that phrase as UNVERIFIED and prefer Duranton–Puga's "matching" as the citable anchor.]**

4. Simmel's metropolis essay (§1.4) supplies the classical version: the "firmly fixed framework of time" into which metropolitan relations must be coordinated — the city as a pre-existing coordination schema its inhabitants inherit rather than negotiate.

## How this serves the argument

**The hosting third, positioned.** Simmel gives the chapter its geometry: the third transforms a world of dyads into a society, and comes in role-flavors — mediator, tertius gaudens, divide et impera (§1.1). Burt and Obstfeld show the third's modern career is all *brokerage*: thirds that act on particular pairs, whether extractively (gaudens) or connectively (iungens) (§1.2). The hosting third completes the typology with a missing cell: **a third that coordinates the whole field of pairs while brokering none of them.** Austin circa 1990 is the exhibit: it supplies the stage (walkable Drag-to-downtown corridor, §3.2), the props (cheap rooms and slack time, §3.1), the cast density (465,622 people plus a giant university, §3.3), and the interaction protocol (civil inattention, familiar strangers, the locational code, §2) — and then does nothing in particular. *Slacker*'s relay structure is the formal proof: forty-some handoffs among strangers, none arranged by anyone, all made possible by the same host.

**Each source's job:**
- *Simmel's triad*: the base typology the hosting third extends; tertius gaudens is also the platform-critique foil (the platform as a gaudens that monetizes the gap between users the city would simply have let meet).
- *Simmel's stranger*: the film's population — persons near and far at once, whose distance is itself a form of relation; and the reminder that the stranger classically *is* a third (trader, arbitrator).
- *Simmel's metropolis*: the city as inherited coordination schema (clock-time, money, reserve) — the host's operating system.
- *Lofland 1973/1998*: the strongest scholarly warrant that stranger-coordination is achieved by the city's ordering of appearances and *locations* — reference points, not introductions. Cite her when defining what the host actually supplies.
- *Goffman*: civil inattention as the micro-convention that makes co-presence without transaction sustainable — the etiquette of being hosted.
- *Jacobs*: the emergent-order image (sidewalk ballet) and the trust-from-casual-contact mechanism; also the design conditions (mixed use, eyes on the street) under which hosting works.
- *Milgram*: the minimal case — a place constituting a relationship (familiar strangers) with zero interaction; *Slacker* as a feature-length familiar-stranger field.
- *Duranton–Puga / Glaeser*: the economic translation — cities improve **matching** by thickening markets without proposing matches; the platform comparison drops out immediately (platform = matchmaker, city = host).
- *Austin material*: the host's material basis is contingent and perishable — bust-priced rent made the slack; the tech boom and gentrification repriced it away; the branding timeline (1991, 2000) shows the city trying to name, then defend, its own hosting function after the fact.

**The Oldenburg collision (§2.6), resolved as an asset.** Oldenburg's "third place" is an ordinal third (after home and work); the chapter's is a relational/structural third (the coordinating party). State the distinction once, then exploit it: Oldenburg's third places are the hosting third's *infrastructure* — the rooms in which the city does its hosting — and Oldenburg's in-venue "host who greets the regulars" contrasts perfectly with the city that hosts without any greeter. The near-collision is not an embarrassment; it is the argument in miniature.
