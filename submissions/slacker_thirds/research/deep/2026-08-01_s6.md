## Finding 1 — The "examiner" sentence is cited to the wrong Benjamin, and in the right Benjamin the examiner is the director (high, 3-0) [C1]

The chapter's §6 sentence "an examiner, if a distracted one" is attributed to the same essay as note
[^42], the Second Version at *Selected Writings* 3: 108. That attribution is wrong. "The public is an
examiner, but an absent-minded one" belongs to Harry Zohn's *Illuminations* translation, §XV. In the
Second Version the word "examiner" occurs exactly once, and it names the film DIRECTOR, not the
audience; the Second Version's figure for the audience is the "quasi-expert" (§XIII, p. 114). The
training sentence in note [^42] itself checks out at p. 108, wording "apperceptions and reactions,"
essay pp. 101–133. Awkwardly for the chapter, Benjamin's examiner is a selector rather than a
spectator. BOTH.

**Sources:** Walter Benjamin, "The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility: Second
Version," in *Walter Benjamin: Selected Writings, Volume 3, 1935–1938*, ed. Howard Eiland and Michael
W. Jennings (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 2002), 101–133. No DOI (book chapter). Page-image scan used
for verification: https://ctwgwebsite.github.io/assets/pdf/zfs/benjamin-wamr-2nd.pdf ; Walter Benjamin,
"The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction," trans. Harry Zohn, in *Illuminations*, ed.
Hannah Arendt (New York: Schocken), §XV. No DOI. https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/philosophy/works/ge/benjamin.htm ;
Shih-Yen Huang, "Body, Technology, and Art: On Walter Benjamin's 'Work of Art' Essay and Related
Writings," *Ex-position* 48 (2022): 123ff, at 125. DOI 10.6153/EXP.202212_(48).0007.
https://ex-position.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/08-Shih-Yen-Huang.pdf

**Evidence:** Second Version, p. 108, verbatim, running head "108 · 1936": "play. This applies
especially to film. The function of film is to train human beings in the apperceptions and reactions
needed to deal with a vast apparatus whose role in their lives is expanding almost daily." p. 111,
verbatim: "The film actor performs not in front of an audience but in front of an apparatus. The film
director occupies exactly the same position as the examiner in an aptitude test." §XIII, p. 114,
verbatim: "It is inherent in the technology of film, as of sports, that everyone who witnesses these

## Finding 2 — Benjamin's apparatus already tests and removes people (medium, 2-1) [C11]

On p. 111 of the Second Version, in §X, the standardized work process "daily generates countless
mechanized tests" whose failures are "excluded from the work process," and film is presented as the
public exhibition of that same test. This complicates the chapter's line between §4/§5 and §6: if a
removal power is already in Benjamin's account of what cinema trains people for, the platform's three
added powers cannot all be introduced as post-cinematic. COMPLICATES.

**Sources:** Benjamin, "The Work of Art... Second Version," in *Selected Writings* 3: 101–133, at 111
(§X). No DOI. https://ctwgwebsite.github.io/assets/pdf/zfs/benjamin-wamr-2nd.pdf

**Evidence:** p. 111, verbatim: "Meanwhile the work process, especially since it has been standardized
by the assembly line, daily generates countless mechanized tests. These tests are performed unawares,
and those who fail are excluded from the work process. But they are also conducted openly, in agencies
for testing professional aptitude." Four lines on, same page: "These tests, unlike those in the world of
sports, are incapable of being publicly exhibited to the degree one would desire. And this is precisely
where film comes into play. Film makes test performances capable of being exhibited, by turning that
ability itself into a test." And, continuing on the same page: "For the majority of citydwellers,
throughout the workday in offices and factories, have to relinquish their humanity in the face of an
apparatus. In the evening these same masses fill the cinemas, to witness the film actor taking revenge
on their behalf not only by asserting his humanity (or what appears to them as such) against the
apparatus, but by placing that apparatus in the service of his triumph."

*Verifier corrections carried, and the dissent.* Two lenses confirmed every quotation verbatim and
tightened the page cite: the whole passage sits on p. 111, so cite p. 111, not pp. 110–111. Both also
noted that the director-as-examiner sentence from Finding 1 sits in this very passage, which makes
Benjamin's examiner-selector and Benjamin's excluding apparatus one thought rather than two.

The third lens refuted the claim's framing and its objection should be carried into the section. "The
exact triad the chapter reserves for platforms" does not hold. The testing and exclusion belong to the
assembly line's work process, not the camera's; where the apparatus does the testing in Benjamin's
account, its subject is the film ACTOR, with the director as examiner. Nothing in the passage has the
camera reading, pricing, or removing a SPECTATOR, and the chapter's triad describes what a platform
does to the parties on either side of a match. Corrected statement, which is narrower and still worth
having: Benjamin already places a testing-and-exclusion power in the technological apparatus of which
cinema is the public exhibition, so removal cannot be called wholly post-cinematic — but the tested
party is the performer, and pricing is absent from the account entirely.

## Finding 4 — The Kuleshov sentences need two repairs: 55% is eleven of twenty, and the effect got new positive evidence in 2024 (high, 3-0) [C5]

The chapter's "experienced viewers fused face and context" implies a unanimity the data do not have.
Ildirar & Ewing report 100% of experienced viewers making the spatio-temporal link but only 55% — eleven
of twenty — reading the emotion, and only in the sadness condition. Separately, note [^44]'s hedge is out
of date: a 2024 study using authentic film footage rather than static images reports the effect
behaviourally and with fMRI, so "contested" now means an active dispute with recent positive evidence.
That study measured no film-experience variable, so it says nothing about whether the reading is learned.
BOTH.

**Sources:** Sermin Ildirar and Louise Ewing, "Revisiting the Kuleshov Effect with First-Time Viewers,"
*Projections* 12, no. 1 (2018): 19–38. DOI 10.3167/proj.2018.120103.
https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/62291/1/Accepted_manuscript.pdf ; Zhengcao Cao, Yashu Wang,
Liangyu Wu, Yapei Xie, Zhichen Shi, Yiren Zhong, and Yiwen Wang, "Reexamining the Kuleshov effect:
Behavioral and neural evidence from authentic film experiments," *PLOS ONE* 19, no. 8 (2024): e0308295.
DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0308295. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0308295

**Evidence:** Ildirar & Ewing, abstract, verbatim: "Results confirm the presence of the Kuleshov effect
for experienced viewers (explicitly only in the sadness condition) but not the first-time viewers, who
failed to perceive continuity between the shots." Results, verbatim: "For the gravestone sequence, 100%
of experienced viewers made spatio-temporal links between the shots and 55% demonstrated a Kuleshov
Effect. That is, they all said that the man was standing in front of a gravestone and when asked how he
was feeling, 55% of them said that he looked sad/sorry." The other two sequences are weaker still: the
soup sequence at 30%, and for the child sequence "No participants showed a clear Kuleshov effect." On
the first-time viewers, verbatim: "Despite an intact ability to perceive and understand the content of
each shot, they perceived them to be wholly separate from each other and did not relate them
spatio-temporally." Sample, verbatim: "Forty participants (half female, 56–72 years old, M = 64.1 years)
took part in the study. The experimental group (20 participants, half female, 58–72 years, M= 66.4
years)..." — so twenty experienced viewers, and 55% is eleven of them. (Author accepted manuscript, UEA
Digital Repository; the manuscript carries interleaved line numbers, which defeats naive string search.)

Cao et al., abstract, verbatim: "However, concerns persist regarding the validity of previous studies,
often employing inauthentic film materials like static images, leaving the question of its existence in
authentic films unanswered... These results affirm the existence of the Kuleshov effect within authentic

functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). The behavioral results confirm the Kuleshov effect in the

*Verifier corrections carried.* The claim refused to print an author list for Cao et al. because two
independent retrievals returned irreconcilable romanisations. The refusal was right and both candidate
lists were wrong. All three lenses independently resolved the byline against the PLOS article page and
the Crossref deposit, which agree exactly: **Zhengcao Cao, Yashu Wang, Liangyu Wu, Yapei Xie, Zhichen

the paper never says "undergraduates," so write "a Chinese university sample"; and it reports no

learned. Write that, not "only experienced" participants.

## Finding 5 — The researchers' own account of what experienced viewers have is the chapter's selector thesis, and it names an agent (high, 3-0) [C8]

In the discussion of the 2018 audio paper, Ildirar, Levin, Schwan and Smith propose that what experienced
viewers have and first-time viewers lack is not a perceptual capacity but a pragmatic assumption: that
someone arranged these shots on purpose, for them, and that the arrangement will reward effort. This is
sharper than the chapter's "they assume the sequence coheres and hunt for its sense," because it names
the arranger. It is an unmanipulated hypothesis floated in a Discussion section, not a result. SUPPORTS.

**Sources:** Sermin Ildirar, Daniel T. Levin, Stephan Schwan, and Tim J. Smith, "Audio Facilitates the
Perception of Cinematic Continuity by First-Time Viewers," *Perception* 47, no. 3 (2018): 276–295. DOI
10.1177/0301006617745782. Author accepted manuscript: https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/20677/1/20677.pdf

**Evidence:** Discussion, MS pp. 21–22, verbatim: "If first-time viewers rely strongly on meaning to
integrate views, why do they sometimes fail when more experienced viewers from the same culture succeed?
One reasonable hypothesis is that the more experienced viewers better understand the pragmatic goals of
cinema. That is, they understand that views are arranged in the service of telling a story, and that in
cases where the story is not initially evident, some effort at problem-solving may be rewarded. This
problem-solving likely involves assumptions about something akin to directorial intent. For example, if
one assumes that views are arranged with the intent not of portraying some specific real environment,
but rather to specify a coherent story, it becomes clear that few salient elements of the film are
accidental." Worked example, MS p. 18, on a pond shot with a buzzing sound cut to an extreme close-up of
a bee, verbatim: "For example, one experienced viewer commented while watching, 'there must be a
motorbike passing by the lake... no, no, it is a bee'. In contrast, first-time viewers often did not
reinterpret the sound, and in some cases misinterpreted the close-up as depicting a very large bee."
Four lines earlier: "Finally, no first-time viewers integrated the views in film 15" — 0 of 20.

*The limit, unanimous across three lenses:* nothing in the design isolates "directorial intent" from
familiarity with content. The passage is hedged in the source itself ("One reasonable hypothesis,"
"likely involves"). Write that the researchers propose it. Never that they showed it.

*Verifier corrections carried:* cite the long passage to MS pp. 21–22 of the BIROn manuscript; a running
head falls inside it, between "it becomes" and "clear that few salient elements." Page cites to this
passage will not correspond to *Perception* 276–295. The source closes the bee quotation with the period
outside the quote marks.

## Finding 6 — The first-time-viewer deficit is substantially an artifact of silent stimuli, and the same team's fourth study is where a hostile reviewer will start (medium, 2-1) [C4]

The chapter's evidence that cut-reading is learned rests on three silent-stimulus studies. The same
research team then added diegetic sound and found first-time Turkish viewers integrating shots
successfully in most conditions tested, including 100% on the outside/inside transition that had defeated
them in the silent studies and 100% across cuts violating the 180-degree rule. The design is
audio-support levels, not sound-versus-silence, so this makes silence a strong candidate explanation for
part of the earlier deficit rather than demonstrating it. COMPLICATES.

**Sources:** Ildirar, Levin, Schwan, and Smith, "Audio Facilitates the Perception of Cinematic Continuity
by First-Time Viewers," *Perception* 47, no. 3 (2018): 276–295. DOI 10.1177/0301006617745782.
https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/20677/1/20677.pdf

**Evidence:** Abstract, verbatim (including the source's own comma splice): "Previous studies concluded
that first-time film viewers (Schwan & Ildirar, 2010; Ildirar & Schwan, 2015) often had difficulty
integrating shots into a coherent representation of the depicted events in the absence of a familiar
action through the film cuts or a salient eye-gazing of a character in the film. In this study we
investigated whether diegetic sound (i.e. sound that seems to originate from the depicted cinematic
space) could effectively bridge shots for first-time viewers. Across a range of films, both dialog, and
salient environmental sound (e.g., barking dogs) helped first-time viewers connect shots. However, sound
was not always successful in supporting first-time viewers' interpretations." Results, MS p. 16,
verbatim: "All first-time viewers reported that they saw two men standing opposite each other greeting
one another (films 1 and 2). This was also the case when interpreting the similar eyeline violation film
clips (e.g. clips violating the 180 degree rule; films 3 and 4)." Discussion, verbatim: "In previous
studies, first-time viewers had difficulty interpreting transitions from an exterior view to an inside
view (Schwan & Ildirar, 2010), even when the outside was indicative of an object or an activity that was
performed inside (Ildirar & Schwan, 2015). However, with the addition of sound, first-time viewers had
little difficulty interpreting these sequences. For example, 100% of first-time viewers correctly
understood the relationship between an initial shot in which a man can be seen outdoors calling out a
woman's name and a subsequent shot that depicted a woman inside a house" — the sentence continues
"...even though she did not react to the call (film 5 and 6), and when the person doing the calling could
not be seen (films 7, 8, 9)." The authors flag their own ceiling, MS p. 21, verbatim: "They did so just
as successfully as rule-consistent films, but it is important to note that 100% of viewers successfully
integrated both kinds of films, so any difference that might have been observed could have been obscured
by a ceiling effect." Method, MS p. 11, verbatim: "Forty participants (23 female, 53-85 years old, M =
66.35 years) took part in the study. The experimental group (20 first-time viewers, 13 female, 53-85
years, M= 71.65 years) knew of the existence of television and had some abstract ideas about it, but had
no prior direct experience with the medium."

*The dissent, and it is the useful part.* The third lens refuted the causal framing and its objection is
carried into the corrected statement above. There is no silent condition in this study; every clip
carried audio, and the manipulation was levels of audio support. The comparison to the 2010 and 2015
silent studies is across studies with different stimuli, different films, and partially overlapping
participants — the paper notes "4 of these participants had been in the Schwan and Ildirar (2010) study
and three had been in Ildirar & Schwan (2015)." So "the same research team demonstrated this" is too
strong. The same lens also flagged suppressed limits that belong in any footnote: the abstract's own
"these viewers still had difficulty integrating views of unfamiliar environments," with "Only 25% of
first-time viewers integrated these views, while all experienced viewers did so" on film 14, and no
first-time viewers integrating film 15.

*Verifier corrections carried:* cite quotations to the BIROn manuscript pagination, not *Perception*
47(3): 276–295. Stopping the 100% quotation at "house" is Chicago-permissible and does not distort — the
omitted clause strengthens the point — but the writer should know the sentence runs on.

## Finding 7 — Note [^44b] cites the wrong paper for gaze prediction, and the right paper's successor says the driver is motion (high, 3-0) [C9]

The chapter cites Smith & Mital 2013 for "the filmmaker's ability to predict where the audience looks."
That paper cannot support it: its stimuli were UNEDITED videos of real-world scenes — no filmmaker, no
editing, no cut — and its central finding is that viewing task REDUCES attentional synchrony. Loschky et
al. measured what the chapter wants and quantified it. Their own team then found the tyranny is driven by
motion: presented as a slideshow, the same scene let the no-context group's gaze wander. BOTH.

**Sources:** Tim J. Smith and Parag K. Mital, "Attentional synchrony and the influence of viewing task on
gaze behavior in static and dynamic scenes," *Journal of Vision* 13, no. 8 (2013): 16. DOI
10.1167/13.8.16. Record: https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/7766/ (cite the open-access ARVO version) ; Lester C.
Loschky, Adam M. Larson, Joseph P. Magliano, and Tim J. Smith, "What Would Jaws Do? The Tyranny of Film
and the Relationship between Gaze and Higher-Level Narrative Film Comprehension," *PLOS ONE* 10, no. 11
(2015): e0142474. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0142474.
https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0142474 ; John P. Hutson, Prasanth
Chandran, Joseph P. Magliano, Tim J. Smith, and Lester C. Loschky, "Narrative Comprehension Guides Eye
Movements in the Absence of Motion," *Cognitive Science* 46, no. 5 (2022): e13131. DOI 10.1111/cogs.13131.
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cogs.13131

**Evidence:** Smith & Mital, abstract, verbatim: "To dissociate exogenous from endogenous factors during
dynamic scene viewing we tracked participants' eye movements while they (a) freely watched unedited
videos of real-world scenes (free viewing) or (b) quickly identified where the video was filmed
(spot-the-location). ... These differences were minimized by the viewing task. In comparison with the
free viewing of dynamic scenes, during the spot-the-location task fixation durations were shorter,
saccade amplitudes were longer, and gaze exhibited less attentional synchrony and was biased away from
areas of flicker and people."

Loschky et al., abstract, verbatim: "Viewers' eye movements showed strong attentional synchrony in both
conditions as compared to a chance level baseline, but smaller differences between conditions. ... Thus,
overall, the results were more consistent with the tyranny of film hypothesis than the mental model
hypothesis." Body, verbatim: "A comparison of effect sizes suggests that the Tyranny of Film effect
(i.e., greater attentional synchrony than the shuffled baseline) is at least six times larger than the
Mental Model effect"; and "our analyses of attentional synchrony showed strong evidence that the
filmmakers influenced when and where viewers attended, irrespective of their viewing condition or
whether they made the critical inference." Stimulus: a 12-second, six-shot clip from *Moonraker*; the
Context condition additionally saw the preceding 2.5 minutes.

Hutson et al., abstract, verbatim: "The current study found that when the scene was shown as a slideshow,
the context manipulation produced differences in attentional selection (i.e., it attenuated attentional
synchrony)." General discussion, verbatim: "motion is likely a key driver of the tyranny of film effect
and an important moderator of the strength of top-down effects on attentional selection." Their own
limit, verbatim: "the study was an experimental case study (i.e., involved only a single complex
stimulus) which used a film clip many consider to be a masterpiece of cinema."

*Verifier corrections carried, and one retrieval failure.* The 6× figure is the ratio of the tyranny
effect to the mental-model effect. It is not a measure of how accurately a filmmaker predicts gaze; word
the sentence accordingly. Hutson's release from synchrony appeared in the no-context condition
specifically — "for the context condition there was no effect of presentation type" — so the slideshow
let impoverished event models wander rather than letting comprehension steer everyone's eyes. The Hutson
stimulus is the opening of *Touch of Evil*, not the *Moonraker* clip; the two studies use different
films, which matters if the section narrates them as one line of work. One lens verified the two Hutson
body quotations through Scholar Gateway full text and one could not reach them at all — *Cognitive
Science* 46(5) is not open access, Wiley 403s every route, the UAL deposit returns 401 — so the abstract
sentence, which carries the point on its own, is the safe thing to print until someone opens the version
of record.

## Finding 8 — The transfer limit is the field's position, stated by the researcher the section already leans on (medium, 2-1) [C7]

The chapter's "the transfer is where I stop" reads as the author's caution. It is also where the
literature stops. Smith, Essex and Bedford reviewed the evidence that screen experience produces general
cognitive benefit, concluded the evidence does not support it, offered domain-specificity as a candidate
explanation, and recorded that the causal trials have never been run. SUPPORTS.

**Sources:** Tim J. Smith, Claire Essex, and Rachael Bedford, "Are Movies Making Us Smarter? The Role of
Cinematic Evolution in the Flynn Effect," *Projections* 17, no. 1 (2023): 52–66. DOI
10.3167/proj.2023.170107. Accepted preprint:
https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21117/3/SmithEssexBedford_2023_Projections_accepted_preprint-3.pdf

**Evidence:** Preprint p. 8, verbatim: "The cognitive benefits may be specific to the domain of movie
narrative comprehension and not transfer to more general applications (such absence of transfer is often
seen in brain training; Nguyen, Murphy, and Andrews 2021)." p. 9, verbatim: "The evidence presented here
does not support the hypothesis that increasing exposure to intensifying movies, TV or touchscreens are
associated with a general IQ advantage." p. 7, on the causal record, verbatim: "Currently these
mechanisms have not been directly tested as the only way to show a direct causal impact of screen time on
attention control and visual processing would be to intervene in an individual's screen time whilst the
rest of their daily routines remain the same. Such randomized controlled trials of screen time with the
precise measures of attention outcomes required have not been conducted (Jones et al. 2021)." Their
summary of the field's split, p. 8, verbatim: "The basic building blocks of movie perception and
comprehension may be a byproduct of typical neurocognitive development (Ildirar and Smith 2018) but only
through experience with more challenging movie structures can we learn how to understand them (Smith
2012a; 2012b)." This is a commentary and review, not a new experiment.

*Verifier corrections carried, and the dissent.* All three lenses caught the same overstatement. "Named
domain-specificity as the likely story" is firmer than the text, which introduces the passage as "One
explanation could be..." and hedges with "may be specific to." Quote the hedge intact. The third lens
also refused the blanket no-transfer reading and supplied the sentence that cuts toward the chapter,
immediately after the IQ sentence: "Cutting's specific prediction that visual processing has quickened
over time in line with the intensification of movies is supported by longitudinal and experimental
evidence (Kostyrka-Allchorne, Cooper and Simpson 2017; Portugal et al. 2020; 2021; 2022)." Two further
fixes: the title is not "Are Movies Making Us Smarter?" alone — the subtitle "The Role of Cinematic
Evolution in the Flynn Effect" is part of the title of record, and a half-title bibliography entry is
what a copyeditor bounces. Preprint page numbers do not correspond to *Projections* 52–66.

## Finding 9 — The chapter's closing move is unsupported by its own footnote and contradicted by the one study that checked worker theories against the algorithm (medium, 2-1) [C6]

The last paragraph of §6 says platform workers are "building the second skill now, the hard way," and
footnotes it to Möhlmann, Alves de Lima Salge and Marabelli. That paper theorises a sensemaking process
that lets workers "keep up with algorithmic instructions"; it never claims they arrive at correct models.
Heiland compared two food-delivery algorithms against what riders believed. On the parameter riders
cared most about — speed — the shared theory was objectively wrong, and the wrongness disciplined them
into riding faster. On another — proximity to restaurants — it was correct and actively exploited.
Pooled sensemaking converges unevenly. COMPLICATES.

**Sources:** Heiner Heiland, "The social construction of algorithms: A reassessment of algorithmic

## How this lands in section 6

Four sentences are wrong as printed and have to change; one paragraph gets a better foundation than it has;
and the section's honest limit turns from a personal caution into a finding.

Start with the two mis-citations, because both are the kind a copyeditor or a board member catches. "An
examiner, if a distracted one" is not in the Second Version. It is Zohn's *Illuminations*, §XV, and in the
Second Version the examiner is the film director — which means Benjamin's examiner is a selector, not a
spectator. Either move the citation to Zohn or rebuild the sentence on the Second Version's own figure for
the audience, the "quasi-expert" of §XIII. The second mis-citation is in note [^44b]: Smith & Mital 2013
cannot support "the filmmaker's ability to predict where the audience looks," because its stimuli were
unedited videos of real-world scenes with no filmmaker in them, and its result is that viewing task
reduces attentional synchrony. Put Loschky et al. 2015 in that slot. Their sentence is closer to what the
section is arguing than anything the section currently cites: "the filmmakers influenced when and where
viewers attended, irrespective of their viewing condition or whether they made the critical inference."
Give the 6× figure as the ratio of the tyranny-of-film effect to the mental-model effect, which is what it
measures, and not as a measure of predictive accuracy.

Two quantities need correcting in place. "Experienced viewers fused face and context" should read eleven of
twenty, in the sadness condition only, with 30% on the soup sequence and no clear effect at all on the
child sequence. And note [^44]'s "a controlled 2016 rebuild found a modest version holds" is out of date:
Cao et al. 2024 report the effect in authentic film footage, behaviourally and with fMRI. That makes the
Kuleshov status an active dispute with recent positive evidence, and the useful thing to add is that Cao
et al. measured no film-experience variable, so the new positive evidence says nothing about whether the
reading is learned. The one contrast that does bear on learning is still Ildirar & Ewing's, and it now
runs eleven of twenty against zero.

Hansen's reach shortens. The quotation and the page are safe, but she bounds herself on p. 59 to "roughly
from the 1920s through the 1950s" and mainstream Hollywood, and "negotiated" is the fourth verb in a list
that opens with "rejected or disavowed." The section can keep the sentence and lose the century, or keep
the century and stop attributing it to Hansen.

The paragraph that gains most is the one about what experienced viewers do. "They assume the sequence
coheres and hunt for its sense" is reaching for something the researchers themselves wrote better, and
what they wrote names an agent: experienced viewers "understand that views are arranged in the service of
telling a story," and the problem-solving "likely involves assumptions about something akin to directorial
intent." Attributing intent to a hidden arranger is exactly the posture the chapter says the platform
inherits, stated by film cognition researchers about film viewers. Two conditions on using it. It is a
hypothesis in a Discussion section, never manipulated, so write that they propose it. And the study it
comes from is the one that complicates the section's evidence base: with diegetic sound added, the same
population of first-time viewers hit 100% on the outside/inside transition and 100% across 180-degree-rule
violations. The section leans on three silent-stimulus studies and must cite the team's fourth or be seen
to have cherry-picked. The honest narrowing is available and costs little: what is learned is bridging
shots without an audio scaffold, and even with sound the first-time viewers still failed on unfamiliar

The limit paragraph gets promoted. "The transfer is where I stop" can now say the field stops there too,
in the words of the researcher the section has been citing all along: the evidence "does not support the
hypothesis that increasing exposure to intensifying movies, TV or touchscreens are associated with a
general IQ advantage," the trials that would settle it "have not been conducted," and domain-specificity
is the candidate explanation the authors offer. Quote the hedge — "may be specific to" — and do not imply
the study would probably come back positive; absence of transfer is the default expectation in this
literature. Behind that sits the harder result of this run, which the section should state flatly: nobody
has run the study. Five search angles found no work relating editing fluency to comprehension of a
recommender or a dispatch queue; two field-defining reviews of algorithmic literacy, one covering 169
studies, contain no occurrence of film, cinema, montage, or shot; the field says of itself that "much
remains unknown, such as what predicts algorithmic literacy"; and the closest available test found general
media literacy "inconsistently related to algorithm awareness." The single paper that attributes
algorithmic mental models to prior mass-media experience has eighteen participants and offers the
attribution as a source of false expectations.

The closing move has to be rebuilt, and the replacement is better than the original. "Platform workers are
building the second skill now, the hard way" is footnoted to Möhlmann, Alves de Lima Salge and Marabelli,
who theorise a process that lets workers "keep up with algorithmic instructions systematically" — keeping
up, not getting it right, and nothing about contestation. The one study that checked worker theories
against the algorithm found riders convinced that speed drove their assignments, an A/B test showing it did
not, and the false belief disciplining them into riding faster: "these theories are objectively wrong."
The same riders were right about proximity to restaurants and exploited it. So convergence is uneven, not
absent, and the platform has no reason to correct the half that flatters it — "these obvious
misunderstandings work in the interest of the platforms and are not corrected by them." What workers build
is partly folklore the platform lets stand, which sharpens §7's enclosure argument instead of softening
it. Drop the bibliography fix that came with this claim: the chapter already spells Möhlmann correctly;
the triple-n is the publisher's.

One complication the section should absorb rather than duck, in two places. Hutson et al. found that a
slideshow of the same scene attenuated attentional synchrony for viewers without prior context, which
points at motion rather than the cut as the driver of the tyranny of film — a more brute mechanism than
learned cut-reading, and one that weakens the bridge from cut-literacy to feed-literacy. And Benjamin's
§X, on p. 111, already puts a testing-and-exclusion power inside the apparatus of which cinema is the
public exhibition: "those who fail are excluded from the work process." Removal is therefore not wholly
post-cinematic. The repair is narrow. In Benjamin the tested party is the performer and the examiner is
the director; nothing there has the apparatus reading or pricing a spectator. Concede exclusion, and rest
the platform's novelty on pricing each match, which no cinematic account contains.
