Run 2026-08-01 via the deep-research workflow, 5 search angles, 3-lens adversarial verification, vote
counts recorded per finding. Twelve claims went into verification; seven survived (refuted by at most one

## Finding 1 — No retrievable critic reported losing track of the film; the walkouts were about meaninglessness (high on the six sources, contested on the universal quantifier; 2-1) [C3]

The chapter's premise as printed — "Audiences watched it and enjoyed it, and never seemed lost in it" — is
refutable in one sentence by the Sundance premiere, where a third of the house walked out and a third fell
asleep. The premise survives when it is narrowed to what the reviews actually show: in the contemporaneous
notices this run could retrieve, no critic, hostile or friendly, reports difficulty tracking who was on
screen or why the film moved on. The complaints are boredom, tedium, monotony and pointlessness. Two
lenses accepted the narrowed statement as established by six sources. The third refused the universal
"across every retrievable contemporaneous review" and was right to: no systematic sweep of trade or
regional press was run, AFI's own source-citation table lists five 1991 trade items nobody in this project
opened, and two of the six sources sit closer to a tracking complaint than the claim allowed. Walking out
on a film you find pointless is a different act from losing the thread, and the section can say so — but
it must say it over an enumerated list of reviews, not over the whole 1991 press. SUPPORTS, in the
narrowed form only.

## Finding 2 — Linklater said in 1991 that the transitional characters were there to hold the viewer's hand, and said something adjacent in 2011 (high on both quotations, refuted on the "same words both times" inference; 2-1) [C9]

The strongest primary support for the section's premise is the director's own account, on the record twice.
In 1991 he described explaining the plan to his cameraman and dolly grip and told them the transitional
characters would carry the viewer. In 2011 he described the same balance at the level of the whole film's
design. The two statements share one phrase — "makes no sense on paper" — and nothing else; the 2011
quotation names an architecture, not the transitional characters, which go unmentioned. Two lenses passed
the finding on the strength of the quotations. The third refused the claim that the two describe the same
device, refused "the one documented case of a 1990 audience finding the film incoherent" as an unsupported
universal, and pointed out that neither source touches the taxi monologue, so neither can replace the
Speed footnote (Finding 6). What the record establishes, and it is a great deal, is that the coordinating
work of the handoffs was designed and stated as such by the filmmaker — which caps how much novelty the
chapter may claim for noticing it. BOTH.

**Sources:** Dana Thomas, "'Slacker' (R)," *Washington Post*, 23 August 1991 (no DOI).
http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/longterm/movies/videos/slackerrthomas_a09ef4.htm (retrieved via
http://web.archive.org/web/20200926233725/) ; Marc Savlov, "Slack to the Future," *Austin Chronicle*, 21
January 2011 (no DOI). https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2011-01-21/slack-to-the-future/

**Evidence:** Linklater in 1991, on explaining the plan to his cameraman and dolly grip, quoted by Thomas:
"They were like 'What?!' And I was like, 'No, no, no. We'll hold {the viewer's} hand. We'll have these
transitional characters. … It won't make any sense on paper, but will be kind of easy to watch in its own
kind of weird way.'" The braces are the *Post*'s insertion convention and the ellipsis is the *Post*'s
own, not this project's — lens 2 checked that specifically. — Linklater in 2011, quoted by Savlov, under
the speaker label RICHARD LINKLATER: "That was part of the plan. 'We're not going to offer a story but at
least we'll offer a comprehensive architecture that you can pass through and not feel completely
disoriented.' That was kind of the balance of making what makes no sense on paper somewhat
audience-friendly." — And the one instance this run could document of 1990 Austin viewers calling the film
incoherent turns out to concern street geography, with Linklater's answer describing continuity rules a
selector would use: "They were saying: 'They're walking around Sound Exchange and then they walk around
the corner and they're at Half Price Books! It doesn't make sense!' I remember trying to explain to some
guy on the street that, 'If you didn't know Austin, the film makes sense.' They're still walking right to
left. Syntactically, it works. It was just funny." The bracketed "[inaccurate]" earlier in that answer is
the *Chronicle*'s own editorial insertion.

*Correction applied, from lens 3 (use this wording):* "Linklater said in 1991 that the transitional
characters were there to hold the viewer's hand — 'It won't make any sense on paper, but will be kind of
easy to watch' — and in 2011 described the same balance at the level of the whole film: 'We're not going
to offer a story but at least we'll offer a comprehensive architecture that you can pass through and not
feel completely disoriented.'" Do not claim the two describe the same device. Do not offer either as a
replacement for the Speed footnote.

*Refutation to respect, from lens 3:* "the one documented case" is a universal nobody established. Macor
p. 102 records a Berlin screening that drew two people, and the *Dallas Times Herald* capsule of April
1990; no one surveyed the 1990 Austin correspondence. Write "one documented case," not "the."

## Finding 3 — The dessert line is Canby's of 22 March 1991, and the review it comes from is mixed, not hostile (high, 2-1 on the characterization only) [C6]

*Times* is an excerpt reprint timed to the Angelika opening and says so above the text. The chapter cites

the framing on two counts: "not from a 5 July 1991 review" overshoots, because the line is printed there;

fills with praise and Pierson calls "the definition of the word mixed," and contradicts Finding 1, which
files Turan as the hostile one. The correction is worth having anyway, because the complaint Canby does
make is monotony, and monotony is a complaint only a viewer who followed the whole film can lodge. BOTH.

## Finding 5 — The closest published anticipation of camera-as-linker sits in the flagship 2022 Linklater collection, and only its abstract has been read (high on the abstract, 3-0; the argument it makes is unverified) [C8]

what the chapter's camera paragraph does: the meandering camera drifts through Austin, "weaving together
the lives of eccentric characters." Boom frames this as topophilia, by way of Bruno, Lyotard and Deleuze,

loves a place — survives. That suggestion is a hypothesis and must be labelled one: only the abstract was
retrievable, through Crossref, because both the Oxford/Edinburgh Scholarship Online page and euppublishing
403 to every fetch and have no Wayback capture. All three lenses passed the finding and all three flagged
the same limit. COMPLICATES.

## Finding 6 — The chapter's first footnote cannot be supported, and asserting its negation would be worse (contested; 2-1, and the dissent is the one to follow) [C5]

Linklater through this speech before." Nothing in the retrievable record of that article supports the
claim. The abstract is about Linklater's relationship to Hollywood and a utopian quest for alternatives to
consumer gratification; the keyword list runs anti-intellectualism, Hollywood, independent, industry,

article's body could not be obtained by anybody in this run, and a negative about what a nine-page article
says cannot be established from four sentences of abstract — least of all when the title, "The
Possibilities of Roads Not Taken," is a plausible allusion to the monologue's own material ("the thing you
choose not to do… fractions off and becomes its own reality," illustrated by Dorothy and the Scarecrow at
"that crossroads"). A utopian reading and a monologue reading are not exclusive. COMPLICATES.

1991 sources put the character figure at about a hundred: Canby, "These are just some of a hundred or so
relentlessly eccentric people who amble in and out of Richard Linklater's 'Slacker'"; the *Times* news
report, "The 100 or so people in the film drift in and out of coffee shops, bars, second-hand-book stores
and ramshackle houses, almost seeming to bounce off each other"; Dana Thomas, "The 100 characters in
'Slacker' wear faded T-shirts and torn-up jeans and high-tops"; Turan, "there are apparently close to 100,
and counting them is probably as good a way as any to pass the time." Jonathan Rosenbaum says "around 90."
Weinstein's "about 97 separate 'slackers'" can be cited as a contemporaneous approximation punning on the
runtime, without asserting that he coined anything. "About a hundred" remains the defensible phrasing.

## How this lands in section 1

Three sentences in the section's first two paragraphs are wrong as printed and can be fixed in ten minutes
each. The credit reads "Should have stayed at bus station," with no article — AFI's cast table and a
frame-level reading of the print agree, and Criterion's website, which inserts the "the," is the divergent
text. The Dobie run was eleven weeks, roughly 27 July to 11 October 1990, not a year; §7's "held it a year"
goes with it, and the eleven-week figure is stronger for the argument anyway, because it is the number
Pierson took to Orion. And the dessert line comes from Canby's review of 22 March 1991, written out of New
Directors/New Films, reprinted in excerpt on 5 July to coincide with the Angelika opening. Call that review
mixed, because it is: Canby calls the cast "amazingly effective" and parts of the film "very funny" before
he complains of monotony, and Pierson, who was selling the film, called it "the definition of the word
mixed."

Footnote 1 has to go or be earned. Nothing retrievable about Speed 2007 supports "Lesley Speed has read
Linklater through this speech before," and — this is the part the section must not get wrong — nothing
retrievable refutes it either. The body is paywalled everywhere and the article's title is a plausible
allusion to the monologue's own crossroads material. So do not replace one unsupported assertion with its
unsupported negation. Order the article through a library and cite a page, or drop the note and disclaim
novelty with a source read end to end. Finding 2's interviews cannot stand in; neither touches the taxi
monologue.

The premise paragraph gets stronger by getting narrower. "Audiences watched it and enjoyed it, and never
seemed lost in it" cannot survive the Sundance premiere, where a third of the house walked out and a third
slept. Restate it over an enumerated list — Ebert, Hinson, Howe, Turan, *Variety*, Canby, Jonathan
Rosenbaum — as the claim that no critic, hostile or friendly, reported losing track of who was on screen or
why the film moved on, and that the complaints were boredom, tedium, monotony and pointlessness. Then put
the walkouts in the body rather than a footnote and say what they show: people left because the film seemed
to mean nothing, which is a different failure from losing the thread. Add the two lines that lean the other
way, since a reader will find them anyway — Canby's "The unusual begins to look numbingly normal" and
*Variety*'s "interest quickly rises and falls by virtue of who happens to be on screen" — and add the
retrieval limit in a note: the 1990–91 trade press, the *Village Voice* and *Sight and Sound* could not be
reached.

The best evidence in the section is the director's, and it caps the novelty claim rather than raising it.
Linklater told the *Washington Post* in 1991 that the transitional characters were there to hold the
viewer's hand — "It won't make any sense on paper, but will be kind of easy to watch in its own kind of
weird way" — and told the *Austin Chronicle* in 2011 that the film offers "a comprehensive architecture
that you can pass through and not feel completely disoriented." Quote both, and do not claim they describe
the same device: the 2011 line is about the whole film's design and never mentions the transitional
characters. The 1990 Austin complaint Savlov records is a gift for §3: viewers protested that the film's
street geography was impossible, and Linklater's answer is a selector's continuity rule — "They're still
walking right to left. Syntactically, it works."

The novelty probe changes what the section may claim, in one word. Nobody has separated a setting from a
selector and nobody has asked the three questions of any film; the decomposition is unoccupied across
eighteen Crossref queries, the full *ReFocus* contents, and Scholar Gateway. But three published readings
already put the linking work on the camera. Macor, p. 94 and pp. 95–96, has Linklater replacing *La
Ronde*'s narrator with "the fluid camera and a kind of 'baton-passing'" under a section heading that reads
"Chaos by Design." Boom's *ReFocus* abstract has the meandering camera "weaving together the lives of
eccentric characters." Ramirez has the camera marking "its dérive" through Austin. Name all three, and
state the wedge as a distinction from them rather than as first contact: they describe drift, relay and
topophilia; the chapter's question is whether the thing doing the linking knows who these two particular
people are. Do not call any of them "the closest anticipation" — the run assigned that superlative twice,
to different sources, and the body of Boom's chapter has not been read.

Two smaller repairs. The Macor gloss in §1 — "the material supplied by a whole scene rather than by
anyone's design" — sits directly against Macor's own heading; and "the least auteur film ever made" is Lee
Daniel's quip about credit, at p. 112, not her thesis about what holds the film together. And on the count:
keep "about a hundred," now footnotable rather than asserted, at 98 credited roles across 97 performers,
with four 1991 sources at "a hundred or so" and Jonathan Rosenbaum's dissenting "around 90." Do not blame
the *Los Angeles Times* for the 97-character error; Weinstein wrote an approximation, and nobody traced
where the hard number came from.
