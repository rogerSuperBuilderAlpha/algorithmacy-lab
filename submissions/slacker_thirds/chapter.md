# **"You gotta be somewhere": *Slacker* and the Search for Voice**

#### **Roger Hunt, Bentley University**

Filmed on 16mm across Austin in the summer of 1989, premiered at the Dobie Theatre in 1990, and released nationally in 1991, Richard Linklater’s *Slacker* follows roughly a hundred strangers with "no plot, no major characters, no suspense: just fleeting glimpses of bohemia in its twilight phase."[1] The movie has no traditional story. There is no hero whose day we follow from start to finish, and no plot that would tell you why the next person appears. The question the film keeps posing is simpler: how do you end up next to someone you were never introduced to?

That question is already in the opening sequence. A cab passenger (played by Linklater) unloads a monologue about alternate realities on a driver who barely listens.[2] He steps out onto the street. Seconds later, a car hits a woman. The camera leaves the victim and tracks the hit-and-run driver back to his house, where police arrest him for running over his own mother.[3] Nothing in the cab ride caused the crash. The passenger and the victim never meet. What joins them is that they were on the same block at the same time, and that the camera used that overlap as a reason to drop one person and take the other. That hand-off is how the rest of the movie moves: from stranger to stranger, down the block.

In ordinary life, when two people get connected, a third person often does the connecting—someone who introduces them, brokers a deal, or steps in to judge. In *Slacker*, that third never steps forward as a person. It stays in two places the characters cannot talk to: the street, which keeps putting bodies in reach of one another, and the camera, which decides which of those bodies to follow. Call that outside force a deferred third: a third that brings people together without ever getting between them. It introduces nobody. It settles nothing. It uses two strangers in the same place as a reason to leave one and take the other.

Linklater's line as he leaves the cab—"should have stayed at the bus station"—names what this setup costs. The only way not to be picked was never to step onto the pavement. Once you are on the street, you are in reach of the lens, and the lens does not ask.

Viewers usually try to explain how *Slacker* holds together with a few metaphors. The most common is the relay race: characters "pass the baton" from one to the next.[4] The baton image gets the hand-off right—someone leaves, someone else continues—and it stops there. It never asks who chooses the next runner, or why this runner rather than the person standing next to them. Others say the movie is held together by a shared attitude, a refusal to work,[5] or by nonstop talk.[6] Attitude and talk are what you hear. They are not what moves the camera. Each speech dies with the person delivering it, ignored by whoever walks on screen next. Linklater's dream of alternate realities only reaches the street because the camera drags it there; the driver never picked it up.

A third explanation is "drift." Critics call this *flânerie*: an aimless walker turning down whatever street looks interesting.[7][8][9] Drift describes how the camera *looks*—it ambles, it loiters. It gets who is in charge backward. A *flâneur* chooses where to look. In *Slacker*, the walkers do choose some things: rides, clubs, whether to speak. They do not choose who the camera takes next. At every intersection, the camera leaves one stranger for another and never looks back. It picks them out of the crowd.

Film theorists (Berg, Jeong) give the same chain a technical name, a "daisy chain": the story jumps from one person to the next without returning.[10] Others (Bizarro, Marlovits) go further and say the film rejects storytelling altogether, that the characters live in disconnected moments and that frustrating the audience's hunger for plot is the point.[11][12] If Linklater had really abandoned structure, the film would play like a shuffle of home videos, each clip independent of the last. Every transition is clean. Even if you cannot explain what *Slacker* is about, you always know where it is: someone leaves one scene, and the camera locks onto the next person down the block. The characters can be aimless because something else is doing the joining. That something is the city packing bodies onto the same streets, and the camera picking which stranger to follow.

Most movies with many characters rely on convergence: storylines meet, one action sets off another, strangers collide, and the collision matters later.[13] After the opening hit-and-run in *Slacker*, the son is arrested and that story ends. Nothing comes back. No later plotline returns to the mother, the cab, or the crime. The people never needed a shared plot in order to share a frame. Film theorists sometimes say the camera weaves a network between people on the ground, as if it were stitching them into one social web.[14] The camera is the web, but it does not stitch. It introduces nobody. It follows someone for a few minutes and leaves them the second another person crosses the frame. What keeps the movie moving is the city putting another stranger within reach, and the lens taking that reach as enough.

If the movie holds together without a plot, a shared attitude, or a weaver, then reading *Slacker* as a love letter to bohemia misses what Linklater built.[15] What he built is a working model of how people get coordinated now: packed into one space, picked by something that never enters as a person, and never asked. Georg Simmel, writing in 1908, already had a name for an outside force that can hold people together without stepping between them. Josiah Royce, a few years later, had a test for whether that holding makes a community or just a crowd. The packing-and-picking those tools describe was already running when the camera rolled. This essay argues that *Slacker* shows how a modern platform works: an indifferent machine that packs people into the same space and picks who meets whom, so that connection, shared belief, and a yes are not required.

## **Not the first to see a platform here**

Critics have been setting *Slacker* against the internet for years, mostly as something we lost. Scott Beauchamp, writing in 2019, calls it "a melancholy artifact of what we've lost": the early web's unpaid, amateur weirdness looked like Linklater's "democratic large-heartedness," and what replaced it was commerce, monetized attention, and algorithmic tribes.[16] Charles Bramesco treats the moon-landing man and the JFK author as harmless local characters in "the sweet, innocent pre-Internet world of the early '90s," before online forums could recruit them; Daniel Broadley reads them the same way, as a joke the platforms later stopped getting.[17] Reviewing Simon Reynolds, Chal Ravens sets the film's unhurried art-making against "restless loops of dopamine and dissatisfaction," and quotes Linklater: "The real conspiracy is that people don't have time to really think."[18]

These readings share three assumptions. There was an open, unhurried scene. The internet destroyed it. The proof is a handful of scenes—the cab dream, the UFO talkers, the camera's patience with whoever wanders in. Each critic finds a resemblance between 1991 Austin and the present, and stops there. The resemblance is the point.

Resemblance is a matter of style and mood: unpaid weirdness, local conspiracy talk, time to think. It never asks how a hand-off in the film actually works. Every hand-off in *Slacker* works the same way: the city puts two strangers in reach, the camera takes one, and nobody on screen is asked. That is not a portrait of a scene the internet later destroyed. It is a picture of coordination that does not need a yes. The film, shot in 1989, is already inside that machine. The thing the metaphors skip is the outside force that does the joining.

## **Simmel's postponed third**

That outside force is easy to miss, because every transition in *Slacker* looks like a meeting between two people: a pedestrian bumps someone on a corner, a customer talks to a clerk, a roommate walks in the door. A pair cannot explain why the movie moves, because neither person in the pair chooses who appears next. If the scene were only two people talking, it would stall in conversation or end when they walked away. The hand-off works because a third force is already there, packing the street and directing the camera.

The question of how something outside two people can organize them goes back to Georg Simmel's 1908 study of the triad. When scholars use Simmel, they almost always mean a third person who steps between two others.[19] A mediator in a dispute. Someone who profits off two rivals. An authority who divides people to keep control. Each of those setups assumes the two already know each other. The third is in the middle, dealing with them.

The cab passenger and the hit-and-run victim have no relationship, and no mediator steps between them. The film splits the work that a person in the middle would have done. Austin packs bodies into the same space without introducing anyone. The camera chooses who to follow when paths cross. That choice is selection, not an introduction: nobody is presented to anybody; we just follow the next person down the block. Neither the city nor the camera ever enters the scene as a person in the middle. They stay in the background.

Simmel already had a name for a third that stays in the background:

It is sociologically very significant that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them. This applies as much to the alliance between states for the purpose of defense against a common enemy as to the "invisible church" which unifies all faithful in their equal relation to the one God. The group-forming, mediating function of a third element will be discussed in a later context.[20]

He postponed the idea because it broke his definition of a triad.[21] For Simmel, a triad needs three people actually interacting. The postponed third stands so far off that no interaction takes in all three at once. An invisible God does not sit down with two believers. Two allied nations do not hold a three-way summit with their enemy. Simmel's postponed third is an outside point people share, not a third person in the room. In the film, Austin and the camera are that outside point. The cab passenger and the woman on the asphalt never interact with it. They are joined only by both being in its way.

When Simmel came back to this two hundred pages later, in his chapter on conflict, he showed why that outside point works: it can line up people who share nothing else.[22] Even rivals fall into line by facing the same outside force. That outside point is the start of what sociology calls the supra-individual: a structure that lives outside people, lasts longer than they do, and coordinates them from above. When two people deal only with each other, the relation dies if one walks away. When they both look toward a church, a state, a market, or the law, the order keeps running whoever comes or goes.

What those models still assumed was buy-in. For Simmel, Durkheim, and the people who followed them, the supra-individual still lived in people's heads.[23] An outside point only coordinates you if you recognize it: believers worship the God, citizens acknowledge the state, allies share a fear of the enemy. The people inside the field have to look up at it and treat it as theirs.

*Slacker* takes that looking-up away. Austin dumps the cab passenger and the hit-and-run bystanders onto the same pavement. Nobody looks up at Austin as a church or a state. Nobody addresses the camera making the movie. Characters on screen handle cameras—a street interview, a toy PixelVision, Super-8 at dawn—but they never turn to the one that is filming them, which is the one doing the picking.

The film's third is an indifferent web. Austin crowds bodies together. The camera is the web: it locks onto a pedestrian, follows the meeting, and turns at the next corner. You do not have to believe in a web to be caught in it. Stepping onto the pavement is enough. That is the shift from Simmel's church or enemy to this film: you do not look up at the setup and claim it. You stand in it. Nobody has to agree. Presence is enough.

If presence is enough to keep the movie moving, a hundred talkative people on the same street will look like a community. Does sharing the pavement make Austin one?

The film answers before the first reel is over. When the camera comes back to the hit-and-run on 24th Street, the victim is still lying on the asphalt. Nobody gathers. One passerby uses the dying woman as a way to talk to a jogger. Another takes her purse. The camera leaves with the thief. Sharing a patch of concrete produces no care. The crowd is isolated bodies on the same street. Being packed together is not belonging to one another.

## **Royce's test for membership**

Simmel shows how an outside force can hold people in one field. Holding them there still does not tell you whether they belong to one another. That is a different question, and it has a test.

In 1913, Josiah Royce laid down that test.[24] A group becomes a community—what he called a community of memory or expectation—when its members claim the same past or future events as their own. A community of memory shares a past each person takes as his. A community of expectation shares a future the same way. His example is two Māori men who claim descent from the same canoe, the *Tainui*.[25] They do not have to be friends. They can be enemies who live miles apart and refuse to speak. They still belong to one community because both look back at that voyage and say: *that happened to me; that is who I am.*

Royce allows a lot. Distance, hostility, silence. Two believers who disagree can still share a church. Two rivals who will not speak can still share a canoe.

He will not drop one thing: you have to claim the event.[26] Each person has to stretch a life in imagination over a past they did not live or a future they have not seen, and take at least one identical event as theirs. Without that claim, people are just in the same place. Simmel's examples pass the test: allies take the enemy as their own coming threat; believers take a sacred history as their own past. They look up, and they claim. *Slacker* fails. Sharing a city is not sharing a history. Bumping into someone is not Royce's event. The people on screen are on the same pavement. They claim no common past and no common future. They are unclaimed strangers.

Linklater runs the test when two men cross paths on the Drag. One is looking for a missing friend named Gary. The stranger who latches on folds Gary into a speech about moon transmissions and corporate mind-control drugs. They share the same concrete. Neither claims a shred of the other's story. Gary is a gap, not a memory: nobody on screen takes him as *ours*. The conversation has no common ground. When they separate, it evaporates, and the stranger tries the next passerby. Two men, same street, no claimed event. That is a crowd, not a community.

A platform is that crowd with software doing the camera's job. Replace Austin with a city of drivers, replace the camera with an interface that assigns the next ride, and the pattern is the same. Drivers and couriers who never meet are packed into a city and picked by an app. They do not claim each other. They do not have to believe in the system. They are caught in it.

## **The platform already running**

The software is new. The packing and picking are not. Later reviews still treat *Slacker* as the "before"—a pre-internet preserve, with platforms arriving later to break public life into monetized attention and tribes.[16][17] They have the sequence backward. People stopped needing to agree in order to be coordinated a generation before the app, when what you held, and what money was worth, came unstuck from things you could own and from any yes you had given.

Charles Reich named the first half of that shift in 1964. What people increasingly held, he said, was *largess*: a license, a benefit, a franchise, a grant—not a farm or a shop you owned, but a line on someone else's list, which that someone could take back.[27] Your right to work, to drive, to stay in a program, lived as a permission. The person who wrote the list could end it without saying why.

Less than a decade later, the same thing happened to money. On August 15, 1971, Nixon closed the gold window. By March 1973 the last fixed rates were gone, and the major currencies floated.[28] From that spring, the dollar in your pocket was no longer tied to gold in a vault or to a public agreement about its value. Dealers in a spread-out market set the price each day, under nobody's pact. You still spent the dollar. You had not been asked.

Three months later, on June 25, 1973, Reuters switched on Monitor Money Rates in London. Banks posted rates to a screen, the system picked which quotes a subscriber saw, and the world's most important market moved onto a ledger you could only see on a terminal.[29] There had never been a single floor for currencies the way there was for wheat, stocks, or gold—one room where everyone met and shouted. Monitor made that floor out of software and screens: a market that existed on the display and nowhere else.[30]

Packing and picking, in that market, had already replaced a person in the room. All the world's money sat in one field. That is packing. A screen picked which rates you saw. That is picking. Nobody whose wages it repriced was asked. Seventeen years before *Slacker* opened at the Dobie, this was already ordinary.

Read against that date, the opening cab ride is a man describing a change he cannot name. A passenger just off a bus talks to a silent driver about parallel worlds, other choices, a "whole 'nother reality" where he stayed at the station—each world that "thinks of itself as this." The change has no office you can walk into and no official who will explain it. So he reaches for the nearest story he has: a dream of other realities, including the one in which he never stepped onto the street.

When something has already rewritten the ground under you and you cannot read it, you report whatever signs you can. Hand people a book they cannot read, and they will do that. Linklater's opening monologue sounds like that. Reich's grants and the money market on a screen had been running the lives of everyone on that pavement for years. Neither had a face. The man in the cab has no words for it, so he talks about parallel worlds.

Caught in a machine he cannot name or leave, he can still do one thing. He can talk. The rest of *Slacker* is people talking.

## **No exit, so voice**

They do try to leave first. Midway through, a man carries a typewriter to a creek bridge, a friend coaching him: "the typewriter isn't the point, the point is it symbolizes the bitch that just fucked him over." In it goes, while a third friend reads a marked passage from *Ulysses* over the splash.[31] That is meant as an exit: throw the tool, leave the life. It fails. The camera is still running. It takes the next stranger on the bank, and the day continues.

At dawn, another group drives to Mount Bonnell and throws a Super-8 camera off the cliff. That throw ends the film, and it belongs to the film, not to anyone in it. A character cannot get out by destroying a machine. The web does not break because metal hit the water. Destroying the gear does nothing. The trap was Austin, which keeps packing bodies onto the same streets, and Linklater's camera, which keeps picking.

Albert Hirschman named this bind in 1970, between Reich's essay and the float. When you can no longer leave an organization, he said, what remains is voice: complaint, protest, argument, the attempt to be heard.[32] Hirschman assumed someone had to listen—a firm that feared losing customers, a state that feared revolt. Voice works, in his account, because the listener has something to lose. This machine has no such fear. The city does not mind if you complain. The camera does not need your loyalty. When you cannot leave and nothing is obliged to hear you, talk is just talk. The characters talk all day, trying to be heard inside a web that does not care.

The talk in *Slacker* takes a few forms, and none of them changes who appears next. Some try to explain everything. The moon-landing man shadows a stranger home with secret colonies and suppressed transmissions. An author in a bookstore pitches *Conspiracy a-Go-Go* to whoever is in the aisle. These speeches try to make the whole world make sense, as if a complete story would count as being heard. Some try taking. A T-shirt seller calls terrorism "the surgical strike capability of the oppressed." A young burglar leaves a house with a rare Marquis de Sade. Taking is a way of acting when talk has no addressee. Some try belief. The old anarchist who catches the burglar pours him coffee and talks about Leon Czolgosz: "there was such a thing as belief put into action in those days." His daughter calls it "the Hemingway Brigade." Then the old man says the line the film turns on: "I can only address myself to singular human beings now." Belief—what churches and states used to need, the looking-up that made Simmel's God or enemy work—survives here as an old man's story about himself, offered to one stranger over coffee.

Some try refusing. A man dictating to a friend who walks off asks "who's ever written the great work about the immense effort required in order not to create." A hitchhiker tells a street camera he will work "when I hear the true call." A passerby gets a card: "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as apathy."[33] That card is the film's name for slacking: stepping back without going numb. Inside this machine, withdrawing still leaves you on the street. Work or slack, presence is enough, and the camera will take the next body either way.

By dawn the addressee is gone. A tape plays a meditation to an empty sidewalk. A car with a loudspeaker tells Austin to wake up. A man shouts a "weapons giveaway program" into a recorder. Voice into plastic and tape: the same talk as at noon, with nobody left to hear it.

None of this talk moves a single frame. The city and the camera ran all day without needing a word of it. The characters speak endlessly because speaking is what you do when you cannot leave and nothing is obliged to hear you. The machine never needed the talk. There is still one place in the film where a voice meets a face and gets an answer. A door.

## **The human gatekeeper**

A door is a place a person still stands in the middle. Voice never moves the camera, but it can still hit a pair of ears. The packing and picking are already running everywhere else. At this door, for a minute, someone can still be asked.

Outside a club at night, a man credited as S-T-E-V-E tells his friends they are in: his roommate's band is playing, he has the van, he got on the guest list.[34] He parks bragging they keep his spot and that he is "tight" there. At the door he quotes the list. The door quotes a price: three dollars, what anyone not on the list pays. He spells his name—"The name's Steve. S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four"—and the bouncer says, "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you." Steve: "Fuck. I'm not on the list."

What happens at that door is four small operations. The club names a price. It checks a private record, the list. It refuses him to his face. And it gives no reason beyond the record itself. Steve's guest-list claim is the largess Reich described: a benefit someone else keeps and can take back.[35][36] Steve treats an informal hope as a ticket he owns. Reich said denial without a reason "should no longer be tolerated." A club door never had to explain.[37] What Steve still has, and what the platform will take away, is a person to talk to. The bouncer is on the sidewalk, hearing the refusal land. The door owes him nothing. The person who said no is still in front of him.

Later, at another door, a man copies a stamp wrist to wrist and the group walks in free. A stamp is a mark on a body; you can see it and pass it. The guest list is a record only the club keeps. That is why Steve could not talk his way in. He was arguing about a line he could not see.

That difference is the platform in miniature. The platform keeps the hidden record and drops the person who could hear you argue about it. You can lick a stamp onto a wrist because the stamp is on a body. A name that is not in Uber's records cannot be licked onto a wrist, and there is no bouncer on the sidewalk to tell you why.

All day the city packed people in, and the camera picked. When David Stark and Ivana Pais describe platforms co-opting "the behavior of providers and users, enrolling them in the practices of algorithmic management without managerial authority having been delegated to them," they mean the software enrolls you by using what you already do—driving, walking, being on the street—without anyone having been put in charge of you.[38] That is the same split as the film's: the software does the camera's job, and the bouncer is gone. On the Austin sidewalk in 1989, someone was still there to be asked. The platform took the person off the door.

## **The same machine without a face**

Once that person is gone, scholars still look for him. Platform writers want a broker, a mediator, Simmel's *tertius gaudens*—the "laughing third" who stands between two people and profits by keeping them apart.[39] That figure is a person in the middle. They see the platform's outside position and reach for those words, so they miss what *Slacker* already showed: a machine that coordinates people without standing between them, and without anyone looking up at it.[40] Uber is that machine with an app.

The four things that happen at Steve's door show up again in platform documents, with the bouncer removed. The cover charge becomes the fare. The list becomes a ratings cutoff: fall below it and you lose the app. The refusal becomes deactivation: the account goes dark. The withheld reason is policy: the company keeps "sole discretion" to shut you off, and explains only when it wants to.[41] A survey of 810 California drivers by Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus found two thirds reporting suspension or deactivation, and thirty percent of those given no reason.[42] The sample is self-selected. The four moves still land, city-wide, with no door in sight.

Steve could talk back. The bouncer owed him nothing, and Steve's frustration still hit a pair of ears. On the app, that person is gone. Drivers type "escalate to manager" into queues that send scripts, and get shutoff notices that say only that the terms were breached.[43] A driver typing "escalate to manager" is the man at dawn shouting into a recorder. Voice with nobody obliged to listen. Hirschman's third term, loyalty, does not apply, because loyalty is what you give an organization that asked you to stay. The platform never asked for a yes.

The European Union now requires that any decision to suspend or terminate a platform worker's account "shall be taken by a human being."[44] The law is trying to put a person back at the door. It would not order that if the post were filled.

Putting a person back at the door would restore a face. It would not give drivers a shared story. A driver in Austin is ranked against every other driver in Austin. They share no past they would call theirs. They do not look at the others and say, under Royce: *that is mine.* The click they gave Uber was a yes to the company, not to one another.[45] They start doing what the software rewards by being on the street when it sweeps past. Packed, picked, and unclaimed: the same crowd as 24th Street, with an interface instead of a lens.

From a distance, a crowded city full of talk looks like a lost counterculture, which is what Beauchamp and the others see. Up close, it is people packed onto the same streets.[46][47] Platforms added the camera's job: picking who actually meets whom.[48][49] They select, and they walk away.

## **Conclusion**

Most movies with many characters need storylines that meet, pay off, and collide. *Slacker* holds together without any of that, because it has an indifferent machine. The city crowds you. The camera picks you. Talk, belief, and a yes are not required. For one more minute in 1989, a door still said no to your face. Platforms kept crowding and picking. They took the person off the door.

The film's last unanswered question is the one it started with. Early on, a man walks the Drag asking after Gary. The movie never produces him. Gary is a gap the camera walks past: the introduction the machine never makes, the person you were never going to be presented to. Now that nobody has to answer, we are still on the pavement with the people on screen, still talking, still asking whether anyone has seen him around.
