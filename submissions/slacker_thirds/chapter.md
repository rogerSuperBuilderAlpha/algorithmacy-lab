# 'You seen Gary around?': The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

*Slacker* follows about a hundred people through one day in Austin. There is no protagonist. Almost no one meets twice. The film still holds. What holds it is a deferred third that does two jobs. The city keeps the next person somewhere on the sidewalk and introduces nobody. The camera chooses which person we see.

In 2011 Richard Linklater told Marc Savlov he saw that drift as "either channel surfing or … going in and out of different movies," a "primitive version of what now would be exponentially more complex."[^1] Maria Teresa Soldani makes the resemblance a matter of form. *Slacker*, she writes, is a twenty-four-hour urban narration whose camera "drifts along the street space, from character to character and from place to place."[^2] The two jobs are already in that sentence: the city holds, and the camera chooses.

A platform later does both. It holds strangers available to one another and arranges none of their meetings. It charges, lists, refuses, and withholds a reason, and no one stands in the street to be asked. In 2020 David Stark and Ivana Pais described platform owners who co-opt providers and users, enrolling them in algorithmic management without anyone having delegated managerial authority to them.[^3] *Slacker* already has contribution without a contract, and someone still there to argue with. The day shows what those platforms replaced: how to argue with a door, copy a stamp, say a join out loud.

David Bordwell has warned against treating multi-protagonist films as symptoms of the internet. Network narratives do solve storytelling problems, and they predate the technologies invoked to explain them.[^4] I am not contesting that. The claim here is only what the film still makes visible. The arrangement was already on the sidewalk. The platforms later ran it with no one there to ask.

## Philosophical Origins in Simmel's Postponement and Royce's Canoe

The film opens on a taxi and a woman struck in the street. What they share is a city. Nobody introduces them.

Georg Simmel had a name for a third that never stands between a known pair, and he set the analysis aside. In his triad chapter the non-partisan and the mediator repair a relation between two identified parties. The *tertius gaudens*, the laughing third, profits by standing between them. *Divide et impera*, divide and rule, sets them against one another in order to rule both.[^5] Each of those thirds already knows the pair. On the opening page of that same chapter he writes of a third that unifies without standing between anyone, then sets the thought aside. Kurt Wolff translates the observation as "it is sociologically very significant that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them." He offers two examples: an alliance against a common enemy, and the invisible church, which joins all believers through their equal relation to the one God. Then comes the postponement: "The group-forming, mediating function of a third element will be discussed in a later context."[^6]

He sets it aside because of distance. The third stands so far from the other two that no properly sociological interaction takes in all three alike. What remains are pairs: either the relation among those who join, or the relation between them as a unit and the outside center of interest. God and two believers do not constitute one three-way field, and neither do two allies and a far enemy. The chapter requires three elements close enough to form a group, so the distant case falls outside its concern. I call what he set aside the deferred third: a third so far off that it never comes between an identified pair. Simmel's examples unify — an enemy, a God — and Austin does not. It holds the man in from the bus station and the people standing around a woman struck in the street in one city, and it introduces nobody. That distance is why the city's job belongs under the postponement.

When the theme returns some two hundred pages later, in the chapter on conflict, the distance that had been his ground for deferring becomes a positive thesis: a certain distance between the elements to be united and the point that unites them turns out to be an especially favorable constellation for the bond. Both of the original examples come back on that page.[^7] The third returns as a condition of a bond, not as an element of a triad. Austin's sidewalk is that kind of distance. It holds people who have never been introduced, and it does not become a third party standing between them.

The question of how holding becomes membership returns us to Josiah Royce. In *The Problem of Christianity* he asks what makes a community out of people who need not have met, and he answers with a definition. A community of memory takes shape when each of its members "accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts," and a community of expectation by the same relation to shared future events.[^8] His illustration is two Maori, each of whom says that he came over in the canoe Tai-Nui. They form a community on that shared past even though, as Royce adds, "Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies."[^9] He permits aloofness, enmity, and the absence of any meeting. What he does not permit is a community of people who have not each, individually, claimed the same past. Royce lists three conditions, and the scholarship reads them as cumulative: the self's power to extend itself ideally in time; a plurality of distinct selves capable of social communication and in general engaged in it; and at least one identical event included in the ideally extended lives of all.[^10] Without the second condition there is no community at all. Acceptance is written into the definition itself. A day of unclaimed strangers cannot meet it. Sharing a city is not sharing a past. The film will ask after Gary and not produce him for that reason.

Simmel gives the distance: a third that does not enter the pair. Royce gives the membership that distance refuses — no yes, no community. Charles Reich gives the form the distance takes when someone still has to administer it, a grant spoken at a door. One afternoon makes those three refusals visible: nobody introduced, nobody claimed, nobody given a reason, and somebody still there to be asked. The film moves from a taxi and a body to a door to a missing friend.

## 'You seen Gary around?' – The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

Linklater himself, credited as Should have stayed at bus station, rides in from an overnight coach and talks at a silent driver. His subject is a dream in which nothing happened, and then a theory that every unchosen path continues somewhere as its own reality. At the station he had almost skipped the cab, and in that other life a woman picks him up and he moves in with her. The driver never answers and he never seems to notice. He gets out on "Man, shit. I should've stayed at the bus station."[^11]

Location notes put the drop on 24th Street, between San Antonio and Nueces, near the Castilian, and say the passenger witnesses a hit-and-run there.[^12] I have not confirmed the witnessing from the disc. What the stretch still shows, even if he walks into the next street, is that Austin holds both beats and introduces nobody. After the drop a woman lies struck. A jogger jogs in place and barks at no one to touch her. Somebody goes for help. A man who has pulled over asks whether he is needed, hears that he is not, and presses his card on the jogger anyway — "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it" — before driving off ahead of the police. The camera then leaves the body and follows the driver responsible into a house, where officers take him away for running over his mother.

In the taxi the man had said there is always someone getting run over. The next stretch of the film is a son who has run over his mother. The rhyme is in the lines. The city holds both without introducing them. I am using selection here for the cut's choosing of the next person, a job the city does not do. The first five minutes already show the split.

Down at the Blue Bayou on South Congress a man credited as S-T-E-V-E with a van has spent the afternoon telling a friend about the night ahead: his roommate's band, his van, a guest list he had made the roommate write. At the curb he claims the club keeps a spot for him because he is "tight" with them. The doorman charges him three dollars. He spells his way onto a list he believes he owns, "S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four," and is refused on it. The door answers, "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you," and Steve supplies the gloss: "Fuck. I'm not on the list."

Steve is refused, and the only reason the door will give is that it does not see him on its list. Wealth of the modern kind, Charles Reich argued in 1964, is increasingly held not as property but as largess, a grant, whose granting, regulation and revocation proceed "by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient."[^13] The Blue Bayou's door does that work in miniature. It sets the price, keeps the record, decides the case in its own forum, and will not say why the list Steve believes he owns does not have him. Reich also demanded that denial on undisclosed grounds "should no longer be tolerated."[^14] The door never reaches that sentence. The demand stays a hope appended to the description. The grant is still spoken to a person. Steve can answer the door, even if the door will not answer him.

Later, at another door, a man offers a hand stamp — "I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in" — and copies it by licking wrist to wrist, over an objection ("No, man. I don't want him licking my arm") that does not stop anyone, and the two of them walk in free. They can copy the stamp because a stamp is property, showable and transferable between strangers. The guest list was a grant, and only the door's own record could confirm it. Later at closing someone says the rule aloud and empties the room with it — "It's past 2:00. Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere. You can't be here. And I wanna go home." Even that sentence is addressed to people who could in principle argue back. The platforms that inherit this arrangement keep the charge, the list, the refusal, and the withheld reason. They drop both the person who can state the rule and the stamp that strangers could copy among themselves.

Gary is missing, and nobody in the room can answer for him. In a café later identified as Captain Quackenbush's on the Drag, a passerby breaks in to ask after a man who is not there — "You seen Gary around?" — and nobody has seen him for months, and the asker leaves without an answer. Outside, a man who has overheard latches onto him with suppressed moon transmissions, Mars since '62, a rain-forest drug that produces "perfect employees," and the suggestion that Gary may already have been recruited. The asker heads off toward a friend's place, and the talker waits behind to be sure they were not followed before stopping a second stranger with "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing" and then waving him along — "You're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense." But Gary never appears. The sidewalk had held both men within reach of one another. The cut put their meeting on screen. No one introduced them.

They are contemporaries, and they are in communication, so Royce's second condition holds on the street in a way it does not hold for most of the people the film shows us. The third condition fails, and the acceptance built into it. Neither man takes Gary's absence into his own life as the same past event. One is looking for a friend. The other converts the absence into evidence for a conspiracy. The day they share remains a day neither of them claims. On Royce's own terms these people are not members of anything. Austin holds them without claiming any of them.

Through the rest of the day the holding keeps working, and two kinds of join take turns. Early on the cut places the taxi and then the body in one sequence for us. A missing-friend question pulls a conspiracy into the frame. A friend breaking off a voting rant to say that Paul has vanished follows it with "Why don't you come look? It's really weird." By the middle of the day the people on screen close meetings themselves, with things they can hand across and sentences they say out loud: a lift ("How about a lift back into town?"), an address ("The address, man. Take me there"), Steve's van ("Do I have a car? I have a van"), the stamp already seen at a door, a PixelVision camera passed around at a party for "a project," handed over with its terms stated aloud — shoot whatever you want, pass it on, but I want it back. Those are people using the city's reach. The cut still chooses which of those uses we watch. The doorman, the same afternoon, administers a grant — a list, a refusal, a person at the door — which is not the camera's job and not the city's holding either. By dawn the cut is joining scenes again, with "Wake up, America" from a passing car, a taped weapons rant, and finally a camera thrown from the top of Mount Bonnell. When the cut is doing that joining there is nothing handed over and nobody addressed.

The same day shows labor without a contract: kids hawking Cokes, a man selling T-shirts, and a hitchhiker coming back from his stepfather's funeral who takes a lift into town, says the man "was a serious fuck-up," and asks for a cigarette. He has nothing to say about work at all. Outside Les Amis a different man, credited as Hitchhiker awaiting "true call," tells a camera crew he will take a job only "when I hear the true call," and when they ask what that is he says he will know it when he hears it. The city holds him within reach and hires him for nothing. He withholds the specification, and he offers that withholding to people standing there to be asked. Stark and Pais call the later version co-optation without delegated authority. On the sidewalk in 1990 there is always somebody standing there to be asked.

Amazon's Prime Video licensing page says that, unless otherwise indicated, removed titles "may not be resubmitted or appealed," that selection sits at the company's sole discretion, and that it "cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not selected."[^15] Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus, surveying 810 California drivers, found two-thirds reporting deactivation and thirty percent of those given no reason at all.[^16] Drivers contribute without being hired, as the people at that party did, and the counterparty that removes them has removed anyone who could be argued with. The Video Backpacker explains that a video image is more powerful than an actual event because he once watched a stabbing and now "can't press rewind." A driver cannot rewind an allocation once the queue has moved on. Steve's list failed in public and the group renegotiated on the sidewalk. An app assigns a queue position whether or not the driver assents to it, and the inclusion waits for nobody's yes.

Because Royce makes acceptance constitutive, the app's assignment is not membership. Calling it one would spend his term twice and in opposite senses. Salomé Viljoen describes the mechanism: a horizontal, population-based data relation includes a person in a grouping through no act of his own, on the basis of what he has in common with others rather than anything he has claimed.[^17] Enrollment of that kind is the platform's answer to Royce's question, and the answer is that it has stopped asking. Rideshare Drivers United exists because drivers organized to compel an answer from a third that will not give one.

## Reach, Selection, and the Refusal to Resolve

The city and the cut do not swap jobs. People use the city's reach: a lift, an address, a stamp. The doorman administers a grant inside that arrangement. That is not the cut taking the city's job.

In 1981 Scott Feld asked how people come to know one another without a matchmaker. His answer was the focus: a social or physical entity around which joint activities are organized (a workplace, a voluntary organization, a hangout, a family), such that people who share the activity "will tend to become interpersonally tied and form a cluster."[^18] Austin looks at first like a focus of that kind, a place around which a day of walking and talking is organized. But the people on screen share sidewalks and form no cluster. The shared activity that produces Feld's ties is missing. What remains is the holding I have been calling reach. Sarah Barns describes digital platforms as infrastructures of urban exchange with "basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects," the picking of who actually encounters whom, which is nearer to selection, and to what the camera does when it chooses the next person.[^19] The city supplies the one. The cut supplies the other. The door, the same afternoon, still has to say the grant out loud.

Michael Warner's account of a public constitutes strangers as a collectivity precisely through their common relation to something outside them, with no interaction among them required and membership arriving by mere attention.[^20] Warner's public is addressed. It does not have someone choosing which two of its members are put in front of each other next. That choosing is the cut's job, and I have been calling it selection. Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga already split match quality from match probability in their account of what cities do for the people in them, a precedent for treating reach and matching as separable.[^21] They split the same two functions in economic form. The film shows them performed by visibly different agents.

The film will not close the questions the city is not built to answer. To produce Gary, or to let the man at Les Amis specify the true call, or to bring the man from the bus station back at dusk, would be to supply from outside precisely the introductions the arrangement does not make. The film's recurring question — asked of Gary, and of Paul, and implicitly of everyone who leaves the frame and does not return — stays open. A door still has to say the rule out loud. A camera still has to choose the next person without telling anyone why.

## Conclusion

The taxi and the struck woman share a city. Steve shares a sidewalk with a doorman who can charge him, check a list, turn him away, and give no further reason. Gary is asked after and never produced. Linklater leaves those questions hanging so that the two jobs stay in view. Simmel describes the distance. Royce describes the membership test. Reich describes the grant. Stark and Pais describe the enrollment that later withdrew anyone who could be asked.

The platform keeps the charge, the list, the refusal, and the withheld reason, and withdraws the person who had to say them out loud. Now that no one has to answer, we can still hear the people on screen ask, of a city that will not tell them, whether anyone has seen Gary around.
