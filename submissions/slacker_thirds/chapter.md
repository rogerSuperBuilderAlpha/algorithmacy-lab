# "You seen Gary around?": The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

*Slacker* (Richard Linklater, 1991) holds roughly one hundred characters, most of them unacquainted, in one legible day, and it does so with "no plot, no major characters, no suspense: just fleeting glimpses of bohemia in its twilight phase."[^1] I treat the film as a limit case of the multi-character form and argue that a deferred third, a party that stands outside every pair it holds together and never steps between them, does the work. The arrangement divides into two jobs and a grant, permission someone gives and can withhold. Austin holds strangers in reach and introduces nobody; the camera selects which stranger the viewer sees next; at the film's doors a person still pronounces the grant: priced, recorded, refused, and left unexplained, by someone the refused can still answer. That is the arrangement.

The film opens with the arrangement already at work. A man played by Linklater himself, credited as "Should have stayed at bus station," rides in from an overnight coach and talks at a taxi driver who never once answers him.[^2] His monologue proposes that every road not taken continues somewhere as its own reality, and it wanders through his dreams, where, he says, there is always someone getting run over. He gets out of the cab on the line from which his credit takes its name. Moments later a woman lies struck in the street, a jogger barks at bystanders not to touch her, and a motorist who has pulled over presses his business card on a stranger, "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it," before driving off ahead of the police.[^3] The camera abandons the victim and follows the driver responsible into a house, where officers take him away for running over his mother. Within its first five minutes, the passenger's dream has leaked into the street. The viewer soon learns to anticipate such hand-offs: a character walks out of one scene and into the next, and the film goes with whichever body the camera prefers.

The scholarship on *Slacker* has answered the coordination question in three ways. The oldest answer is the relay: a "fluid camera and a kind of 'baton-passing' among the characters," in the production history's phrase, each walker handing the film to the next.[^4] The relay describes the surface accurately, and it stops at the surface, because a baton names the hand-off and says nothing about who holds the film between hands or why any particular stranger receives it. A second answer locates a property that all the scenes share. The characters "unite by unanimously doing nothing," so the film coheres as a common ethic of refusal.[^5] Alternatively, "conversation, and ideas themselves, become the determining force of the narrative," so the film coheres as one continuous exchange of talk.[^6] These observations hold for the film's tone, which is consistent. Shared tone and recurring talk help the scenes cohere, but they do not explain why the film follows one available stranger rather than another, and the monologues do not travel. Each dies with its speaker, unanswered by the scene that follows. The opening dream reaches the street only because the camera carries it there; what does travel, later in the day, is a name.

A third answer attributes the continuity to drift. The camera "drifts along the street space, from character to character and from place to place," revealing a generation in the act of performing itself;[^7] the characters' walking is philosophy in practice, flânerie, the stroller who chooses his own turns, raised to a method;[^8] the scenes flow into one another until they create "the sense of a seamless urban tissue."[^9] The drift readings describe the camera's path accurately. What they mistake, however, is the grammatical person of the figures they borrow. Flânerie and the dérive, the Situationists' aimless drift through a city, are first-person figures, in which the walker chooses where to turn. Flânerie is something *I* do; the film's hand-offs are done *to* the walkers. In *Slacker* the walkers choose nothing about what we see. At every hand-off the film picks a single passerby from several available, and it never goes back. Whatever performs that choice is not strolling, and the drift concepts have no name for it.

The formal literature names the structure and stops there. In Charles Ramírez Berg's taxonomy of alternative plots, *Slacker* defines the daisy chain, in which the story jumps from one protagonist to the next, never to revisit any of them.[^10] The philosophical literature goes further and makes the namelessness a virtue. On Sara Bizarro's Strawsonian reading, the film's people are Episodics, people who do not experience their lives as a story: "We get only episodic moments of these characters' lives, without any larger meaning to the story, except for how these characters choose to spend their time," and their apathy is "a refusal to participate in traditional narrativity."[^11] Bizarro is right about the characters. Her formal extension, that the refusal itself organizes the film, is where the reading fails. John Marlovits draws the consequence: "the frustration that viewers experience in the film's refusal to provide a coherent plot *is the point*."[^12] At the level of plot the refusal is total. As an account of the film's construction it proves too much, because a genuine refusal of coordination would play as shuffle, and *Slacker* is legible at the hand-off: the character who walks away from one scene walks into the next, and a viewer who cannot say what the film is about can still say where the film is. The characters can afford to be Episodic because something else is doing the joining. Existing accounts explain *Slacker*'s discontinuity but not its rule of continuation.

Network narrative theories, accounts of films whose many characters are linked without a single hero, run their principal accounts through convergence: plotlines that intertwine, consequences that propagate across strands, strangers whose paths finally cross.[^13] *Slacker* appears on those filmographies and supplies almost none of this. After the opening accident nothing propagates, and no line re-crosses. The two theories that come nearest do without convergence. For Margrit Tröhler, the mosaic film holds together the way a mosaic does, by placement: the montage starts an association, the spectator completes it, and chance itself, Tyche, the Greek goddess of chance, presides. Seung-hoon Jeong goes further and names the selector outright: a camera that shifts from one person to another without cutting is "the real agent of networking on the ground," and he hears its hesitation about whom to follow between characters whose paths cross.[^14] Jeong's camera makes links. The one at issue here picks one passerby from several and never returns. All of these joining agents are textual, and the question of a worldly arrangement, of what in the depicted city keeps the next stranger available for the camera to find, does not arise; *Slacker* surfaces in both accounts once, in passing, unanalyzed. The newest scholarship on the film reads its Austin as a myth a generation uses to mourn a city that has since become something else; the reading is right about the afterlife, and the arrangement described here is what the myth mythologizes.[^15] The film's coordination is real and divided. Austin holds, in the world the story shows; the camera selects, in the telling; at the doors a person still pronounces the grant. I call the holder the deferred third. Platforms later kept both jobs and the grant and withdrew the person. The film asks after a man named Gary and declines to produce him, the one introduction the arrangement never makes. The doorman's unexplained list and the platform's unexplained deactivation have the same four acts, thirty-five years apart; only the later one has nobody left to pronounce them. In other words: the city keeps strangers within reach, the camera picks which one we follow, and a person at the door still says yes or no to your face. Platforms do all three and take the person away.

## Simmel's postponed third

The sociology of the third begins with Georg Simmel, who in 1908 was the first to ask what a third party does to a relation between two. His triad chapter, the study of groups of three, is best known for the thirds who stand between a pair. The non-partisan and the mediator repair a relation between two identified parties; the *tertius gaudens*, the laughing third, profits from standing between them; *divide et impera*, divide and rule, sets the two against one another in order to rule both.[^16] Each of these figures presupposes an acquaintance: the third works on a pair it already knows. The taxi passenger and the woman in the street have no such third and no such acquaintance; what they share is a city.

On the chapter's opening page Simmel registers a third of another kind:

> It is sociologically very significant that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them. This applies as much to the alliance between states for the purpose of defense against a common enemy as to the "invisible church" which unifies all faithful in their equal relation to the one God. The group-forming, mediating function of a third element will be discussed in a later context.[^17]

Simmel does not redeem the promise in the triad chapter,[^18] and when he returns to it, two hundred pages later, the third has changed office.

Simmel's reason for deferring is distance. The third stands so far from the other two that no properly sociological interaction takes in all three alike; what remain are configurations of two, the relation among those who join, and the relation between them as a unit and the outside center of interest.[^19] In plainer terms, God and two believers never sit at one table, and neither do two allies and a far enemy. There are only ever pairs. I call the figure Simmel set aside the deferred third — a third so distant that it never comes between an identified pair. Whereas an enemy unifies those who league against it, and a God unifies those who worship, Austin holds the man from the bus station and the people around the struck woman in one city, all day, and introduces none of them.

When the theme returns, in the chapter on conflict, the distance that had been grounds for deferral has become a positive thesis: a certain distance between the elements to be united and the point that unites them is an especially favorable condition of the bond, and the same two figures return in altered form, a league held together by a standing enemy threat, a distant God gathering the most heterogeneous peoples.[^20]

The deferred third is never a member of a triad; on Simmel's own account only pairs of relations obtain around it. When I call Austin, a door, or a platform a third, I mean the far point over against the people it holds in one field. Simmel's far point has two properties: it stands outside every pair it holds, and the held take it up. The allies league against the enemy; the believers worship the God. Austin has the first property and not the second. People only stand in a city. In other words, God unites believers because each believer looks to God; nobody in *Slacker* looks to Austin. Whether holding without taking-up can amount to membership of anything is the question the next section's test decides.

## Royce's test for membership

Simmel does not decide whether such holding produces community. Josiah Royce, Harvard's idealist philosopher, writing in 1913 about how a church holds together, supplies a demanding criterion for one form of it. A community of memory takes shape when each of its members "accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts," and a community of expectation stands in the same relation to shared future events.[^21] His illustration is two Maori, each of whom says that he came over in the canoe Tai-Nui. They form a community on that shared past even though, as Royce adds, "Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies."[^22]

The definition is permissive at every point where one might expect strictness. It tolerates aloofness, enmity, distance, and the absence of any meeting; two believers with incompatible theologies still share the one God. It is strict at one point only: acceptance. Royce writes it into the definition itself and sets three conditions, which the scholarship reads as cumulative and which come to this: each self can stretch its life in imagination to take in a past it never lived; the selves can talk to one another; and every one of them claims at least one identical event as its own.[^23] Royce does not require every pair to communicate directly, but he does require the members to belong to a communicative social relation. Royce allows the Tai-Nui men to remain aloof or hostile while still claiming the same voyage as part of their lives. And without each person's act of claiming there is nothing to have in common. In other words, a community exists when each member says of the same event, *that happened to me*. Simmel's examples pass on that count: each ally claims the standing threat as his own future, each believer the same sacred history as his own past. Austin's residents claim no common event. A day of unclaimed strangers cannot pass the test. Sharing a city is not sharing a past. The film runs the test once on two men who talk and fail it; the last section runs it again on drivers who never meet and pass.

## The two jobs, with someone still there to be asked

A club door, a minute long, is the film's clearest statement of what a grant is. A man credited as S-T-E-V-E with a van has been telling friends about the evening ahead: his roommate's band is playing, he has the van, and he made the roommate put him on the guest list.[^24] Parking the van, he brags that they keep his spot and that he is in "tight" there. At the door he quotes the guest list and the door quotes a price back, three dollars, which is what entry costs anyone the list does not cover. He spells his name at it, "S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four," and the door answers, "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you," and Steve supplies the gloss himself: "Fuck. I'm not on the list." The list is real, and the club keeps it; Steve's error concerns what being on it would have been worth. The legal scholar Charles Reich argued in 1964 that modern wealth increasingly takes the form of largess — a grant whose granting, regulation and revocation proceed "by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient."[^25] Largess, in plain terms, is a benefit someone else grants and can take back, as opposed to a thing you own. Reich wrote about government largess, and by 1990 he had extended the diagnosis to large organizations of any kind; the door is the small private instance, and the platform is the scale at which that extension applies.[^26] The door reproduces the procedural asymmetry Reich associated with largess: it sets the price, keeps the record, decides the case in its own forum, and declines to say why the list does not hold Steve's name. Steve's guest-list claim does not carry the legal or economic weight of the benefits Reich analyzed. Reich also demanded that denial on undisclosed grounds "should no longer be tolerated," and six years later *Goldberg v. Kelly*, the 1970 decision that gave welfare recipients a hearing before their benefits stop, attached a hearing, an impartial decider, and stated reasons to qualifying public entitlements.[^27] Private club admission carries none of the three. In other words, the door can refuse you without explaining.

The scene preserves the grant's address, and the address buys Steve nothing. The door prices him, checks him, and refuses him, and arguing moves it not an inch. A person at a door can be as arbitrary as any system, on grounds no better disclosed, as the discriminatory history of club doors attests. Addressability, whether you can speak to the person who decides, and accountability, whether that person has to justify the decision, are distinct properties, and the film keeps only the first in view. What the door has and the platform will lack is co-presence — the person who refuses you is the person who hears your reply, at the moment of refusal. A person nonetheless pronounces the grant to Steve's face, and he can talk back even though the door owes him nothing.

Later, at another door, a man offers a hand stamp, "I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in or something," and copies it by licking wrist to wrist, over an objection ("No, man. I don't want him licking my arm") that stops nobody, and the group walks in free. The pair of doors is as close as the film comes to a controlled comparison. The stamp functions as a visible, transferable credential; the guest list remains a record-dependent permission controlled by the club. At closing time someone says the rule aloud and empties the room with it: "It's past 2:00. Everybody out."

Gary is missing, and nobody can answer for him. In a café on the Drag a man asks after a friend, "You seen Gary around?," and gets the answer he has evidently gotten before: "I told you. No one's seen him for months." He leaves with an answer and without Gary. Outside, a stranger who has overheard latches onto him with suppressed moon transmissions, Mars since '62, a rain-forest drug that produces "perfect employees," and the suggestion that Gary may already have been recruited. The asker heads off toward a friend's place; the talker waits behind to be sure they were not followed, stops a second stranger with "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing," and waves him along: "I mean, you're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense." Gary never appears. The pairing on the sidewalk is the one that matters: the asker and the talker had never met, the city held them within reach of one another, the camera carried their meeting in one unbroken drift from the café into the street, and nobody introduced them. Gary's name, overheard, is what set them talking, and Austin is what put the name in earshot.

The two on the sidewalk do more than Royce asks; they actually converse, and community fails anyway. The failure lies in acceptance, on both sides of the conversation. Royce's test tolerates divergence; the Tai-Nui men may fight over what the voyage meant and remain one community, because each has claimed the crossing as his own. The scene does not show either man incorporating Gary's absence into a shared history. The asker is still seeking the fact of the absence, holding it at arm's length as a question, and the talker never accepts the absence at all, substituting for it an event of his own manufacture, a recruitment nobody witnessed. No identical event has entered both lives, and so, on Royce's terms, there is no community, however fluent the conversation. Austin holds them without claiming any of them.

Through the rest of the day the holding keeps working, and two kinds of join take turns. Early on, the camera does the joining, placing the taxi and then the body in one sequence, and pulling a conspiracy into the frame behind a missing-friend question; a friend's disappearance ("Why don't you come look?") pulls the next walker along. As the day wears on, the people on screen close meetings themselves, with things they can hand across and sentences they say out loud: a lift ("How about a lift back into town?"), an address ("The address, man. Take me there"), Steve's van ("Do I have a car? I have a van"), the hand stamp, and a PixelVision camera passed around at a party for "a project," handed over with its terms stated aloud, shoot whatever you want, pass it on, but I want it back. Characters select one another constantly. The talker singling out his listener is a choice inside the story. The camera ratifies it by staying. What no character chooses is which of those choices the film follows next. Selection in the story and selection of the story are different jobs, and only the second one is the camera's. The doorman administers a grant, which belongs to neither job. By dawn the camera is joining scenes on its own again, and in those joins nothing changes hands and no character singles anyone out.

The city's reach comes with no single authority that allocates, evaluates, and sanctions the informal exchanges shown on the sidewalk. The same day shows an informal economy running on that reach: kids hawking Cokes, a man selling T-shirts, a woman offering what she swears is a Madonna Pap smear. Outside Les Amis a man credited as Hitchhiker awaiting "true call" tells a camera crew he will take a job only "when I hear the true call," and when they ask what that is he says he will know it when he hears it. The city holds him in reach of whoever might call and asks nothing of him. The PixelVision camera at the party is a limited formal analogue of what came next: contribution handed along under conditions somebody states out loud. When David Stark and Ivana Pais describe platforms co-opting "the behavior of providers and users, enrolling them in the practices of algorithmic management without managerial authority having been delegated to them," they describe the same arrangement with people gathered into the software's practices and the person who states the conditions removed.[^28] Algorithmic management, here, is the platform's software, not a human supervisor, assigning work, rating it, and removing people. On the sidewalk in 1990 somebody is standing there to be asked. The next section shows the same four acts with that position empty.

## The same jobs without an answerer

Platforms have entered Simmel's triad as thirds of the between, that is, brokers and mediators of the public sphere. Scholars of algorithmic management see the platform's outside position and reach for other words, so they never ask Simmel's question: whether the held are members of anything.[^29] The postponed third, the one that stands outside without mediating, is still where he left it.[^30]

The four acts at Steve's door recur, act for act, in the documents of a platform he could not have named. What the film distributes across a city, a camera, and a doorman, the platform performs alone. Uber sets the fare and takes its fee. The ratings ledger is the list: drivers "who don't meet the minimum average ratings for their city may lose access to all or part of the Uber Marketplace Platform." The refusal arrives as deactivation, at the company's "sole discretion." The deactivation page carries the withheld reason as policy, promising "the reasons behind our decision, except where doing so poses a risk to other users."[^31] A survey of a self-selected sample of 810 California drivers by Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus found two thirds reporting suspension or deactivation, and thirty percent of those given no reason at all.[^32] Linklater later glossed the viewing-side of the same structure as channel surfing, "a really primitive version of what now would be exponentially more complex."[^33]

What the platform withdrew is more exact than an address. Uber maintains a Review Center where a deactivated driver can lodge an appeal; the support channel that preceded it ran on template responses, drivers writing "escalate to manager" in the hope of reaching a person, and suspension notices whose entire explanation read that the account had violated the terms and conditions.[^34] The address exists. Co-presence does not. You can still write to Uber. The person who reads it did not decide, and cannot undo it. Steve's list failed in public, face to face on a sidewalk; a deactivation notice arrives after the fact, and the queue has already moved on. A driver cannot recover the work lost while a deactivation stands. The law has begun to require a human decider at the point of termination: the European Union now requires statements of reasons for platform terminations, and its platform-work directive orders that any decision to suspend or terminate the account of anyone performing platform work "shall be taken by a human being."[^35] Each remedy presupposes that the decider's position is empty.

Because Royce makes acceptance the condition of community, the platform does not produce one. Two enrollments are in play. Stark and Pais's is enrollment in a practice: you start doing what the software rewards. Salomé Viljoen's is enrollment in a population: the software groups you with people you have never met, by a horizontal, population-based data relation that places a person in a grouping through no act of his own, on the basis of what he shares with others he has never met.[^36] Calling either one membership would spend Royce's term twice and in opposite senses. The city-wide "minimum average ratings" threshold is that relation in the documents already quoted: a driver is grouped with every other driver in the city, and cut against their average, without claiming the grouping as his own. The driver's click on an agreement is a yes to the company, not to the grouping. In other words, the platform can enroll you without your ever saying of the group, *that is mine*. Rideshare Drivers United shows drivers organizing collectively to demand information and review that individuals could not compel alone. The drivers pass Royce's test where the two men on the Drag failed it: each has claimed the same event, a deactivation without reason, as his own, and the community they form is the one the platform never offered. The film shows what it took to live with a grant while a person still pronounced it. The next section keeps the three functions distinct so the comparison does not collapse them.

## Keeping the jobs apart

Austin is not a focus in Scott Feld's sense. A focus is a social or physical entity around which joint activities are organized, such that people who share the activity "will tend to become interpersonally tied and form a cluster."[^37] Feld explains why participation in a shared focus makes ties likelier over time. His theory does not specify which available pair will meet on a particular occasion. Every hand-off in this film is one pair, picked, and the café and the club sit inside the holding I have been calling reach. Reach, here, is the public realm, the part of a city where the people around you are strangers or known only by type.[^38] Digital platforms, in Sarah Barns's phrase, add "basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects" to that kind of urban reach, the picking of who actually encounters whom, and picking is the camera's job in this film.[^39] Urban economists, writing about agglomeration, the crowding of many people into one place, ask two things of a city: how likely you are to meet anyone, and how good the match is.[^40] Austin supplies the first. The camera supplies something agglomeration economics does not model, the picking of which meeting the record keeps.

The film declines to resolve the questions the city is not built to answer. To produce Gary, to let the man at Les Amis specify the true call, to close Paul's disappearance, or to bring the man from the bus station back at dusk would be to supply from outside the introductions the arrangement does not make. The door says the rule aloud and the camera says nothing about its choice. The conclusion sets both beside a platform that does neither.

## Conclusion

What the network theories lacked was an instance: the thing that remains after you subtract convergence, consequence, and coincidence and the film still holds. This chapter has named the holder the deferred third, and set beside it a selector and a door. The taxi and the struck woman share a city that introduces nobody. Steve shares a sidewalk with a doorman who prices him, checks a list, refuses him the free way in, gives no further reason, and hears about it to his face. Someone asks after Gary and the film never produces him, because producing him would supply the one thing the arrangement never supplies.

Platforms keep the four acts and separate the decision from any person the refused can answer. Now that no one has to answer, we can still hear the people on screen ask, of a city that will not tell them, whether anyone has seen Gary around.
