# Domain sweep — Linklater and *Slacker* scholarship, and network-narrative theory

**Chapter audited:** `chapter.md` (v17, body 4,470 words, 39 endnotes), "'You seen Gary around?': The Deferred Third of *Slacker*"
**Sweep date:** 19 August 2026
**Question put to the domain:** the chapter claims the film's coordination divides into a holding done by
the city and a selecting done by the camera, and that the existing readings — relay, shared property,
drift — have no name for the second. **Has a film scholar already named the camera as the thing that
chooses?**
**Short answer:** yes, once, in an article the chapter already cites for something else.
**Verification standard:** every quotation below was read in a fetched full text and is page-mapped.
Where a source could not be reached, the sweep says so and claims nothing about it.

---

## 1. Terrain

The chapter surveys three answers to *Slacker*'s coordination question and dispatches each. The relay
(Macor's "baton-passing," quoted in Małecka) describes the hand-off and says nothing about who holds
the film between hands. The shared property — Małecka's "unite by unanimously doing nothing," Price's
conversation as determining force — cannot join any two scenes, because a property every scene
possesses joins none of them. The drift — Stone, Soldani, Boom, Ramirez — describes the camera's path
precisely but borrows first-person figures, flânerie and the *dérive*, for a film in which the walkers
choose nothing about what we see.

That survey is accurate and, on the evidence below, fairly conducted. Soldani grounds the drift in
Debord and quotes the *dérive* definition the chapter's objection targets — "one or more persons ...
let themselves be drawn by the attractions of the terrain and the encounters they find there" — which
is first-person exactly as the chapter says (Soldani 2017, 81, quoting Debord 50). The chapter is not
attacking a straw drift.

What the chapter has never done is search for a fourth answer. The three rivals it lists all come from
*Slacker* criticism. The fourth comes from network-narrative theory, which the chapter treats as a
literature that has passed the film by — and it is there, in print, since 2021.

The domain also has a wider structure worth naming. *Slacker*'s form is universally traced to Buñuel's
*The Phantom of Liberty* (1974), whose camera enters a scene with one set of characters and leaves with
another. That genealogy matters below, because it is the film through which the camera-agent claim
enters the literature.

## 3. Audit — the finding

### F1. The camera-agent is already named, on a page the chapter already cites. **Would change the argument.**

Seung-hoon Jeong, "Network Narratives in Global Cinema," *Panoptikum* 26 (2021), at **144**:

> However, it is notable that the camera autonomously shifts from one person to another the hero
> encounters, without cutting and often without his presence. The entire film indeed looks like a
> single-shot long take, which renders palpable **the camera's own agency** unlike the cross-cutting
> made under the director's full control. **This 'camera with or without a man' is the real agent of
> networking on the ground** …

> One might recall that such a **camera-agent** embodies the movement of networking in Buñuel's *The
> Phantom of Liberty* (1974). Though it does not have a single-shot look, we sometimes feel even **the
> camera's hesitation about whom to follow between different characters whose paths cross**. This
> surrealist film is a remarkable precursor of the "daisy chain plot" (Berg 2006, 24–26) in which one
> character's story leads to the next one's with no central character …

*Verified: full text of the article PDF read; both passages on printed p. 144.*

Three things make this the sharpest exposure the chapter has.

**It is in the chapter's own field, in an article the chapter already cites.** Note 10 uses Jeong as
corroboration for Berg's page range and nothing else. This is the exact shape of the panel's mandatory
M3 — Rosenblat and Stark's foreman vacancy sitting on page 3771, the page note 33 already uses — and it
is worse, because a film-studies referee is likelier to know Jeong than a film-studies referee is to
know *IJoC*.

**It attaches to the right film.** Jeong names *Slacker* as Linklater's "other network narrative film"
at **143**, one page earlier. And the camera-agent claim is introduced through *The Phantom of
Liberty*, the film every account of *Slacker*'s structure names as its model. The chapter's proposition
and Jeong's are one page and one homage apart.

**The phrasing is close.** "The camera's hesitation about whom to follow between different characters
whose paths cross" is the chapter's selecting job, described as an event at a hand-off.

### What survives, and it is more than it looks

Jeong's camera-agent is an agent of **networking** — of connecting, of making links. The chapter's
camera is an agent of **selection**: it picks one passerby from several available and never goes back.
Those are different claims, and the difference is the chapter's, not a hedge. Jeong never says the
camera declines to introduce; he has no account of what is withheld at a hand-off, no reach, no door,
no grant, and no person who could be asked. His framework in fact runs the other way: network
narratives are ones in which "nothing precedes and exists outside ever-changing networks of
relationship," which is a rival ontology to a chapter whose whole argument is that something outside
the relation does two jobs neither party does.

So the novelty holds at the joint the chapter cares about. What does not hold is the chapter's claim to
have found a gap where nobody has spoken. The drift paragraph currently ends "the drift concepts have
no name for it." That sentence is now false, and it is false against a source in the chapter's own
notes. Note 36's "no retrieved source pairs the city as reach with the cut as selection" is also
exposed — though the panel has that note flagged already, under C1, for its retired vocabulary.

**The repair is an upgrade, not a retreat, and it is the same move the panel prescribed for M3.** Cite
Jeong for the camera-agent, credit him with naming it, then draw the line the chapter can hold: his
camera connects, this one chooses and declines. A claim that survives a named rival is worth more than
a claim that had no rivals because nobody looked.

### F2. The Berg corroboration is verified. **Closes half an author task.**

Note 10 says "Jeong 2021 cites the daisy-chain section at 24–26." Confirmed: Jeong 144 reads "the
'daisy chain plot' (Berg 2006, 24–26)," verbatim. The Berg back issue itself is still unread and stays
in `AUTHOR_TASKS.md`, but the corroborating half of note 10 no longer rests on an unchecked assertion.

### F3. The door evidence the chapter needs is in a source it already cites. **Would strengthen, zero words in the body.**

Soldani 2017, in the night section, at 78–79:

> we see bars overflowing with beer, non-stop smoking, characters jumping into a van to go to a
> concert, **others trying to avoid paying for tickets by using the guest-list or copying an admission
> stamp onto their skin** and so on.

*Verified: full text of the article PDF read.*

That is peer-reviewed description of both halves of the chapter's door contrast — the guest list and
the hand stamp — from a scholar who noticed them as a pair, in an article the chapter cites only for
drift and the twenty-four-hour frame. It corroborates the film reading that the panel's M2 correction
rewrites, and it does so from outside the chapter's own viewing. One note.

### F4. The drift objection is correctly aimed. **No change needed.**

Soldani 81 quotes Debord: "In a dérive one or more persons during a certain period drop their usual
motives for movement and action … and let themselves be drawn by the attractions of the terrain and the
encounters they find there" (Debord 50). First-person, walker-chooses, exactly as the chapter's
objection says. The chapter's grammatical-person argument is verified against the source it targets —
which is worth knowing, because it is the argument that carries the whole rejection of rival three.

## 4. Missing works, ranked

### Would change the argument

1. **Jeong 2021 read for its own argument rather than its footnote.** Not a missing work — a missing
   reading of a present one. See F1. Cost: roughly +25 words in the drift paragraph, and the panel's
   word budget has 530 of headroom.

### Reviewer would expect it

2. **Johnson, David T. *Richard Linklater*. Contemporary Film Directors. Urbana: University of Illinois
   Press, 2012.** ISBN 9780252036927; DOI 10.5406/illinois/9780252036927.001.0001. *(verified:
   publisher record via Illinois Scholarship Online.)* The **second book-length study of Linklater**,
   in a major single-director series, and absent from a chapter whose Linklater apparatus is Stone
   plus two *ReFocus* chapters. Johnson's organising concern is time and narrative, so he is unlikely
   to contest the selecting claim; the exposure is bibliographic, not argumentative, and it is the
   first thing a Linklater specialist will check.

3. **Speed, Lesley. "The Possibilities of Roads Not Taken: Intellect and Utopia in the Films of Richard
   Linklater." *Journal of Popular Film & Television* 35, no. 3 (2007): 98–106.** *(verified: journal
   record.)* The title takes its premise from the cab passenger's monologue — every road not taken
   continues somewhere as its own reality — which is the chapter's own opening evidence. A chapter that
   opens on that monologue and does not cite the article named after it looks unread.

4. **Psaltidis, Nico. "American indie cinema between DIY cultures and global Hollywood: Exploring the
   paradigm of *Slacker*." SAGE, 2024. DOI 10.1177/27538702241229379.** *(verified: publisher record and
   abstract.)* The most recent *Slacker* article, and an industry-and-scene reading rather than a formal
   one, so it costs the argument nothing. It is the recency marker the film section lacks — the
   chapter's newest scholarly *Slacker* source is Boom 2022.

### Nice to have

5. **Buñuel, *The Phantom of Liberty* (1974), as an acknowledged model.** Standard in *Slacker*
   criticism and the film through which Jeong introduces the camera-agent. If the chapter cites Jeong
   for F1, naming the Buñuel line costs a clause and makes the credit legible.

## 5. Threats — what this domain would say against the chapter

1. **"You cite Jeong and missed his argument."** The single strongest available objection, and it is
   the same objection platform studies raises about Vallas and Schor. Twice in one chapter is a pattern
   a referee will name.
2. **"The camera-agent is not new and neither is the daisy chain."** True of the agent, false of the
   selection-and-refusal reading. The chapter has to say which half it claims.
3. **"Your Linklater bibliography is Stone and two *ReFocus* chapters."** Johnson 2012 and Speed 2007
   are the works whose absence shows.
4. **"Network-narrative theory has not passed the film by; you have read it selectively."** The chapter
   argues the theory ignores *Slacker*, citing Ciafone's single mention. Jeong names it twice on facing
   pages. The claim needs scoping to the *Anglophone formalist* line — Bordwell, Ciafone — where it is
   defensible.
5. **"Your best film evidence comes from a tourism page."** The hit-and-run witnessing is sourced to the
   Austin Film Commission. Soldani and the disc are the fixes.

## 6. Verdict

**The thesis survives; one paragraph does not.** The chapter's selecting job is real, its
grammatical-person objection to the drift readings is precisely aimed and verified against the sources
it targets, and nothing in this domain pairs a camera that selects with a city that holds, a door that
grants, or a platform that keeps both and withdraws the person. The three rivals the chapter lists are
fairly stated and correctly dispatched.

But the chapter claimed a silence, and there is no silence. Jeong named the camera-agent in 2021, on
the page after he named *Slacker*, in the article the chapter cites for a page range. The fix costs
about twenty-five words and makes the chapter stronger, because a distinction drawn against a named
rival is load-bearing in a way that a gap is not. That the same shape of error — prior art on a page
already cited — turned up independently in platform studies and in film studies suggests the habit is
the problem, not the field: **the chapter cites for pins and does not read for arguments.** That is the
durable lesson of round 2, and it is worth more than the finding.
