# Domain sweep: Platform Studies and Algorithmic Management

**Chapter audited:** `chapter.md` — "'You seen Gary around?': The Deferred Third of *Slacker*" (7,290 words, 39 endnotes)
**Sweep date:** 18 August 2026
**Question put to the domain:** the chapter claims that platforms keep the holding, the selecting, and the grant, and withdraw the person who can be asked. Is that withdrawn answerer already named in this literature under another name?
**Short answer:** yes, four times over, in four different vocabularies — and none of the four is in the chapter.

---

## 1. Terrain

Platform studies converged after 2016 on a triangular picture. Rosenblat and Stark showed that Uber's drivers and riders sit at two corners of an information asymmetry whose third corner is the firm, and that the firm's side of the conversation runs through outsourced customer-service representatives whom drivers call "Uber's robots."[^p1] Vallas and Schor generalized the geometry: platforms are "permissive potentates" that govern "not by expanding their control over participants but by relinquishing important dimensions of control and delegating them to the other two parties," which lets the platform "play the role of *tertius gaudens*."[^p2] Stark and Pais gave the form its verb — markets contract, hierarchies command, networks collaborate, *platforms co-opt* — and its topology, a Möbius strip on which value is created "in a social and economic space that is neither inside nor outside of the platform."[^p3] Stark and Vanden Broeck then ran the topology through five dimensions of management and landed, in the accountability column, on the sentence this chapter most needs to answer: "In algorithmic management cybernetic accountability is neither hierarchical nor heterarchical. It is distributed, deflected, and denied."[^p4]

The live debates run in three directions. The first is definitional: is the platform a third party at all? Ajunwa's *tertius bifrons* and Neuberger's mediating third say yes, it stands between; Stark's Möbius says the inside/outside distinction that "between" presupposes has dissolved. The second is empirical, and it is where the chapter's evidence lives: Rahman's "invisible cage" documents freelancers reacting to evaluation criteria they cannot learn and cannot contest; Curchod and colleagues document eBay sellers facing an implicit coalition of raters and platform; Cameron and Rahman map where resistance can still land.[^p5] The third is remedial and now statutory. Alkhatib and Bernstein argued in 2019 that street-level algorithms lack the bureaucrat's capacity to hear a novel case explained, and prescribed recourse; five years later the EU wrote that prescription into law, requiring a "contact person designated by the digital labour platform to discuss and to clarify the facts, circumstances and reasons having led to the decision," with "the competence, training and authority necessary."[^p6]

The chapter sits inside this literature without quite declaring itself. It uses the domain's primary sources well — Stark and Pais, Rosenblat and Stark, Uber's own pages, Rideshare Drivers United — and its four-act reading of the door (price, record, decision, withheld reason) is a genuinely new descriptive instrument. What it does not do is meet the domain's own name for its punchline. The oldest such name is twenty-four years old and comes from public administration rather than platform studies: Bovens and Zouridis, writing in 2002, opened with the finding that "in some realms, the street-level bureaucrats have vanished," and traced the passage from street-level to *screen-level* to *system-level* bureaucracy, where system analysts and software designers, not counter clerks, are the key actors.[^p7] That is the chapter's thesis, in a different building, before Uber existed.

## 3. Audit

### Body claims

**§Intro, ¶ beginning "Theories of the network narrative" (final sentence block): "The platforms that later assembled a comparable arrangement kept both jobs and the grant intact and withdrew the person."**
*Verdict: contested-by-domain.* Two of the field's most-cited recent statements deny the premise that the platform occupies the same outside position the film's city does. Stark and Pais: platforms create value "in a social and economic space that is neither inside nor outside of the platform" (48); Stark and Vanden Broeck close on "a Möbius-like topology with no inside or outside." A domain reader will grant the withdrawal and refuse the "kept intact." *Fix:* one clause conceding that the platform's geometry is not the city's — the platform does not stand outside the encounter, it constitutes it — and then making the argument that the *grant* nonetheless survives the topological change. That concession costs the chapter nothing and buys it the domain's trust.

**§Simmel's postponed third, final sentences: "Platforms have entered Simmel's triad before, as brokers standing between the parties and as mediating thirds of the public sphere, and the recent literature on algorithmic management describes the platform's outside position without Simmel's vocabulary."**
*Verdict: overclaimed on "outside position."* The first two clauses are sound. The third is not: Stark and Vanden Broeck describe not an outside position but the collapse of the inside/outside distinction, and they attribute the triangular picture explicitly to Vallas and Schor, who reach it through Simmel. So the "without Simmel" is one citation link away from Simmel. *Fix:* "…describes the platform's apex position in a triangular geometry, and does so without invoking Simmel directly, having borrowed the triangle from an account that does."

**§Simmel, note 21 tail: "The third of the postponed passage, the one that stands outside without mediating, has remained where Simmel left it."**
*Verdict: sound, within this domain.* I searched for a platform-studies or algorithmic-management use of Simmel's *distant unifier* — the enemy, the invisible church, the postponed §103 passage — and found none. Every platform invocation of Simmel I retrieved is a between-third: *tertius gaudens* (Vallas and Schor 282; Stark and Pais 54), *tertius bifrons* (Ajunwa), the mediating third (Neuberger). The negative claim survives the adversarial pass.

**Note 20: "David Stark and Pieter Vanden Broeck, 'Principles of Algorithmic Management' … on the platform's apex position, without Simmel."**
*Verdict: sound but badly underclaimed.* The word "apex" is theirs — "a triangular structure (Vallas & Schor 2020) with the platform operator at the apex and producers and users at the other corners" — and Simmel appears nowhere in the article. But this is the single article in the chapter's bibliography that already names the chapter's punchline, and the note spends it on a geometry point. *Fix:* add to the note, or better to the body of §"The same jobs without an answerer": "Stark and Vanden Broeck put the same finding in the register of accountability: in algorithmic management it is 'distributed, deflected, and denied.'"

**§The two jobs, ¶ ending on Stark and Pais: the co-optation quotation at 49, and note 29's second quotation at 48.**
*Verdict: sound.* Both quotations and both page numbers are exact against the published PDF. This is the chapter's best-verified platform citation.

**§The same jobs without an answerer, ¶1: the four acts recur "act for act" in Uber's documents — fare, ratings ledger, deactivation at "sole discretion," withheld reason as policy.**
*Verdict: partly misattributed.* Three of four check out. (i) The minimum-ratings sentence is verbatim from the General Community Guidelines. (ii) "Sole discretion" is verbatim but governs a different act than the chapter assigns it: the Guidelines' sentence is "Uber may, at its sole discretion, put a hold on your account or turn your account inactive until our review is complete" — a *suspension pending review*, not the refusal itself. (iii) The withheld-reason quotation does not match its source. The chapter has Uber promising to communicate "the reasons for the decision, except when doing so poses a risk to other users"; the page reads "specific and transparent about the reasons behind our decision, except where doing so poses a risk to other users." *Fix:* quote the page ("specific and transparent about the reasons behind our decision, except where doing so poses a risk to other users") and move "sole discretion" to the clause about holds, or drop it. A single mis-transcribed quotation inside the chapter's most load-bearing evidentiary paragraph is the kind of thing a hostile reader finds first.

**§The same jobs without an answerer, ¶1: the Rideshare Drivers United figures (810 drivers; two thirds; thirty percent).**
*Verdict: sound.* Sample size, deactivation share and no-reason share all confirmed against the publishing organizations' own summaries. Note 32's acknowledgment of self-selection is exactly the right hedge and should stay. Page cites 15 and 19 remain unverified against the PDF.

**§The same jobs without an answerer, ¶2: "The address exists; the answerer of the doorman's kind, someone standing in the doorway who hears the objection as it is made, does not."**
*Verdict: underclaimed, and this is the chapter's most consequential omission.* The sentence is true and the chapter has the source for it on the same page it already cites. Rosenblat and Stark, at 3771: drivers "are not empowered to negotiate the terms of their work by communicating to a representative of higher management." The chapter cites 3771 for template responses and "escalate to manager" and leaves the sentence that states its own thesis on the page unused. *Fix:* quote it.

**§The same jobs without an answerer, ¶2: "The law has begun trying to legislate the doorman back into the doorway."**
*Verdict: sound, and underclaimed.* Article 10(5) is verbatim. Article 11(1) is stronger than note 34's summary ("the right to an explanation and a designated contact person") and reads, verbatim: "Member States shall ensure that digital labour platforms provide persons performing platform work with access to a contact person designated by the digital labour platform to discuss and to clarify the facts, circumstances and reasons having led to the decision. Digital labour platforms shall ensure that such contact persons have the competence, training and authority necessary to exercise that function." The EU legislated the doorman — competence, training, authority, standing where the decision landed. *Fix:* quote it in the body. It is the chapter's thesis restated as statute by a body that had never heard of the chapter.

**§Keeping the jobs apart, ¶2: Barns's "basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects," said to be *added* to urban reach.**
*Verdict: sound on the quotation, slightly strained on the use.* The words are exact. Barns's argument, though, is about the capture and enclosure of urban data, not about a matching function layered on a prior condition of reach. The chapter borrows a phrase from an argument going elsewhere. Defensible; worth a hedge ("in a phrase from a different argument").

**Note 36: "Reach and selection are this chapter's terms; no retrieved source pairs the city as reach with the cut as selection."**
*Verdict: sound as written; nearly falsified in spirit.* The cut is film-specific and no platform source touches it. But the *reach/selection* split itself is old and standard one field over: market design distinguishes making a market thick from clearing it, and the two-sided-market literature distinguishes getting both sides on the platform from determining who transacts with whom. The chapter already cites Duranton and Puga for probability-versus-quality of match, which is the same distinction in urban economics. *Fix:* keep the negative claim, narrow its scope explicitly to the cut, and add one clause acknowledging that the underlying separation is familiar in market design. Otherwise a reviewer from economics reads the note as innocence rather than restraint.

### Negative claims, hunted adversarially

**Implicit claim throughout the platform section: that the withdrawal of the answerable person is this chapter's finding.**
*Verdict: contested-by-domain.* The chapter never says "nobody has noticed this," which is prudent, but the prose is written as discovery and will be read as discovery. Four prior namings, in four vocabularies:

1. **Public administration, 2002.** Bovens and Zouridis: "In some realms, the street-level bureaucrats have vanished." Street-level → screen-level → system-level bureaucracy; the citizen's counter clerk replaced by a system analyst who is not in the room.
2. **HCI, 2019.** Alkhatib and Bernstein: street-level algorithms cannot do what Lipsky's bureaucrats do, which is "recognize the uniqueness of a situation when it's explained to them." Their prescription is recourse and a fair appeals process staffed by someone with no stake in the original decision.
3. **Organization theory, 2024.** Stark and Vanden Broeck: accountability under algorithmic management is "distributed, deflected, and denied," on the ground that "one who is accountable to many, in many different registers, can be one who is accountable to none."
4. **Computer ethics, 1996.** Nissenbaum's four barriers to accountability — many hands, bugs, the computer as scapegoat, and ownership without liability via click-wrap terms — describe the erosion of the answerable person before the platform existed, and the fourth barrier is the exact legal form of the Amazon clause the chapter quotes.

None of the four has the chapter's door, its grant, or its film. That is the chapter's actual contribution and it survives all four. What does not survive is silence about them.

## 5. Threats — the strongest objections this domain would raise

1. **The geometry is wrong, and the chapter's own sources say so.** The deferred third is defined by outsideness: "the outside term of the second pair, the far point over against the people it holds in one field." Stark and Pais, whom the chapter quotes approvingly, argue that platforms operate in a space "neither inside nor outside," and Stark and Vanden Broeck close their article on "a Möbius-like topology with no inside or outside." If the platform has no outside, the chapter's central analogy between Austin and Uber fails at the joint. The chapter can survive this — the *grant* is a relation, not a position — but only if it says so.

2. **The withdrawn answerer already has four names.** Bovens and Zouridis's vanished street-level bureaucrat (2002), Nissenbaum's four barriers (1996), Alkhatib and Bernstein's discretion gap (2019), Stark and Vanden Broeck's deflected accountability (2024). A referee who knows any one of these will read the chapter's platform section as an uncredited restatement. The defense is easy and must be written: none of the four has the door, the grant, or the film, and none of them says that the arrangement predates the technology.

3. **The chapter cites the field's headline article for a footnote and misses its thesis.** Vallas and Schor's "permissive potentate" — power retained while responsibility is delegated away — is the closest existing figure for a grant nobody has to pronounce. The chapter cites the article for *tertius gaudens* and never mentions the concept the article exists to introduce. That looks like reading the abstract.

4. **One quotation does not match its source.** Uber's deactivation page reads "specific and transparent about the reasons behind our decision, except where doing so poses a risk to other users"; the chapter's quotation marks enclose a different sentence. It is a small error in the chapter's most heavily evidentiary paragraph, and it is the kind that costs credit on everything nearby.

5. **The evidentiary base for the platform claim is thin where it should be thickest.** One advocacy survey with acknowledged self-selection carries the empirical weight of the "no reason given" claim. Rahman's ASQ ethnography and Curchod and colleagues' ASQ study exist and are peer-reviewed. A reviewer will ask why they are not there.

6. **The remedy already exists in statute, which weakens the closing question.** The chapter ends on "what it takes when nothing pronounces it is a further question." Article 11(1) of the platform-work directive answers a version of it: a designated contact person with competence, training and authority. The chapter cites the article and does not quote it. Quoting it strengthens the chapter (the vacancy is real enough to have been legislated against) and requires the closing question to be re-pitched (the question is no longer *whether* the doorman can be restored but whether a statutory contact person is a doorman at all).

## 6. Verdict

The chapter is defensible in this domain, with three repairs that are all additive and none of which touch the thesis. Its primary-source work is genuinely good — every page cite I could check against Stark and Pais, Rosenblat and Stark, the Community Guidelines, the Amazon agreement, the Barns article and the platform-work directive came back exact, which is a better hit rate than most published platform-studies prose manages. Its four-act reading of the grant is a real instrument that this literature does not have. What it lacks is any sign that it knows the domain has already named its punchline four separate times, and that its central geometric metaphor is contradicted by the two articles it leans on hardest. Neither problem is fatal, because the chapter's actual contribution is not the vacancy but the door: the claim that access was a grant *before* it was automated, pronounced on a public sidewalk by someone with no obligation to explain and every obligation to be present. Nobody in platform studies has said that, because platform studies starts with the platform. Add Bovens and Zouridis and Alkhatib and Bernstein, concede the Möbius point in a clause, quote Article 11(1) and Rosenblat and Stark 3771 instead of paraphrasing around them, fix the Uber quotation, and the section stops being a film scholar's excursion into a neighboring field and becomes an argument that field would have to answer.
