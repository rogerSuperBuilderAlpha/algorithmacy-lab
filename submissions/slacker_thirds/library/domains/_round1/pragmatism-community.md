## 1. Terrain

Royce's late theory of community answers a question very close to the chapter's own. In the second volume of *The Problem of Christianity* he asks how "many different selves, all belonging to the present time," can come to possess one past, and he answers with a definition rather than a sentiment: a community exists when each member accepts the same past or future event as part of his own individual life and self (2:50–51). Two Maori who say "I came over in the canoe Tai-Nui" are one community even though "Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies" (2:46). The definition is deliberately thin at the emotional end and strict at one point only — acceptance — and Royce hedges it with three named conditions: the self's power to extend itself ideally into far-off time (2:60–61), the presence in the social world of distinct selves "capable of social communication, and, in general, engaged in communication" (2:67), and identical events inside those extended selves (2:68). He then says flatly that not every social group "meets all the conditions of our definition" (2:55). So the cumulative reading is Royce's, not merely his commentators'.

What the chapter does not use is the half of Royce that is itself a theory of the third. In the interpretation lectures Royce takes over from Peirce the "mediating idea, or what he also calls 'a third'" and insists that "Comparison must be triadic in order to be both explicit and complete" (2:172–73). He then converts the logic into a sociology: "I mean to interpret you to somebody else, to some other neighbor, who is neither yourself nor myself. Three of us, then, I seek to bring into the desired unity of interpretation" (2:209); "we three constitute a Community… a Community of Interpretation" (2:211). Royce's answer to *how do you end up next to someone you were never introduced to* is therefore: somebody wills to interpret you to a stranger, and the willing is what makes the three of you one. That interpreter is addressable by construction, and the offices are interchangeable at will (2:212).

The live debates in the domain are two. The first is textual — John E. Smith set the terms in 1950 and Frank Oppenheim's late trilogy pressed the Peirce dependence; Chris Voparil's 2021 chapter is the newest full-dress reading. The second is political and hotter: Tommy Curry's *Another white Man's Burden* (2018) argues that Royce's provincialism and loyalty are settler-imperial constructions, and *The Pluralist* ran a symposium on the charge in 2021 (Curry, Tunstall, Brunson). Any chapter that uses Royce as a neutral instrument for testing who belongs now writes into that argument whether it says so or not. The chapter sits, at present, entirely inside the pre-1913 definitional Royce and outside both debates.

## 4. Missing works

### High priority

**`royce1913interpretation`** — Josiah Royce, *The Problem of Christianity*, vol. 2, lectures XI–XII ("Perception, Conception, and Interpretation," 107–64; "The Will to Interpret," 165–222), esp. 2:172–73, 208–9, 211, 213–14. New York: Macmillan, 1913. **Verified.**
*Where:* §"Royce's test for membership," after the three conditions; one note.
*Why:* Royce's positive answer to the chapter's question is a third. Following Peirce he requires a "mediating idea, or… 'a third'" for any complete comparison (2:172–73), and he socialises it: "I mean to interpret you to somebody else, to some other neighbor, who is neither yourself nor myself. Three of us, then, I seek to bring into the desired unity of interpretation" (2:209); "we three constitute a Community… a Community of Interpretation" (2:211). Royce's third introduces, addresses, and can be answered — and the offices are interchangeable (2:212). Austin does none of it; the camera does the selecting without the addressing; the platform keeps the selecting and withdraws the addressee. The chapter's central distinction gets a pragmatist name and a 1913 date, and the Royce section stops being a single-purpose test.
*Consequence:* would-strengthen. *Priority:* high.

**`dewey1927public`** — John Dewey, *The Public and Its Problems* (New York: Henry Holt, 1927), esp. 15–16, 35, 98, 116–17, 126, 211. **Verified** (Project Gutenberg #71000, page-anchored).
*Where:* §"Keeping the jobs apart," beside Warner (n.38); or a note in the Royce section.
*Why:* Dewey gives the pragmatist name for people held together without introductions: "The public consists of all those who are affected by the indirect consequences of transactions to such an extent that it is deemed necessary to have those consequences systematically cared for" (15–16); "Those indirectly and seriously affected for good or for evil form a group distinctive enough to require recognition and a name. The Great Society created by steam and electricity may be a society, but it is no community" (98), with the diagnosis that "the resultant public cannot identify and distinguish itself" (126). Warner is downstream of this; a reviewer in this domain will ask where Dewey is. Handled well, Dewey confirms holding-without-membership from a second direction and lets the chapter claim the *split* (reach vs. selection) rather than the general point as its contribution.
*Consequence:* would-strengthen. *Priority:* high.

## 5. Threats — the strongest objections this domain will raise

1. **You used half of Royce.** Royce's answer to "how do you end up next to someone you were never introduced to" is not a test; it is a third. "I mean to interpret you to somebody else, to some other neighbor, who is neither yourself nor myself" (2:209). A chapter arguing that a deferred third does two jobs, in a book about thirds, cannot leave the community of interpretation unmentioned. Worse, a reviewer can turn it into a rival reading: the camera *is* Royce's interpreter — it interprets one stranger to a third party, the viewer — which would make selection an act of interpretation and put an addressee inside the film's structure that the chapter's account does not have. The chapter should either take that reading up (it strengthens the platform contrast: the platform interprets and refuses to be addressed) or foreclose it (the camera never addresses anyone and nobody's office is interchangeable with it, so the will to interpret is absent).

2. **Royce forbids your application.** At 2:44 Royce says that many present selves cannot possess "the recent past" in common at all. Gary vanished within months, not generations. On Royce's own restriction the Drag scene fails the memory test before acceptance is even in question. The chapter's verdict survives; its stated reason does not do the work alone. Cite 2:44 and turn the objection into an argument.

3. **The SEP note will not hold.** Note 24 says the SEP entry "orders the conditions the same way." It does not enumerate them. Anyone who follows the citation finds nothing there, and one bad note damages the rest.

4. **The gloss you credit to Kegley is Royce's sentence.** Note 23 has Kegley glossing "We are of the same canoe" as "We are of the same community." Royce writes both, adjacently, at 2:47. Attributing his sentence to his commentator is the kind of slip a Royce specialist reads as a sign the primary text was not opened.

5. **Royce is not a neutral instrument.** Since Curry 2018 and *The Pluralist*'s 2021 symposium, using Royce's community conditions to adjudicate who belongs is a positioned move. The chapter's own aside about "the discriminatory history of club doors" makes the omission louder, not quieter. One endnote closes it.

6. **Dewey said the general thing first.** "The Great Society created by steam and electricity may be a society, but it is no community" (*Public*, 98); "the resultant public cannot identify and distinguish itself" (126). If the chapter does not cite him, a reviewer will read the Royce section as reinventing 1927. If it does cite him, Dewey becomes corroboration and the contribution narrows correctly to the reach/selection split and to the addressable grant.

7. **The permissiveness list overshoots.** "It tolerates… the absence of any meeting" contradicts the chapter's own next paragraph and Royce's second condition (2:67). Small, but it sits three sentences from the section's load-bearing claim.

8. **Provincialism cuts both ways.** Royce's own account of a place holding strangers demands assimilation and calls unassimilated newcomers a social danger (*Race Questions*, 73). If the chapter reaches for it, it inherits a normative pull toward assimilation it does not want, and Curry's critique arrives with it.

## 6. Verdict

The Royce section is textually accurate where it quotes and defensible in its conclusion. Every quotation in notes 22 and 24 is verbatim at the page given; the Tai-Nui pages are right; the three conditions are in Royce's own order; and the inference — that a day of unclaimed strangers fails a definition built on acceptance — is sound, indeed better supported than the chapter knows, since Royce says at 2:55 that not every social group meets all the conditions and at 2:44 that contemporaries cannot hold the recent past in common. Two apparatus defects need repair before submission: note 24 credits the SEP entry with an ordering it does not contain, and note 23 credits Kegley with a sentence Royce wrote at 2:47. The larger exposure is not error but omission. The chapter uses Royce as a filter and ignores that Royce's positive theory of how strangers become one is itself a doctrine of the third — an interpreter who addresses somebody, whose office is interchangeable, and whose absence is exactly what the chapter says platforms produce. Adding three sentences and one note on the community of interpretation, one clause from *The Philosophy of Loyalty* on the assent of the will, one note acknowledging the Curry controversy, and one placement of Dewey's public would convert the section from a borrowed instrument into the chapter's second load-bearing argument. As it stands the section is defensible; it is not yet doing the work available to it.
