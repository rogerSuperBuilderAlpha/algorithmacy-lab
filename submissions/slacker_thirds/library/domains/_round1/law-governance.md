## 1. Terrain

Reich's argument has two halves, and the chapter uses one of them. The descriptive half — that modern wealth arrives as largess, and that "the granting, regulation, and revocation of government largess is carried on by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient" (73 Yale L.J. at 751) — is the half the door scene borrows, and it borrows it exactly. The prescriptive half, that "the denial of any form of privilege or benefit on the basis of undisclosed reasons should no longer be tolerated" (at 783), did not remain a hope. Justice Brennan wrote it into constitutional law six years later: *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970), requires an oral hearing because "written submissions are an unrealistic option for most recipients" (at 269), an opportunity "to confront and cross-examine the witnesses relied on" (at 270), an impartial decision maker, and a decision maker who "should state the reasons for his determination and indicate the evidence he relied on" (at 271). *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 577 (1972), then narrowed the doorway: a benefit is property only where the claimant has "more than a unilateral expectation of it," and instead "a legitimate claim of entitlement to it." Steve at the guest list is Roth's plaintiff in miniature.

The live debate since has been about what process is *for* once the decider is a machine. Mashaw's dignitary line (61 B.U. L. Rev. 885) holds that procedure matters apart from accuracy because it shows respect for the person; his *Bureaucratic Justice* (1983) then shows how mass adjudication substitutes bureaucratic rationality for that respect. Citron carried the argument to code in 2008; Van Loo, Klonick and Suzor carried it to platforms; and the current fight, after Green (2022) and Crootof, Kaminski and Price (2023), is whether a mandated "human in the loop" restores anything at all. That fight has a judgment attached. On 4 April 2023 the Amsterdam Court of Appeal (ECLI:NL:GHAMS:2023:793) held that Uber's deactivations of four drivers were decisions based solely on automated processing under GDPR art. 22, rejecting the company's human reviewers as "not… much more than a purely symbolic act."

Where the chapter sits: its legal apparatus is accurate at the points it pins, and its three EU citations are exactly right. It is exposed on three fronts. It treats the human-answerer requirement as new, when GDPR art. 22(3) — "the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision" — has been in force since 2018 and its ancestor, art. 15 of Directive 95/46/EC, since 1995. It treats the enactment of remedies as evidence of the vacancy, when California enacted one in 2020 (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 7452(c)) and the vacancy persisted anyway. And it does not know that Australia has already put the doorman back in the doorway, in terms nearly identical to the chapter's own.

## 3. Audit — every claim in this domain, with a verdict

### n. 26 — Reich's largess quotation
**Claim:** Wealth increasingly takes the form of largess, granted, regulated and revoked "by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient."
**Verdict: sound.** Reich, "The New Property," 751, verbatim. The chapter also correctly flags that the four acts named in its own text are its reading of the scene and not Reich's scheme.

### n. 26–27 — carrying Reich to a private door
**Claim:** "Reich wrote about government largess, and he later gave up the public–private line for large organizations himself; the door performs his structure in miniature and in private."
**Verdict: sound.** Reich, "Liberty Impact," 300–01. Worth knowing that Reich's own example there is *NCAA v. Tarkanian*, 109 S. Ct. 454 (1988), which he cites as the Court's *failure* to require a quasi-governmental private body to observe constitutional fairness. Reich abandoned the line as a matter of policy; the doctrine did not. A due-process reader will notice that the chapter is standing on the losing side of *Tarkanian*, which is fine — but say so.

### n. 28 — "the demand remains a hope appended to the description"
**Claim:** Reich demanded that denial on undisclosed grounds "should no longer be tolerated." "The door never reaches that sentence, and the demand remains a hope appended to the description."
**Verdict: contested-by-domain.** The quotation is exact (783). The gloss is not. *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254, 271 (1970), turned that demand into constitutional doctrine six years later: the decision maker "should state the reasons for his determination and indicate the evidence he relied on," before an impartial decision maker, after an oral hearing. Read as a claim about the club door the sentence is true; read as a claim about Reich's demand it is false, and a legal reader will read it the second way.
**Fix:** "The door never reaches that sentence. Six years later a welfare hearing did — *Goldberg v. Kelly* required exactly the statement of reasons Reich asked for — and the club door was never in its reach."

### body, §"The two jobs" — "Steve's error concerns what being on it would have been worth"
**Verdict: underclaimed.** The error has a doctrinal name. *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 577 (1972): "To have a property interest in a benefit, a person clearly must have more than an abstract need or desire for it. He must have more than a unilateral expectation of it. He must, instead, have a legitimate claim of entitlement to it." Steve has a unilateral expectation; the door's record is what would have converted it into an entitlement. One clause would buy the chapter the whole entitlement doctrine.

### body, §"The two jobs" — "Addressability and accountability are distinct properties"
**Claim:** "A person at a door can be as arbitrary as any system, on grounds no better disclosed… Addressability and accountability are distinct properties, and the film keeps only the first in view. The grant nonetheless passes from one person to another, and Steve can talk back to the door even though the door owes him nothing."
**Verdict: unsupported.** This is the chapter's load-bearing normative joint and it currently rests on assertion. The literature that argues process has value apart from outcome is the dignitary line — Mashaw, "Administrative Due Process: The Quest for a Dignitary Theory," 61 B.U. L. Rev. 885 (1981) — and it is a *contested* line; Mashaw's own title concedes the quest is unfinished. Without it, a reader can simply answer: if the door owes Steve nothing, addressability buys nothing, and the platform took nothing away.

### body, §"The two jobs" — "as the discriminatory history of club doors attests"
**Verdict: unsupported.** No citation. In this domain the obvious anchor is public-accommodations law (Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964; state analogues), which is exactly the body of law that treats a private doorman's discretion as reviewable. Either cite something or cut the appeal to history.

### body, §"The same jobs" + n. 34 — "the European Union now requires statements of reasons for platform terminations"
**Verdict: overclaimed (scope).** True but jurisdictionally narrower than the sentence implies. P2B art. 4 protects *business users of online intermediation services*; DSA art. 17 protects *recipients of hosting services*. Neither reaches a rideshare driver in the capacity the chapter cares about. The instrument that does is the Platform Work Directive — which is not yet operative.
**Fix:** name the addressee in each case, in one clause.

### body, §"The same jobs" — "its platform-work directive orders that any decision… 'shall be taken by a human being'"
**Verdict: overclaimed (temporal).** The quotation is exact (art. 10(5), verified verbatim). But art. 29(1) sets the transposition deadline at **2 December 2026**; the directive binds Member States, not platforms, until then. As of the chapter's own access date (18 August 2026) no platform is subject to the requirement.
**Fix:** "orders Member States to ensure, by December 2026, that any decision… 'shall be taken by a human being.'"

### n. 34 — the EU pin cites
**Verdict: sound, all three.** P2B art. 4 (statements of reasons; thirty days' notice for termination) ✓. DSA arts. 17 and 20(6), including the quoted "not solely on the basis of automated means" ✓. Directive 2024/2831 arts. 10(5) and 11 (the human-being requirement; the explanation right and the designated contact person) ✓. This is the most precise footnote in the chapter and it survives checking intact.
**One addition worth making:** art. 11(1) requires the written statement of reasons "without undue delay and **at the latest on the date on which it takes effect**." That is the chapter's own irreversibility point, in the instrument's words. And art. 11(5) excludes platform workers who are also P2B business users — a seam a reviewer may probe.

### n. 34 — "RCW 49.46.310"
**Verdict: misattributed.** RCW 49.46.310 is *Transportation network companies—Driver resource center fund—Selection of driver resource center—Intent*, a funding mechanism. Washington's deactivation-review regime is **RCW 49.46.300(15)** (as amended by 2025 c 229 § 3, effective 1 July 2026): it defines "eligible account deactivation," requires each transportation network company to enter a department-approved agreement with the driver resource center, and specifies the appeals process — representation, disclosure of "an explanation and information the transportation network company may have relied upon in making the deactivation decision" within 30 calendar days, an informal resolution stage, and "a formal process that includes a just cause standard… by a panel that includes a mutually agreed-upon neutral third party."
**Fix:** `Wash. Rev. Code § 49.46.300(15)`.

### n. 34 — "on the same pattern"
**Claim:** "Washington State and Seattle have enacted deactivation-review regimes on the same pattern."
**Verdict: overclaimed.** Washington's is not the EU pattern. The EU pattern is a unilateral duty to state reasons; Washington's is a *negotiated* regime — a statutorily compelled agreement between the company and a driver resource center, culminating in binding arbitration under a just cause standard. That is closer to labour arbitration than to administrative due process, and it is arguably a better fit for the chapter's thesis: it restores a *counterparty*, not merely a notice.

### body, §"The same jobs" — "That such remedies were thought necessary is itself evidence of the vacancy they address"
**Verdict: underclaimed.** The available evidence is stronger and more embarrassing. California enacted a remedy in 2020: Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 7452(b)–(c) (Proposition 22, eff. 16 Dec. 2020) provides that "a network company shall not terminate a contract with an app-based driver unless based upon a ground specified in the contract" and that companies "shall provide an appeals process for app-based drivers whose contracts are terminated." The chapter's own exhibit — the Rideshare Drivers United survey of **California** drivers, February 2023, thirty percent given no reason at all — is therefore a record of a statute already on the books and not working. That reframes the inference: the vacancy is not merely unlegislated, it is unfilled *after* legislation.

### n. 35 — Viljoen
**Verdict: sound.** The paraphrase (a horizontal, population-based relation places a person in a grouping through no act of his own, on the basis of what he shares with others he has never met) tracks Viljoen at 607–08 and 610; the "collective, democratic governance" remedy tracks 644–46 and 649–51. Currently cited without a pin.
**Fix:** add `, at 607–10, 644–46`.

### body, §"The same jobs" — "The address exists; the answerer of the doorman's kind… does not"
**Verdict: sound, and now judicially confirmed.** Gerechtshof Amsterdam, 4 April 2023, ECLI:NL:GHAMS:2023:793 (case 200.295.742/01) held that Uber's deactivation of four drivers was a decision based solely on automated processing within GDPR art. 22, rejecting the company's asserted human reviewers as "not… much more than a purely symbolic act." The chapter is making, as an interpretive claim, something a court has found as fact.

### n. 36 — the negative claim
**Claim:** "Reach and selection are this chapter's terms; no retrieved source pairs the city as reach with the cut as selection."
**Verdict: sound, as to this domain.** Nothing in the new-property, due-process, or platform-regulation literature I searched pairs urban availability with cinematic selection; the pairing is not a legal-scholarly move and no counterexample surfaced. This is weak confirmation only — the claim's real exposure is in film and urban theory, not here.

### Bibliography formatting
**Verdict: overclaimed by omission.** The three EU instruments appear without Official Journal citations or dates. Chicago (17th ed., 14.286 and the *Bluebook* conventions it defers to for foreign material) would carry: Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 of 20 June 2019, OJ L 186, 11.7.2019, p. 57; Regulation (EU) 2022/2065 of 19 October 2022, OJ L 277, 27.10.2022, p. 1; Directive (EU) 2024/2831 of 23 October 2024, OJ L, 2024/2831, 11.11.2024.

## 4. Missing works, ranked

### Would change the argument

**1. Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 7452 (Proposition 22, approved 3 November 2020, eff. 16 December 2020).**
`ca2020prop22`. §7452(b): "A network company shall not terminate a contract with an app-based driver unless based upon a ground specified in the contract." §7452(c): "Network companies shall provide an appeals process for app-based drivers whose contracts are terminated by the network company."
*Where:* §"The same jobs without an answerer," at the Rideshare Drivers United sentence, and n. 32.
*Why:* the chapter's central empirical exhibit is a California survey. California had required an appeals process for two years when that survey ran. The thirty-percent-no-reason figure is therefore evidence of a *failed* remedy, not of an unregulated state of nature — which is a stronger fact for the chapter and a different argument from the one it now makes at "that such remedies were thought necessary is itself evidence of the vacancy."
*Verified:* leginfo.ca.gov, BPC § 7452, full text read.

**2. Gerechtshof Amsterdam, 4 April 2023, ECLI:NL:GHAMS:2023:793 (Drivers v. Uber B.V.), case no. 200.295.742/01.**
`ghams2023uber`. The "robo-firing" judgment: deactivation of four drivers held to be a decision based solely on automated processing under GDPR art. 22; the asserted human review dismissed as "not… much more than a purely symbolic act."
*Where:* §"The same jobs without an answerer," at "The address exists; the answerer of the doorman's kind… does not," and at the sentence on legislating the doorman back into the doorway.
*Why:* it forces the chapter to distinguish two things it now runs together — a human *in the decision loop* and a person *in the doorway*. The doorman's properties were presence, priority in time, publicity, and the possibility of being answered on the spot. Art. 10(5) supplies only the first of those, and this judgment is the proof that supplying only the first supplies nothing.
*Verified:* metadata-only. ECLI, court, date and case number confirmed via rechtspraak.nl's index and GDPRhub; the quoted phrase is taken from English-language reports (Worker Info Exchange; Fountain Court; Ponce del Castillo), not from the Dutch original. **Do not quote it as the court's words without reading ECLI:NL:GHAMS:2023:793 itself.**

### Would strengthen (high)

**3. Fair Work (Digital Labour Platform Deactivation Code) Instrument 2024 (Cth), F2024L01573, made 3 December 2024, commenced 25 February 2025 — esp. ss 8, 11, 13.**
`aus2024deactivationcode`. Section 13(4)–(7): the worker "may… request the digital labour platform operator to make a representative of the operator available to discuss the preliminary deactivation notice"; the operator "must make a representative available for the discussion within a reasonable time"; a support person may participate; and "a human representative of the digital labour platform operator must consider the employee-like worker's response (if any), including the discussion (if any)."
*Where:* §"The same jobs without an answerer," at "The law has begun trying to legislate the doorman back into the doorway."
*Why:* this is the chapter's own sentence enacted. Australia does not merely require a human decision-maker after the fact; it requires a person to be produced, on request, for a conversation, before the deactivation takes effect. Omitting the one instrument that fills the vacancy while asserting the vacancy is the sweep's sharpest exposure.
*Verified:* authorised PDF from the Federal Register of Legislation, s 13 read verbatim; commencement table (s 2) confirms 25 February 2025.

**4. GDPR, Regulation (EU) 2016/679, art. 22 — with Directive 95/46/EC, art. 15, as its ancestor.**
`eu2016gdpr`. Art. 22(1): a right "not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing… which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her." Art. 22(3): "at least the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision."
*Where:* n. 34, opening.
*Why:* the requirement of a human who can be told your side is not new and did not begin with the platform-work directive. It has been directly applicable EU law since 2018 and a Member State obligation since 1995. The chapter's "the law has begun trying" understates by three decades, and the correction costs one clause while making the chapter's structural point better: the vacancy was legislated against long before anyone thought it a novelty.
*Verified:* EUR-Lex CELEX 32016R0679 art. 22 and 31995L0046 art. 15, both read verbatim.

**5. *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970).**
`goldberg1970kelly`. At 269: "Written submissions are an unrealistic option for most recipients"; a recipient "must be allowed to state his position orally." At 270: an opportunity "to confront and cross-examine the witnesses relied on." At 271: "an impartial decision maker is essential," and the decision maker "should state the reasons for his determination and indicate the evidence he relied on."
*Where:* §"The two jobs," at n. 28.
*Why:* it is the case Reich's demand became, and it is a case about *orality* — about why a hearing must be with someone, out loud, rather than on paper. That is the chapter's thesis in a Supreme Court opinion, and the chapter currently says the demand went nowhere.
*Verified:* Library of Congress *U.S. Reports* scan, all four passages located and page-mapped.

**6. *Board of Regents of State Colleges v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564 (1972).**
`roth1972regents`. At 577: "He must have more than a unilateral expectation of it. He must, instead, have a legitimate claim of entitlement to it."
*Where:* §"The two jobs," at "Steve's error concerns what being on it would have been worth."
*Why:* names Steve's error and, with it, the whole difference the chapter draws between the guest list (a grant, confirmable only in the grantor's record) and the hand stamp (property, showable and transferable).
*Verified:* Library of Congress *U.S. Reports* scan, passage located on p. 577.

**7. Jerry L. Mashaw, "Administrative Due Process: The Quest for a Dignitary Theory," *Boston University Law Review* 61 (1981): 885–931.**
`mashaw1981dignitary`.
*Where:* §"The two jobs," at "Addressability and accountability are distinct properties."
*Why:* the only body of theory that answers "so what?" to a form of address that buys the addressee nothing. Dignitary theories hold that procedure shows respect for a person as a rational agent, independent of outcome. Citing it converts the chapter's assertion into a position inside a known debate — and Mashaw's own scepticism gives the chapter a way to be modest about it.
*Verified:* metadata-only. Volume, pages and year confirmed via the Yale Law School repository record and secondary citations; I did not read the article.

## 5. The strongest objections this domain would raise

1. **A misattributed statute in the chapter's most precise footnote.** RCW 49.46.310 is a fund, not a deactivation regime. Any US labour or administrative lawyer opening n. 34 will find it in a minute, and the error will cast doubt on the three EU pin cites, which are in fact perfect.
2. **The human-being requirement is not yet law for anyone.** Directive 2024/2831 art. 29(1) sets transposition at 2 December 2026. "The European Union now requires" and "orders that any decision… shall be taken by a human being" both read as descriptions of operative law, four months early.
3. **And it is not new.** GDPR art. 22(3) has guaranteed "the right to obtain human intervention… to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision" since 2018; art. 15 of Directive 95/46/EC since 1995. "The law has begun trying" misdates a thirty-year-old project.
4. **The chapter's inference from remedy to vacancy is the weakest link, and better evidence is available.** "That such remedies were thought necessary is itself evidence of the vacancy they address" invites the standard reply that legislation proves the strength of a lobby, not the existence of a wrong. California's § 7452 (2020) plus the chapter's own 2023 California survey give a much stronger argument: the remedy existed and the vacancy persisted.
5. **Australia already did what the chapter says the law is only beginning to attempt.** The Fair Work Deactivation Code s 13(4)–(7) requires the operator to produce a representative for a discussion, on request, before deactivation. The chapter's closing gesture ("the law has begun trying") is behind the instruments.
6. **The normative joint is unsupported.** If the door owes Steve nothing, why does it matter that he can talk back? Without Mashaw's dignitary line the chapter has no answer, and the platform section's pathos loses its warrant.
7. **Reich's demand did not remain a hope.** *Goldberg v. Kelly* is the most-taught case in American administrative law and it is Reich's sentence enacted. The chapter says the opposite.
8. **Three regimes, three different protected persons.** P2B protects business users; DSA art. 17 protects recipients of hosting services; the Platform Work Directive protects persons performing platform work — and art. 11(5) explicitly excludes those who are also P2B business users. The chapter runs from a club door to Uber's deactivation page to all three instruments without saying which subject the doorman analogy governs.
9. **Reich stands on the losing side of the state-action doctrine, and the chapter inherits that.** Reich's 1990 abandonment of the public–private line was a criticism of *NCAA v. Tarkanian*, not a description of the law. The door scene borrows an apparatus that American constitutional law has declined to extend to private doors.

## 6. Verdict

The chapter is defensible in this domain, and closer to defensible than most film-studies chapters that reach for Reich. Its three EU pin cites are exactly right, verbatim, down to paragraph numbering — art. 10(5) really is the human-being sentence, art. 20(6) really is "not solely on the basis of automated means," art. 4 really carries both the statement of reasons and the thirty days. Its two Reich pages check out to the line. Its Viljoen paraphrase is faithful. What fails is narrower and entirely repairable: one misattributed statute (RCW 49.46.310 for 49.46.300(15)), two temporal overreaches (a directive not yet transposed, and a human-answerer requirement thirty years older than the chapter implies), and one unsupported claim at the argument's normative hinge. The deeper exposure is that the chapter's closing move — the law is beginning to legislate the doorman back into the doorway, and that attempt is evidence of the vacancy — is the one place where the domain has newer facts than the chapter does. Australia produced the doorman in February 2025; California required an appeals process in 2020 and drivers still report no reason given; the Amsterdam Court of Appeal found Uber's human reviewers symbolic in April 2023. Each of those makes the chapter's point sharper rather than weaker, but only if the chapter distinguishes what it has so far run together: a human somewhere in the decision, and a person standing in the doorway before the refusal takes effect, who can be answered on the spot. That distinction is the chapter's real contribution to this domain, and it is currently implicit.
