## 1. Terrain

The chapter's first job — REACH, "Austin holds the next person in reach and introduces nobody" — is not a novel proposition in this domain. It is the domain's founding proposition, and it has been stated four times under four names.

Simmel stated it first, and not in the triad chapter the chapter quotes. In "The Metropolis and Mental Life" the metropolitan person meets so many others that inner response to each becomes impossible, and the resulting form is *reserve*: "we frequently do not even know by sight those who have been our neighbors for years." In "The Stranger" the same year, nearness and remoteness are welded into a single position — "he, who is close by, is far." Goffman then converted reserve into an observable ritual. *Civil inattention* is the "enough visual notice to demonstrate that one appreciates that the other is present … while at the next moment withdrawing one's attention" (1963, 84): recognition granted, engagement withheld, in one gesture. Lofland gave the territory its name — the public realm is the sector of a settlement where people in co-presence are "personally unknown or only categorically known to one another" — and Jacobs gave it its normative defense: sidewalk trust accumulates from "many, many little public sidewalk contacts," and its precondition is that the contacts not oblige anyone to anything. Milgram named the residue, the familiar stranger you recognize daily and never address.

The live debates are three. First, whether co-presence produces anything. Valentine's "geographies of encounter" line, and Aelbrecht and Stevens's 2023 systematic review after it, warn against romanticizing contact: sharing a plaza is not sharing a life, and most encounter research since 2008 has been an argument about what converts fleeting contact into anything durable. Second, and cutting the other way, Blokland's *public familiarity* and *absent ties* claim that recognition without interaction already produces belonging — a direct counter-thesis to this chapter's Roycean verdict that holding is not membership. Third, the measurement question: Hägerstrand's time-geography formalizes reach as the prism of who can be met within a day; Duranton and Puga formalize it as two separable urban functions, "improving the quality of matches" and "improving the chances of matching." Small and Adler's 2019 review is where the sociology now sits: propinquity, composition, configuration — three mechanisms, of which mere nearness is the weakest.

Where the chapter sits: squarely inside this domain, using almost none of it. It advances an urban-sociological claim from film evidence, cites Feld only as a foil, Warner and Duranton–Puga only in notes, and Barns for a single phrase. Every other urban work in the folder — Simmel's two urban essays, Goffman, Lofland ×2, Jacobs, Milgram, Anderson, Oldenburg, Klinenberg, Glaeser — sits unspent.

## 3. Audit

### Thesis-level urban claims

**L5 / L15 / L27 / L65 — "Austin holds the day's strangers within reach of one another and introduces none of them."**
**Verdict: underclaimed.** The chapter offers this as its own reading of the film. It is an established property of metropolitan public space, stated by Simmel (reserve), formalized by Goffman (civil inattention), named by Lofland (the public realm), and defended by Jacobs (contact without entanglement). As written, the sentence carries no urban source at all — the entire REACH job rests on a foil (Feld), one phrase from Barns, one note on Duranton and Puga, and one note on Warner.
*Fix:* one sentence and one note in §"Keeping the jobs apart" attaching reach to Lofland's public realm and Goffman's civil inattention, and conceding Simmel 1903 explicitly.

**L29 — "The third of the postponed passage, the one that stands outside without mediating, has remained where Simmel left it."**
**Verdict: overclaimed.** True within the sociology of the third, false as stated. Simmel took up the holding-without-relating structure himself, twice, in 1903 and 1908 — under *reserve* and under *the stranger* rather than under the triad. A Simmel reader will produce the *Metropolis* essay before the end of the section.
*Fix:* scope the sentence ("within the literature on the third…") and add a note: Simmel's own urban essays describe the metropolis as a form that holds without acquainting, but they treat it as a psychology of the individual, not as a third term over against a field — which is the distinction the chapter needs and does not yet draw.

### The Feld contrast (§"Keeping the jobs apart," ¶65, n. 36)

**"…people who share the activity 'will tend to become interpersonally tied and form a cluster'" (1016).**
**Verdict: unsupported as a verbatim quotation.** Feld's *definition* of a focus at 1016 is confirmed twice over. The quoted clause is not: the one citing source retrieved that quotes this sentence with a page gives "tend to become interpersonally connected to form a cluster" (Feld 1981: 1016), a different wording at the same page.
*Fix:* check the AJS page against a copy before submission, or quote the definition of a focus, which is verified.

**"The people on screen, however, share sidewalks and form no cluster, and the joint activity that produces Feld's ties never materializes."**
**Verdict: contested-by-domain.** Feld's foci are explicitly "social, psychological, legal, or physical," hangouts included. Les Amis, the Drag, Sound Exchange, the club, the band, the film crew itself are textbook foci, and clusters demonstrably formed around them — that is what the production histories already cited (Macor, Erickson, Soldani) describe when they call it a *scene*. The no-cluster claim is true of the film's day and false of the city that made the film.
*Fix:* mark the claim diegetically, and concede the scene in a note. Straw's "scene" is the term Soldani is already using.

**n. 36 — "no retrieved source pairs the city as reach with the cut as selection."**
**Verdict: sound, and fragile.** I hunted the counterexample and did not find it. The nearest misses, none of which lands: Friedberg's mobilized virtual gaze (the *viewer* mobilized through urban space, not a world-side holder plus a text-side selector); Bruno's *Atlas of Emotion* (sight/site, motion/emotion, again one continuum rather than two agents); the cinematic-urbanism literature (AlSayyad; Barber), which reads city-through-film rather than splitting the labor; and Smets, Vannieuwenhuyze, and Ballon on urban recommender systems, which performs exactly the split — a city that holds, an algorithm that picks — outside cinema.
*Fix:* keep the hedge, name Friedberg and Smets et al. in the note as the nearest, and the claim becomes hard to attack.

### The Warner note (n. 38)

**"…a public is a relation among strangers constituted by attention, addressed as a whole, with nobody choosing which two members meet next; *Publics and Counterpublics* (New York: Zone Books, 2002), 62ff."**
**Verdict: misattributed.** Page 62 falls inside chapter 1, "Public and Private," which runs 21–64. The premises cited are in chapter 2, "Publics and Counterpublics," 65–124. In the *Public Culture* version I fetched and read, "2. A public is a relation among strangers" sits on p. 55 and "4. A public is constituted through mere attention" on p. 60 (14, no. 1 [2002]: 49–90); the book's standard pages for these are 74ff and 87ff, which I could not confirm against a copy.
Second, smaller problem: "with nobody choosing which two members meet next" is the chapter's inference, not Warner's sentence. Warner's public is constituted by attention to circulating discourse, not by spatial co-presence, so it is a contrast to reach in a different register than the note implies.
*Fix:* cite "chap. 2, esp. the second and fourth premises," or confirm 74–76 and 87–89; and mark the "which two members meet" clause as a gloss.

### The agglomeration note (n. 39)

**"…distinguishing whether density improves the quality of a match or the probability of one."**
**Verdict: sound, with a small overreach.** Verified against the authors' text: §3.1 "Improving the quality of matches," §3.2 "Improving the chances of matching." The "whether … or" implies a disjunction Duranton and Puga do not make — they present the two as complementary mechanisms, alongside a third (mitigating hold-up).
*Fix:* "distinguishing density's effect on the quality of matches from its effect on the chances of one." Add Duranton and Puga 2020 (JEP) so the note is not twenty-two years old.

### The Barns quotation (¶65)

**"basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects."**
**Verdict: sound.** Verified verbatim from the Frontiers full text, opening paragraph of the Introduction: "platform services institute basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects." Page 1 is right for a Frontiers article's opening.

### The door scenes (¶39–43)

**"A person at a door can be as arbitrary as any system, on grounds no better disclosed, as the discriminatory history of club doors attests."**
**Verdict: unsupported as cited.** The concession is the right one and it carries real weight in the argument — it is what keeps the chapter from sentimentalizing the doorman. It arrives with no source. Rivera's ethnography of an elite nightclub door is exactly this evidence: doormen sort on status cues, perceived social connections outweigh everything else, and admission is conferred as a status prize with no reasons given.
*Fix:* one note to Rivera 2010; optionally Mears 2020 for the guest list as an institution.

### The sidewalk pairing (¶45–47)

**"…the asker and the talker had never met, the city held them within reach of one another … and nobody introduced them."**
**Verdict: underclaimed.** The talker's approach is a *breach of civil inattention* — Goffman's term for it, and Goffman's rules for who may open a face engagement with whom, are precisely the vocabulary the chapter says it lacks when it calls this "the film's hard case, a choice inside the story that the camera then ratifies."
*Fix:* Goffman 1963, 84 and the face-engagement chapter, in a note at the hard case.

### The scope claim about network-narrative theory (¶15)

**"All of these joining agents are textual, and the question of a worldly arrangement … does not arise."**
**Verdict: sound, narrowly.** Within the named literature (Bordwell, Poulaki, Ciafone, Tröhler) I found no counterexample. Outside it, the question is the whole subject of cinematic urbanism (AlSayyad 2006; Barber 2002), and Ramirez — whom the chapter already quotes on the "seamless urban tissue" — is asking it about this very film.
*Fix:* scope the sentence explicitly to the network-narrative literature.

## 5. Threats — what this domain would say against the chapter

1. **Simmel already wrote the REACH section.** "The Metropolis and Mental Life" describes the city as holding people in unceasing contact while withholding acquaintance. The claim that the postponed third "has remained where Simmel left it" is false of Simmel's own corpus, even if true of the triad literature. A Simmel reader raises this immediately, and the chapter has no sentence ready.
2. **The two jobs restate Goffman.** Unfocused interaction (co-presence, mutual monitoring, no engagement) and focused interaction (a face engagement opened by someone toward someone) is the distinction between holding and selecting, in print since 1963, done by world-side agents. The chapter's novelty is that the *selector* is the camera rather than a person — which is defensible, and which the chapter must say out loud rather than leave for a reviewer to say.
3. **The Feld foil is unfair to Feld and to Austin.** Feld's foci include hangouts and physical entities. The film's Austin is dense with them, and the historical record already cited says a scene formed. The no-cluster claim survives only if scoped to the film's day.
4. **Holding may already be membership.** Blokland's public familiarity and absent ties, and Zahnow and Corcoran after her, argue that recognition without interaction produces belonging. If they are right, the Royce section's verdict is contestable on empirical grounds and not only on definitional ones.
5. **The Warner note is in the wrong chapter, and in the wrong register.** Page 62 is "Public and Private"; and a public is discursive, constituted by attention to circulating text, not a spatial arrangement of bodies. The contrast still works, but not as drawn.
6. **Who is not held?** Reach is described as uniform. Anderson's canopy, Lofland's public realm, and the encounter literature all insist that public space holds people differentially by race, gender, and class, and that its refusals are as structured as its holdings. The chapter concedes this once, about doors, and never about sidewalks.
7. **Reach is asserted, never measured.** Two literatures already measure it — time-geography (prisms, coupling constraints) and agglomeration economics (matching probability). The chapter borrows the second in a note and ignores the first. A reviewer will ask what would falsify "the city held them in reach."
8. **The "third" collision is live.** Oldenburg's *third place* is a place-type: home, work, hangout. The chapter's third is a party in a relational geometry. *Slacker* is set almost entirely in third places, and the chapter never says the two thirds are unrelated. **Not a trap the chapter has fallen into — a trap it has not yet stepped around.** One sentence in a note, at the first mention of a café or a bar, closes it permanently.

## 6. Verdict

Defensible in outline, under-evidenced in this domain. The chapter's REACH job is a real urban proposition and a correct one, and every claim I could check against a fetched record came back either sound or repairable by a footnote — Barns verbatim, Duranton and Puga verbatim in structure, Feld's page right if not his sentence. What the chapter does not have is a single body sentence connecting its own urban claim to the urban literature that has been making it since 1903, while the folder holds Simmel's two urban essays, Goffman, Lofland twice, Jacobs, Milgram, Anderson, Oldenburg, Klinenberg, and Glaeser unused. That is not a citation-count problem. It is that three claims — the postponement claim at the end of the Simmel section, the no-cluster claim against Feld, and the Warner note's locus — are each falsifiable as written, and each is falsified by a work already sitting in the library. The repairs are small: scope two sentences, move one citation, add four notes (Goffman at the sidewalk, Rivera at the door, Blokland at Royce, Small and Adler at Feld), and disarm the Oldenburg collision. Nothing here requires the thesis to move.
