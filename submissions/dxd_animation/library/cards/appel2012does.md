## What it argues

Abstract-only card; the full text was not read. Per the abstract, Appel, von der Pütten, Krämer and Gratch set two accounts of why artificial entities draw social behaviour from people against each other. The agency assumption says a human partner (shown as an avatar) always exerts more social influence than an artificial one (an embodied conversational agent). The Ethopoeia concept says social reactions fire automatically whenever a situation contains social cues. They test both in a 2x2 between-subjects design: agency (low: agent, high: avatar) crossed with interface (low in cues: text chat, high: virtual human). Participants in the virtual-human condition reported stronger mutual awareness, imputed more positive characteristics and gave the virtual human more attention than the text-chat participants. Only one result supported the agency assumption: those who believed they were talking to a human reported stronger social presence. The authors discuss how far the results support the social-cue assumption.

## What the paper uses it for

- c6 (the viewer locks in; the user also animates): it is an empirical test of whether cues alone recruit the user, whoever stands behind the character. The abstract's result that cues mattered more than believed agency fits the paper's claim. It does stretch the source in one way: the study compared a text chat with a virtual human, and the abstract reports the virtual human drawing stronger reactions than the text chat, so it does not show that a text-only LLM interface locks users in. The abstract also reports one effect for believed agency, which cuts a little against a cues-only story.
