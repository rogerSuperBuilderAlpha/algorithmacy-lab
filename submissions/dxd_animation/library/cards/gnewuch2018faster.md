## What it argues

Abstract-only card; the paper was not read. Per the OpenAlex abstract, Gnewuch, Morana, Adam and Maedche start from the problem of making conversation with a chatbot feel natural and human-like. Many chatbots delay their replies to mimic how long a human would take, but delay may cut satisfaction where fast replies are expected, as in customer service. They report an online experiment in a customer-service setting that compares a chatbot sending dynamically delayed replies (delay length computed from the complexity of the reply and of the previous message) with one sending near-instant replies. Dynamic delays raised users' perceived humanness and social presence and also raised satisfaction with the whole interaction. Building on social response theory, they argue that a chatbot's response time is a social cue that triggers social responses shaped by social expectations.

## What the paper uses it for

- c5 (animation is opaque: missing frames; the interstices are the craft): a chatbot's reply timing is a made interval, and the experiment shows users read meaning into it. The abstract does not use animation vocabulary, so framing delay as an interstice is the paper's.
- c6 (the viewer locks in; the user also animates): it is a contrast. The finding that delay as a social cue increased satisfaction runs against the view that hiding or filling the wait is the safe design, and it shows users responding to timing cues. It rests on one customer-service experiment, as far as the abstract shows.
