## What it argues

Daniel Szafir, Bilge Mutlu and Terrence Fong ask how a free-flying robot can show the people around it where it is going. Their robots are "assistive free-flyers" (AFFs): small aerial robots such as quadrotors, and NASA's Smart SPHERES, "designed to be used inside spacecraft" (§1, p. 358). The authors never define intent formally. They gloss it as the robot's plans for "where, when, how far, and how fast it will move" (locus 2). From animation they take three techniques. They list eleven animation principles, crediting Krasner (2013, [22]) and Lasseter (1987, [25]) (locus 4), and pick arcs, easing ("slow in and out") and anticipation from among them (locus 5). Each becomes a parameterised change to a flight primitive. An arc bends the straight path. Easing ramps velocity up and down along a Gaussian profile, adapted from Kwon and Lee's slow-in and slow-out filter [24] and Gielniak et al.'s velocity profiles [12] (§3.3.2, p. 360). Anticipation, credited to Kim et al. [19], sends the robot about a third of the trajectory's length the wrong way first, which lengthens the flight by 33% (locus 6).

Two studies test the techniques. The first, which the authors treat as "design exploration lacking a priori hypotheses" (§4, p. 361), showed 85 Mechanical Turk workers ten 10-second videos of a virtual AFF in a warehouse, in a 2 × 2 × 2 between-subjects design with arc, easing and anticipation each present or absent (locus 8). Each viewer paused the video once they could tell where the robot was going (response time), then picked one of four destinations (accuracy; locus 7). A Guttman-based composite combined the two. No manipulation significantly raised accuracy for any single primitive (locus 9). Pooled over all primitives, easing plus anticipation raised accuracy only marginally, p = .071 (locus 10). Arcing plus easing beat the baseline on the composite when the robot avoided a person (p = .003 and p = .002). Anticipation, by contrast, significantly lowered the composite for several primitives, and for all primitives together F(1, 77) = 13.66, p < .001 (Table 2, p. 362). The authors put that down to slower responses to longer paths (locus 11).

The second study built flights from "the motion manipulation that had achieved the highest composite score in our initial study" for each primitive (§5, p. 362) and flew them in a 2 × 1 within-participants lab experiment with a Parrot AR.Drone 2.0, run Wizard-of-Oz, with 24 participants from the University of Wisconsin–Madison campus and a confederate posing as a fellow participant (§§5.2–5.3, pp. 362–363). Anticipation appeared in one primitive only: distances for it were "used only for approaching objects" (§5.2, p. 363; Table 3). The experimenters told participants they were rating two operators and asked them to identify which QR codes each operator targeted (locus 12). The paper reports no result for that identification task. It reports ratings: participants preferred the "operator" whose flight was manipulated, F(1, 23) = 13.39, p = .001 (locus 13), found the manipulated paths "more natural, smooth, elegant, graceful, and intuitive", F(1, 23) = 5.24, p = .032, and rated the robot "more safe and in control", F(1, 23) = 5.18, p = .033 (§5.5, p. 363). The authors conclude that the work shows "a potential link" running from the animation principles, through intent expressed in the first study, to usability in the second (locus 14).

## What the paper uses it for

- **m5 (support, with a caveat).** The paper does not show that anticipation, or any of its cues, improved observers' prediction of the robot's path. In the formative study no manipulation significantly raised accuracy for any primitive (locus 9), the pooled gain for easing plus anticipation was marginal (locus 10), and anticipation on its own slowed viewers down (locus 11). The lab study measured no prediction at all, only ratings, and there the manipulated flights won on preference, naturalness and felt safety (locus 13; §5.5). The direction matches Takayama, Dooley and Ju (2011; takayama2011expressing), whose forethought cue raised sureness and appeal but left keyword accuracy unchanged. The caveat is design: Szafir et al. split the two outcomes across studies, so no single sample shows ratings rising while accuracy stays flat. Cite the paper for 'ratings rose; gains in prediction were not shown'. Do not cite it as a within-study dissociation, and do not cite it as evidence that anticipation makes intent more readable. The authors' own summaries read more strongly: the first study "found that modified flight motions might allow AFFs to more effectively communicate intent" (locus 1), and "our manipulations proved effective" (§6, p. 364).
- **m3 (supports the technique; cuts against its payoff).** Anticipation here is motion before the move, the shape of row 3's cue before a rule change. In this study the cue cost time and bought no significant accuracy, and the authors name the cost as "a potential trade-off between how quickly and how accurately users are able to predict robot intent" (§4.4, p. 362). A cue that lengthens what comes before a change has a price, and the model should state it. The cue also announces the robot's next move, not a change to rules that bind anyone; carrying it to governance changes is an analogy this source does not test.
- **m1 (indirect; my reading of their design).** The lab study's cover story gave participants two "operators" who did not exist; the experimenter flew both paths (§5.2, pp. 362–363). The usability scale asked "how well they believed the operator performed" and how confident they were in "the operator's control of the AFF" (§5.5, p. 363). Motion craft therefore shifted the credit people gave to a seat that was not there. That bears on idempotent interpretation, the reading of which seat produced an output, but the authors do not discuss it.
- **m4 (partly).** The authors choose three of eleven principles for a robot without limbs or a face, arguing that earlier work "utilizes humanlike robots with limbs and expressive faces—features AFF's generally lack" (§2.2, p. 359), and they screen the manipulations against viewers' readings before building the lab flights (locus 5; §5, p. 362). That is selection through a measured response of the receiver. They offer no account of the receiver beyond those measures, and their screen ranked manipulations on the composite, which rewards speed as well as accuracy.

## Caution

- **How the authors define "intent" and "legibility".** They give intent no formal definition. They gloss it as where, when, how far and how fast the robot will move (locus 2) and measure it as a choice among four destinations plus the time taken to commit (§4.3, p. 361). "Legibility" is not their own term. They take it from Dragan, Lee and Srinivasa (2013, [5]) and Lichtenthaler et al. (2012, [26]): legible motion lets users "correctly infer the robot’s goal", predictable motion matches "the expectations of an observer who knew the goal a priori" (locus 3). They report that viewers of Dragan et al.'s legibility-optimised, arced trajectories "were better able to predict an end target" (§2.1, p. 359), and they cite [5] again for "optimized arc trajectories can improve motion legibility even for point robots" (§3.3, p. 360). Their own phrase for the goal is "intent-expressive AFF flight motion" (§3.3, p. 360). In each use, legibility means an observer reading a robot's goal from its motion. That is the robotics sense, not the author's sense of a user reading the algorithm, which his ruling sets aside ("Algorithmacy is not legibility"; `../MODEL.md` §3). The paper should gloss the homonym if it cites this work (see dragan2013legibility).
- **Accuracy as printed.** Table 2 names the best manipulation for each primitive on accuracy, marks perfect accuracy with a degree sign, and gives a p-value only for the pooled row (p = .071). The paper prints no accuracy rates or means for any condition. Several cells reach 100% accuracy, including the no-manipulation baseline for departing a person seen egocentrically ("Ease, none˚", Table 2, p. 362). A ceiling may therefore have limited what any cue could add; that is my inference, not the authors'.
- **The two studies measure different things.** The formative study has accuracy and speed but no ratings; the lab study has ratings but no accuracy. The lab study also bundles manipulations by primitive, so it cannot credit any rating to anticipation, which appeared in one primitive of nine (Table 3, p. 363). Figure 6 prints p = .003 for the manipulation check, where the text gives F(1, 23) = 12.00, p = .002 (§5.5, p. 363; Figure 6, p. 364). The authors' post hoc power for naturalness was .518 (§5.5, p. 363).
- **Prior claims they report about anticipation.** Szafir et al. say that Gielniak and Thomaz [13] generated anticipation "that increased observers’ abilities to accurately perceive intent" (§2.2, p. 359), and they describe Takayama et al. [40] as enabling a robot "to effectively express forethought and reactions to task outcomes" (§2.2, p. 359). They do not mention Takayama et al.'s null result on accuracy (takayama2011expressing, locus 10). I have not read Gielniak and Thomaz; it is an unchecked lead that may cut the other way on m5.
- **Confounds the authors state.** The quadrotor's own pitch and roll "may themselves provide anticipatory movements telegraphing motion intent" and, being larger without easing, may have shrunk the differences between conditions (§6, p. 364). The virtual AFF had no front, and its orientation was kept constant (§4.1, p. 361); the AR.Drone had a front, and in both conditions the operator turned it to face the current target before each primitive (§5.2, p. 363).
- **Ethics.** The lab study misled participants by design: the two "operators" and the fellow participant were a cover story, and the experimenter "debriefed the participant" at the end (§5.2, p. 363). The authors say nothing about motion cues misleading observers.
- **Elsewhere in this run.** Schulz, Torresen and Herstad (2019) say this paper "used the animation principles of Arcs, Anticipation, and Slow in and Slow out for Assistive Free Flying robots" (p. 12:6; checked this run against the Schulz text layer), which matches locus 5. Szafir et al.'s list of eleven principles (locus 4) agrees with `../MODEL.md` §1.6's flag that Lasseter numbers eleven, though Szafir et al. cite Krasner alongside Lasseter.
- **What I read.** The whole text, Tables 1–3, the captions of Figures 1–6, and the reference list.
