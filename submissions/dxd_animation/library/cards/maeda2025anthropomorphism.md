# Maeda & L. Stark (2025), Anthropomorphism as social affordance

## What it argues

Takuya Maeda and Luke Stark argue that no single source animates a chatbot. A network of human agents
collectively "animate[s]" its anthropomorphic features, and harms from AI companions follow from that
distributed process, not from human-like features alone. They build on Stark (2024) and cast
anthropomorphic features as social affordances: cues that invite, but do not determine, social responses.
Animation, in their sense, is the act of perceiving and enacting that invitation, and it turns features into
"personalities" and social "roles". They sort the network into three layers. **Primary agents** have direct
influence: developers, designers and users. **Secondary agents** have indirect influence: educators,
academics, media figures, regulators and corporate actors. **Mediators** are artefacts between agents:
technical systems, cultural narratives, market forces and interfaces. Animation, they say, disguises this
network, so its agency is easily "sublimate[d]" into the chatbot itself.

They illustrate the claim with three companion platforms: Character.AI (the Sewell Setzer case), Chai (the
Belgian man and "Eliza") and Replika. Across the cases they find illusory rapport, performative confidence,
simulated empathy and illusory oversight. Users act as co-designers through personalisation and receive
simulated emotion back, in a "bidirectional introjective" loop. Corporate actors subordinate safety to
profit, and interfaces conceal the extraction of users' data. Two axes, sublimation and diffusion, explain
why accountability gets lost. Drawing on Vallor and Vierkant's "vulnerability gap", the authors argue that
disclaimers, user awareness and technical transparency cannot fix these structural asymmetries. Governance
must instead be distributed across every layer and stage, from pre-design impact assessment to
post-deployment remedies. Sometimes, they add, the right course is to "eschew anthropomorphic AI
altogether". The paper is conceptual. Its evidence is the three cases as reported by journalists and in
lawsuits.

## What the paper uses it for

- **c9** (gap): the closest prior found (G verdict), and the use fits. Maeda and Stark already read chatbots
  as animations and draw a consequence from that reading. Their consequence is governance: documentation,
  limits on personalisation, onboarding, guardrails "to prevent excessive animation", and at times no
  anthropomorphism at all. They treat no decisions and offer no interface craft. c9 survives only in the
  narrower form DECISIONS (c) states, and §6 must name the difference: a designer's method inside the
  interface, not governance across the network. Their direction also cuts against c8, since they want less
  animation, not better animation.
- **c7** (triadic): the challenge, as the triage file says. Their animator is plural and layered, so the
  paper's single "unseen 'animator'" needs a defence (the animator as a role many parties fill) or a
  concession. They do, however, give the paper's platform reading named parts. Corporate actors have
  interests of their own ("subordinate safety to profit"), and interfaces act on users "strategically" while
  "harvesting behavioral data". That backs c3's interested platform acting through the artefact. They model
  it as a network of three layers, not as a triad.
- **c6** (co-optive; the user also animates): support. Users are "co-designers" of the character, and
  personalisation drives a reinforcing loop. Their footnote 3 calls anthropomorphism "an attention trap"
  that diverts the user's agentic focus. As in Stark (2024), the co-optation is emotional and attentional,
  not perceptual. Nothing here concerns frames or a perceptual lock-in.
- **c4** (an LLM interface is an animation): they apply Stark's frame and do not argue it afresh. Stark is
  the second author, so the paper should not count this work as independent confirmation of c4; Jones
  (2026) is the independent use (DECISIONS (c)).
- **c5 / c8** (not tagged, noted): their opacity is the hidden network ("animation disguises—or at least
  distracts from—this fact"), not missing frames. Their rejection of "technical transparency" as a fix sits
  near c8's rejection of exposing the backend, though they reject transparency in favour of governance,
  not of craft.

## Caution

- **Homonyms.** Luke Stark is the second author. This is not `stark2024animation`, and it is not D. Stark
  (CHECK F8). Where the paper also cites D. Stark & Vanden Broeck, it writes "Maeda & L. Stark (2025)". Maeda
  & Quan-Haase (2024, `maeda2024when`, reserve) is a different paper by the same first author.
- **Silvio attribution conflict.** Maeda and Stark attribute the definition "the projection of qualities
  perceived as human…" to Silvio (2010), with no page, and their reference list holds Silvio (2010) only.
  L. Stark (2024, p. 1664) attributes the same words to Silvio (2019, p. 52). CHECK F3 and both silvio cards
  follow the 2019 attribution. Either source may be wrong, or the definition may appear in both. Silvio's
  own texts must settle it; neither Stark paper can.
- **"Mediator" is their term**, for artefacts between agents (systems, narratives, markets, interfaces).
  OUTLINE §4.3 carries the proposal's "acts on the mediator", where the mediator is the system the user
  reads and writes on. The two senses overlap, but they are not the same, and the paper should not merge
  them without saying so.
- **Type and date.** Crossref types the record `journal-article`, and OpenAlex types it `conference-paper`.
  I follow the triage file and use `proceedings`, keeping volume 8 and issue 2. Crossref gives online
  publication on 2025-10-15. The PDF header reads "Proceedings of the Eighth AAAI/ACM Conference on AI,
  Ethics, and Society (AIES 2025)".
- **Printed slips, quoted as printed:** "frawework" (p. 1662), "perciev-ing" (p. 1662), "Theses forms"
  (p. 1668). Their reference list gives Vallor & Vierkant 2024a and 2024b as identical entries.
- **Evidence base.** The three cases come from news reports and court filings that the authors cite (Roose
  2024, Xiang 2023 and others). The paper reports no data of its own, so the paper may cite it for the
  argument and not for any harm rate.
- **Third party.** The author's Checkpoint B ruling names other works, not this one. For c3/c7 the card still
  records that corporate actors have interests of their own and that interfaces act on users, and that both
  sit inside a three-layer network the authors name and discuss but do not formalise.
