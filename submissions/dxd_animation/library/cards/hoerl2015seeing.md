# Hoerl (2015), Seeing motion and apparent motion

## What it argues

Christoph Hoerl argues that philosophers have misdescribed what apparent motion looks like, and that the
mistake hides a direct-realist account of it. In the classic experiment, two brief stationary flashes appear
one after the other at two places, and viewers "often report an illusory impression of movement between A and
C" (EV p. 1). Rick Grush, an intentionalist, and Barry Dainton, an extensionalist, explain that report in
rival ways (in Dennett's terms, "Orwellian" and "Stalinesque"). Both assume that the visual system builds
something that is not on the screen, and so both rule out direct realism (§§1–2).

Hoerl's §3 goes back to Wertheimer. Wertheimer's "pure motion" cases give an impression of movement while it is
introspectively obvious that only two stationary stimuli are seen, so perception cannot be filling in the
positions between them, what Wertheimer called subjective supplementation. Hoerl then borrows Michael Martin's
comparative reading of "looks" statements: the display looks like movement because its spacing and timing
resemble paradigm cases of visible movement. On this reading apparent motion shows a "psychological
relativity" (EV p. 12), not a psychological construction, and the viewer experiences nothing beyond the two
flashes.

§4 separates the cinema from this. Hoerl denies that Wertheimer's apparent motion explains motion at the
cinema, for two reasons: paradigm film motion offers no visible gap for perception to fill, and it may not be
an illusion at all. With Gregory Currie, and against Kania (2002), he treats it as the real movement of
"cinematic images" (EV p. 14), images that persist on the screen and change place. Kania objects that slowing
the projector reveals static images and dark intervals. Hoerl answers with Goodman's (1977) notion of a
dissective property, one that every part of a thing shares. Colour is spatially non-dissective: Mark
Kalderon's pointillist painting looks green, yet every dot is yellow or blue. Pitch is temporally
non-dissective: a train of clicks at 100 Hz has a pitch that no single click has. If cinematic motion is
temporally non-dissective, slowing the projector shows parts that lack the motion, and the motion itself
becomes invisible; that does not show that the motion seen at normal speed was illusory. Hoerl grants that
ordinary motion is dissective and that slowing the projector does establish that cinematic motion lacks
continuity. Continuity, he replies, follows from the billiard ball's continuous existence, not from motion as
such. The question that remains is ontological: whether to admit "re-identifiable spatial particulars other
than physical objects" (EV p. 15).

§5 keeps that realism from swallowing apparent motion as well. Hoerl reviews the evidence for the
"indistinguishability claim" (EV p. 17), that apparent motion can look just like real motion, and finds it
weak: Wertheimer's own report, Boring's reliance on Dimmick and Scahill, and De Silva's results all fall short.
Kaplan and Sperling's subjects, once they had seen multi-stimulus displays, "virtually never used a rating of
more than one" for two-flash displays (EV p. 18, Hoerl quoting Sperling et al. 1985). What subjects mean,
Hoerl suggests, is that they cannot shake off the impression of movement. His conclusion (§6) is a two-way
split. In apparent motion only stationary spots are seen, though the display may look as if something moves.
In cinematic motion, moving images are seen, and they really move.

Two notes touch film craft. Note 21 ties the editor's "bad cut" to apparent motion produced by accident across
a cut, and says such experiences differ from ordinary motion experience in the cinema and detract from it.
Note 29 reports Kolers's (1972) finding that adding flashes between the two end points first weakened the
impression of motion, which recovered only once 32 flashes had been added. The article never mentions
animation, persistence of vision, interfaces or decisions.

## What the paper uses it for

- **m1** (opaque row; the paper may argue that exposing an AI system's internals, "showing the frames", does
  not show the user what they need). Hoerl supports a narrower, perceptual version of that claim. Slowing the
  projector shows the static images and the dark gaps (locus 6), and in doing so makes the motion invisible
  (locus 10); each speed shows aspects of the screen that the other hides (locus 9, and the pitch case, locus
  8). As an analogy: showing the frames shows the parts, and the property the viewer was seeing, which no part
  has, drops out. Two limits apply. Hoerl concedes that slowing the projector establishes a real fact, that
  cinematic motion lacks continuity (locus 11), so "show the frames" is not empty. And his question is whether
  an appearance is illusory, not what a viewer needs for a task. Carrying non-dissectivity from screen motion
  to an AI system's output is the paper's analogy, not Hoerl's claim. Loci 1 and 13 add a separate point: in
  Wertheimer's displays the impression of movement survives the viewer's knowing that nothing moves. That bears
  on whether disclosure undoes a reading, but it concerns apparent motion, which Hoerl keeps apart from cinema.
- **m4** (transfer warrant through an account of the receiver). Hoerl supplies part of a receiver account at
  the level of perception: he explains why a film viewer sees real motion and why that is not Wertheimer's
  apparent motion (loci 3, 12, 14). Note 21 ties one editing rule, avoid the bad cut, to that account (locus
  15), which is the shape m4 needs: a craft rule grounded in what the receiver experiences. Hoerl says nothing
  about craft transfer, interfaces, or what a receiver does with what they see, so he backs the receiver
  account for film motion and nothing further.
- **c6** (run 1; the viewer "locks in" and "the perceptual gaps disappear", `../SOURCE.md` Pass 2). The full
  text confirms the run-1 contrast and sharpens it. Hoerl denies that apparent motion explains cinema (locus 3),
  denies that perception supplies the missing positions even in Wertheimer's displays (locus 2), and finds no
  visible gap in paradigm film motion for perception to fill (locus 4). Pass 2's "enlisting the viewer's own
  perception to supply continuity where none exists" therefore finds no support here: on Hoerl's account the
  viewer supplies nothing, and the motion is on the screen. The nearest point to "locks in" is that the
  impression persists despite knowledge (loci 1 and 13), and that point holds for apparent motion only.

10. "The fact that cinematic motion becomes invisible when we slow down the projector does nothing to show that the appearance of it at normal speed must be illusory." EV p. 15 (PDF p. 16), §4.

  the motion is no illusion at all (loci 10, 12, 14). A sentence saying the screen fools or tricks the viewer
  cannot cite Hoerl.
- **"No frame has the motion; the sequence does."** The first half paraphrases Hoerl's non-dissectivity fairly,
  though in §4 he speaks of "individual static images" (locus 6) and uses "frames" only in note 21. The second
  half is not his. Hoerl puts the motion in cinematic images, re-identifiable particulars that persist and move
  (EV pp. 14–15; locus 14); he never says that a sequence moves or has the motion. The sibling card's c7 bullet
  writes "no frame has it, and the sequence does"; I found no such wording in the text. §4 also states the
  non-dissective reply first as what "the realist about cinematic motion may argue" (EV p. 15); §5 then adopts
  it as Hoerl's own view (locus 12).
- **What cuts against the planned m1/m4 use.** (a) Hoerl grants that slowing the projector reveals a real fact,
  the discontinuity (locus 11), so he supports "showing the frames hides the motion", not "showing the frames
  reveals nothing". (b) His realism makes the normal-speed view the one that shows something real; an argument
  in which the frames are the hidden truth and the moving image a cover runs against him. (c) He writes about
  perceiving motion in film projection and laboratory displays; nothing in the text concerns comprehension,
  tasks, interfaces or AI. (d) He separates cinema from apparent motion, so the paper cannot cite apparent
  motion and Hoerl for the same lock-in claim.
- **More frames.** Hoerl reports two results on adding intermediate stimuli. In Kolers's study (locus 16), added

  cases of cinematic motion". Hoerl uses both results to separate two-stimulus from multi-stimulus displays,
  not to say anything about revealing frames. Cite Kolers (1972) and Sperling et al. (1985) only after reading
  them.
