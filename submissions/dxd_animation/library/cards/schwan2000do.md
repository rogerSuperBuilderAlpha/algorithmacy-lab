## What it argues

Stephan Schwan, Barbel Garsoffky and Friedrich Hesse ask whether film cuts help viewers break a continuous activity into units, as the event boundaries ('breakpoints') in the activity itself do. Forty viewers watched about 15 minutes of an activity (upgrading a computer or cleaning a pistol). One film version placed cuts at breakpoints and the other at points between them. Viewers pressed a button to mark segments, then wrote down what they remembered (pp. 214–218).

Segmentation followed the breakpoints and 'was largely unaffected by the occurrence of cuts' (abstract). Overall recall was the same in both versions. The one effect of cuts was local: where a breakpoint came with a cut, viewers recalled more actions from that stretch (abstract; pp. 219–221). The authors read this as support for an 'accentuation' view in which cuts sharpen a boundary that the activity already has. They conclude that cuts should not be treated as a content-independent device and work mainly by being coupled to the structure of the content (p. 222).

## What the paper uses it for

- c6 (animation is co-optive: the viewer locks in; the user also animates): the triage used it for the claim that cuts placed at event boundaries help viewers organize activity. That is half right and the card should say which half. Cuts at breakpoints raised local recall; cuts did not drive how viewers segmented, and they did not change overall recall (pp. 219–222). What the source supports is that viewers organize the flow by the events they see, with the cut as an accent. It does not show that a cut makes the viewer's organizing easier in general. The study uses live-action video of one actor, not animation or interfaces, so the link to the viewer who also animates is the paper's own step.
