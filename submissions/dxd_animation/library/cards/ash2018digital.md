## What it argues
Abstract card. The full text was not read.

Ash, Anderson, Gordon and Langley use High-Cost Short-Term Credit products reached through digital interfaces to study interface design and "digitally mediated power". From interviews with credit-website designers and users, they show that these interfaces are designed and tested to manage "frictions": practical, affective or emotional contestations that interrupt or stop users from applying and entering debt. They suggest that interface design manages friction "by guiding action in such a way to minimise negative affective states at key thresholds of the application process", through data-driven design in which "the contingency of human response is engineered through analytics in order to increase rates of application". They complicate the notion of control as "a smooth or automatic operation of power", stressing that "both continuity and discontinuity" modulate action, and offer a vocabulary of friction, thresholds and transitions.

## What the paper uses it for
- c1 (contrast). The credit interfaces are conversion-era design in the plain sense: the goal is the application, and design is tuned to raise its rate. This reading is the paper's; the abstract says nothing about moving goals.
- c8 (contrast, partly support). The paper rejects hiding frames to produce a seamless experience. The authors say control works through continuity and discontinuity together, not smoothness alone, which gives §5 a critical vocabulary (friction, threshold, transition). The authors write about credit websites, not animation or LLM interfaces, and their "transition" is not the animator's sense.
