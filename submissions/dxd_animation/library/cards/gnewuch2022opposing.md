## What it argues

Gnewuch, Morana, Adam and Maedche ask why earlier studies disagree about chatbot response time. Some found that instant answers make a chatbot seem unhuman-like. Others found that delayed answers are judged less positively (Abstract, p. 773). The authors draw on social response theory and expectancy violations theory. They predict that a user's prior chatbot experience decides which effect appears. In a between-subjects lab experiment (N = 202) they compare a chatbot that answered "instantly" (about 200 to 400 ms) with one that answered after a delay of 2.3 s on average (§4.1, p. 779). The task was to find a cheaper mobile phone plan. A delayed response raised social presence for novice users and lowered it for experienced users. Social presence mediated usage intention, and prior experience moderated that mediation (§5.4, pp. 781–784). The authors call response time a "chronemic cue": a time-related social cue that users rarely notice. They conclude that artificial delays can backfire, that response time is a feature designers can set deliberately, and that one design for all users is a weak approach (§6, pp. 784–786). They flag that they did not study how users made sense of the delay (§6.3, p. 787).

## What the paper uses it for

- **c5** (animation is opaque: missing frames; the interstices are the craft): Gnewuch et al. show, with a controlled experiment, that the time between a user's message and the reply is a designable cue that changes how the user perceives the system. They say chronemic cues are "often overlooked due to their invisibility" (p. 785). That supports the paper's claim that the designer controls the interstices and that users do not see this control. It stretches the source in two ways. First, the manipulation is a single wait before the whole message appears, not a streamed sequence of frames, a typing indicator or token pacing. Second, "missing frames" is the author's vocabulary, not the source's. The source cuts against any reading of delay as a uniform "craft" gain: the same delay helped novices and hurt experienced users.

## Caution

- Title. OUTLINE §4.1 and CITED_WORKS row 31 call this work "Typing delays in chatbots". That is the name of the lab's file, not the title. The printed title is "Opposing Effects of Response Time in Human–Chatbot Interaction", with the subtitle "The Moderating Role of Prior Experience" on its own line on p. 773. The card's `title` field omits the subtitle and the `apa` field adds it with a colon; the PDF prints the subtitle without a colon. The paper does not study typing indicators or "typing delays" in the sense of a typing animation. Its delay is a wait before the reply arrives. I left OUTLINE and CITED_WORKS alone.
- The delayed condition mixed two kinds of delay: a static delay and a dynamic delay scaled to message length. The authors report that perceptions did not differ between the two and refer to Online Appendix A for the analysis (p. 779). The online appendices (A and B) are not in the held copy, so I did not see that analysis.
- The longest average delay was 2.3 s, and the authors say so (p. 787). The paper cannot speak to waits near 9 s or 20 s; Tan et al. (2026), another card in this batch, tests those.
- The sample is students at one European university. The authors list this and the dichotomous novice/experienced split as limitations (pp. 786–787).
- Read depth: I read the whole article (pp. 773–788) and skimmed the reference list.
