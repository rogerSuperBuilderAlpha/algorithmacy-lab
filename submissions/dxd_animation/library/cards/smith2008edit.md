## What it argues

Tim Smith and John Henderson ask whether viewers fail to notice film edits, a case they call 'edit blindness' and treat as an extreme form of change blindness. Film editors have long assumed that the Continuity Editing Rules hide cuts, and the authors say nobody had tested that directly (pp. 1–2). Seven participants watched seven five-minute excerpts from feature films, pressed a button whenever they saw an edit, and had their eye movements recorded (pp. 4–5).

Overall 15.8% of edits went undetected (p. 7). Among cuts to similar close shots, cuts that kept scene continuity were missed 25.1% of the time and cuts that kept scene and action continuity 32.4%, against 9.4% for cuts with no continuity (pp. 7–8). Gaze-match cuts, which the editors' lore also predicts, were not missed more often (10.9%, p. 8). Blinks and saccades explained little of the missing: the authors say most misses seem due to inattentional blindness, as viewers attend to the narrative (abstract; p. 11). They also say viewers keep some representation of the scene, because 70–90% of cuts were detected (p. 13), and they call the result the first empirical evidence of edit blindness (p. 13).

## What the paper uses it for

- c6 (animation is co-optive: the viewer locks in; the user also animates): Smith and Henderson give measured evidence that viewers absorbed in a narrative miss a global change on screen, so the viewer's own attention helps hide the join between frames or shots. The source cuts the other way in two places. The participants were told to find the cuts, so the 9–32% miss rate is a floor on what attention does under instruction (p. 11). And the study says nothing about a viewer who also produces the display, so "the user also animates" is the paper's step, not the source's. The source is about live-action film cuts, not animation or interfaces.
