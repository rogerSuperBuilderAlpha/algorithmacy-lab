## What it argues

Partial read. I read every page of the article body (abstract, introduction, method, results, discussion, limitations, conclusion, pp. 159–179), the reference list from its start through the letter E entries (to p. 180), and Appendix A (the coding scheme, pp. 184–185). I did not read the rest of the reference list (pp. 180–184), Appendix B (the channel scatter plot and correlations, p. 186), or the figures' contents beyond their captions.

Jachimowicz, Duncan, Weber and Johnson meta-analyse studies that compare an opt-out default with an opt-in condition. They pool 58 datasets from 55 studies in 35 articles (n = 73,675; p. 163) and find a medium-sized average effect, d = 0.68 (a three-level model gives d+ = 0.63; p. 164). Defaults raise uptake by 27.24 percentage points on average in the binary-choice studies (p. 165). The effect varies a great deal. Forty-six observations show a significant positive effect, ten show none, and two show a significant negative effect (p. 166); heterogeneity is I2 = 98.21% (p. 168).

To explain the spread, they test study features and three psychological channels from Dinner et al. (2011): endorsement, ease and endowment. Consumer domains show larger effects and environmental domains smaller ones (p. 170). Two coders rated each study's design for how far it triggers each channel. Endorsement and endowment predict larger effects; ease does not (p. 174). Endorsement means the decision-maker reads the default as showing what the choice architect thinks they should do (abstract, p. 159; Appendix A, p. 185). The authors warn that the channel codes are noisy, since few studies measured the mechanisms (p. 176). They also name preference strength as an untested moderator: defaults "may be less likely to influence those who have strong preferences" (p. 178).

## What the paper uses it for

- c1: the evidence behind OUTLINE §2's d = 0.68 and the endorsement channel, and the answer to CHECK F5, which asked for evidence that how options are presented shapes what people choose. It shows how large the conversion-era lever (a default set by a choice architect) is when the architect knows the target. Endorsement reads the default as the architect's advice about what the user should do. That presumes the architect holds a fixed target for the user, which is the assumption the paper says breaks when the goal moves.
- c1, where the use stretches the source: the authors do not discuss goal formation or moving goals. The inference from "endorsement presumes a fixed target" to "defaults presuppose a fixed goal" is the paper's own, and the card records it as such. The authors' own hint points the same way, mildly: they suggest defaults work less on people with strong, settled preferences (p. 178) and speculate that consumer preferences are held less strongly (p. 176). They state this as a hypothesis, not a finding, and the meta-analysis does not test it.
- c1, where the source cuts against the paper: the effect is large and robust enough that defaults will not disappear from design. The paper should say the effect is real, then argue about the target.

## Caution

- Count of studies. The abstract says "the 58 default studies (pooled n = 73,675)", and `../CHECK.md` row F5 repeats "58 studies (p. 159)". The method section says 58 datasets from 55 studies in 35 articles (p. 163; also Table 1 notes 58 observations, 55 studies). The card keeps the body figure. The paper should write "58 effect sizes from 55 studies" or follow the abstract's wording and accept the mismatch.
- d = 0.68 is the single-level random-effects estimate. The three-level model gives d+ = 0.63 (p. 164), and the trim-and-fill estimate is d+ = 0.80 (p. 166). The authors also say the Egger test is unreliable under high heterogeneity (p. 166). The paper should cite d = 0.68 with the 95% CI of 0.53 to 0.83 and say it varies.
- Endorsement is a coder's judgement from the study design. Interrater agreement was κ = 0.59 for endorsement (p. 173). Under robust variance estimation, endowment drops to marginal significance, and the authors say the other results hold (p. 174). Endorsement's effect is therefore supported but the authors call the measurement "noisy approximations" (p. 176).
- Presentation mode (online or not) was coded and showed no significant moderation (Table 1, p. 171; Table 2, p. 175). The meta-analysis therefore gives no separate evidence on digital interfaces. The paper should not cite it as a result about digital defaults.
- `../OUTLINE.md` §2 lists this work under the F2 facet entry, which read only the abstract, the introduction and the funnel section. This card reads the article body and appendix A from its own copy, so the OUTLINE's "part" depth stands for the facet entry and not for this card.
- Crossref records the authors in capitals (JACHIMOWICZ, DUNCAN, WEBER, JOHNSON) and the volume as 3, issue "02". The card follows the printed header: 3(2). The F2 facet key `jachimowicz2019defaults` is not a card key.
