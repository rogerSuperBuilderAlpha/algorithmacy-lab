# Bettman, Luce & Payne (1998), Constructive consumer choice processes

## What it argues

This card rests on the Crossref abstract only; I read no text of the article.

Bettman, Luce and Payne note that consumer decision making "has been a focal interest in consumer research".
They expect it to stay "critically important" given "current marketplace trends (e.g., technological change, an information
explosion)". Their thesis is short: "consumer choice is inherently constructive". The reason they give is
cognitive: "Due to limited processing capacity, consumers often do not have well-defined existing
preferences, but construct them using a variety of strategies contingent on task demands". The article first
describes constructive choice, consumer decision tasks and decision strategies. It then offers "an
integrative framework for understanding constructive choice", reviews the evidence against that framework,
and names gaps for further research.

## What the paper uses it for

- **c1** (a moving goal breaks conversion-era design): the abstract supports the premise that a shopper often
  arrives without a well-defined preference and builds one during the task. That fits the commuter who sets
  out for a raincoat. The stretch: the authors speak of preferences built among options, contingent on the
  task. The paper's claim is stronger, that the goal itself shifts (raincoat → umbrella). The abstract does
  not say goals move; the paper should mark that step as its own.
- **c2** (the user is human): the abstract ties construction to "limited processing capacity", a cognitive
  limit of the human decider. That supports c2's "cognitive". It says nothing about the biological or the
  emotional.
