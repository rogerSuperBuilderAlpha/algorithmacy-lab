## What it argues

Rodney Jones, a sociolinguist, argues that AI voice chatbots seem human because they are animated, and that animation is a social practice, not a property of the machine. He joins two ideas. Silvio's account of animation (liveliness projected onto non-human figures through "diagrams of personhood") and Blommaert and Varis's "enoughness" (a performance is authentic when audiences take it as socially recognisable, not when it is accurate). His data are social-media posts, news items and short transcripts about two products, ChatGPT's advanced voice mode and Sesame.ai's voice demo. He shows that OpenAI packages voices with named personalities, that users prompt the model into stereotyped accents and ratify them, and that Sesame's users help produce the chatbot's emotion in real time. He names many co-producers of animation: engineers, the people whose language made the training data, raters, designers, users, journalists and company spokespeople. His conclusion is that "anthropomorphism" is the wrong frame, that designers should ask which kinds of person a voice invokes, and that bias in AI language is made at the packaging and prompting stages as well as in training data. He cites Luke Stark's 2023 blog post "ChatGPT is Mickey Mouse" as making the same argument about large language models.

## What the paper uses it for

- **c4.** Jones applies Silvio's animation to AI chatbots, with designers named among the animators, so it is a second route to c4 beside Stark. It is not independent of Stark. Jones cites Stark's 2023 blog post and says it "makes the same argument about LLMs" (locus 6); the FAccT paper `stark2024animation` is not in his reference list. Jones studies voice interfaces and their social identity (accent, stance, personality), not text LLM interfaces and not decisions.
- **c6.** He says the viewer or user does half the work: users "fill in the blanks" and help to produce the persona (loci 3, 7, 8). This is the nearest match in the library to "the user also animates", but his user supplies social meaning, not a decision, and his examples (accent prompting, ratifying a bot's sadness) are about identity.
- **c9.** It supports the gap claim only in part. Jones does call chatbots "technologies of animation" and offers it as an alternative to anthropomorphism (locus 9), so the idea of an animation frame for AI interfaces already exists outside Stark. He draws design advice from it (locus 10), but about voice and social identity. Nothing in the text links animation to decision design. That makes the paper a near miss for c9, not a counter-example; one paper's silence is not evidence about the field.

## Caution

- Jones writes "Luke Stark (2023)" as the source and lists only the Daily Nous blog post. The triage note in `triage/G.md` calls this "a second, independent use of Silvio's animation" and says it shows "c4 does not rest on Stark alone". The text supports the first half (Jones reads Silvio directly and develops her ideas with Blommaert, Agha and Keane) but not full independence from Stark; the card records both.
- The same triage note says designers are "named among the co-producers". That is right (locus 8), but Jones names a plural field of co-producers, not a viewer, character and animator triad. c7 gets no support from this source, and the plural list is nearer to the multi-party reading than to a triad.
- Silvio is Terry Silvio (cited as Silvio, 2010, "Animation: The new performance?", *Journal of Linguistic Anthropology* 20(2), 422–438). Jones refers to her as "she"; the card follows his reference list for the 2010 date. The paper's own use of Silvio is in `../SOURCE.md`; this card does not check it.
- Volume 46 is the 2026 volume (Crossref and OpenAlex agree); Crossref gives the issue date as March 2026 and the online date as 10 July 2026.
- Scope: a single author, qualitative analysis of social-media artifacts and a few transcripts about two voice products. It makes no experimental claim about users' decisions.
