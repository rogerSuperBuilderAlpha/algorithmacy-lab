## What it argues

Johnson and eleven co-authors write a short review, a "roadmap", of the tools open to a "choice architect", meaning "anyone who present[s] people with choices" (Abstract; §Introduction). Their starting claim is that "there is no neutral architecture—any way a choice is presented will influence how the decision-maker chooses" (p. 488). Every presentation has a default, even if the default is no choice at all, so a parent offering bedtime options is as much an architect as a government (p. 488).

They sort the tools into two groups. Tools for structuring the choice task decide what to present: the number of alternatives, technology and decision aids, defaults, choice over time, and how task structure affects search (§1, pp. 488–493). Tools for describing the options decide how to present it: partitioning options and attributes, and designing attributes through parsimony, linearity, comparability, evaluability, translation and expansion (§2, pp. 494–496). A third part covers implementation: individual differences (numeracy, politics) and the gap between predicted and experienced outcomes (§3, pp. 496–498). A short fourth part applies the tools to environment, consumer finance and eating (§4, pp. 498–500).

The authors limit the claim. They "do not provide a theoretical account of why these tools affect choice, nor suggest a normative analysis" (p. 488). On ethics they say defaults raise "ethical risks" (p. 491) and "impinge upon liberty" when consumers do not know they have choices or when switching costs are high (p. 492). They end by proposing "full disclosure of decision design" as a possible routine duty (p. 500). On technology, they say decision aids "could be designed to steer consumers" toward individually or socially desirable choices (p. 491). They note that consumers "appear to show little resistance" when such influence benefits sellers (p. 491).

## What the paper uses it for

- **c1.** SOURCE.md (Pass 2, opening) cites Johnson et al. (2012) for "Choice architecture proves that how options are presented systematically shapes what people decide." The source supports the point that presentation shapes choice, and says "no neutral architecture" outright. It does not support "proves". It is a tool inventory that runs on cited studies and offers no test or theory of its own (p. 488). CHECK F5 and OUTLINE (Flags: F5) already flag the verb. The card confirms the flag from the full text. "Shows" or "reviews evidence that" fits the source. The meta-analytic evidence in the paper's design rests on `jachimowicz2019when`, not on this review.
- **c1, vocabulary.** The two-part sort (what to present, how to present it) gives the paper a plain way to name the conversion-era toolkit that a moving goal breaks. The source assumes a chooser with a fixed set of options and a designer who knows the preferable end (for example, setting the default to "the alternative most people prefer", p. 492). That assumption is the one c1 says no longer holds, so the source supplies the old design vocabulary and also shows its limit.
- Where the use stretches it: the source treats LLMs, conversation and animation not at all. It covers static or menu-style presentations in marketing and policy settings. Any link to LLM interfaces is the paper's, not the source's.
