## What it argues

Takayama, Dooley and Ju test whether animators' techniques can make a robot's actions "readable". Their abstract glosses readable as people being able to "figure out what the robot is doing, reasonably predict what the robot will do next, and ultimately interact with the robot in an effective way" (locus 1). The problem they start from is that a robot stands still while it plans and then moves suddenly, so even researchers mistake a "thinking" robot for an idle one (locus 2). From Thomas and Johnston they take anticipation and follow-through. From Lasseter they take the "thinking character", whose eyes or head lead the action (loci 4–6). They turn these into two cues. Forethought is expressive motion just before the task: a small move forward or back for engagement, a torso lift for confidence, and a look in the direction the robot will turn. Reaction is happiness or disappointment after success or failure. A co-author the paper describes as "a professional character animator" (the author list places Dooley at Pixar Animation Studios) animated a model of the PR2 robot in four tasks: opening a door, delivering a drink, ushering a person, and asking for help to plug in. The experiment was an online video study with 273 adults recruited through local mailing lists and Mechanical Turk, in a 2 (forethought vs. none, between) × 2 (reaction vs. none, between) × 2 (success vs. failure, within) design. The authors measured readability in two ways: whether a participant's open-ended description of what the robot was about to do contained one of two keywords for that task, and how sure the participant felt (1–7). They also collected seven adjective ratings. Forethought did not change keyword accuracy, F(1,271) = 0.65, p = .42 (locus 10). The task did: door 0.84, power 0.78, drink 0.64, ushering 0.29. Forethought did raise sureness (5.69 vs. 5.20), appeal (4.83 vs. 4.27) and approachability (5.05 vs. 4.54) (loci 11–12). Reaction raised a combined "smart" rating (competent plus intelligent; 4.72 vs. 3.86, F(1,267) = 28.12, p < .00001) and confidence (4.53 vs. 4.14, F(1,267) = 7.51, p < .007), whether the robot succeeded or failed. The authors read the results as support for using animation to improve how people perceive robots, and they propose that a robot acknowledge its failures to soften negative perceptions (locus 14).

## What the paper uses it for

- **m5.** This is the strongest evidence in this run for the dissociation, and it supports m5. The forethought cue left the correct reading of the robot's next action unchanged (locus 10) but made viewers surer of their readings and made the robot seem more appealing and approachable (loci 11–12). The reaction cue made a failing robot seem smarter: the authors say it "can make the robot seem to be more intelligent/capable, even if it fails to achieve the functional task at hand" (§6, PDF p. 8). Craft raised confidence and attribution without improving the reading, which is the split m5 names. There are two limits. The outcome is a viewer's reading of a robot's action, not a decision. And the authors present these effects as benefits, not risks: they recommend that a robot "acknowledge its failures" to "mitigate the negative perceptions" (locus 14). Cite the null result and the positive effects together. Do not cite the paper as showing that anticipation makes intent more readable.
- **m3.** It supports the technique, not its effect. The authors build forethought from anticipation: Lasseter's eyes or head leading the action (locus 6), and expressive movement "just an instant before" the task (locus 8). They frame readability as predicting "what the robot will do next" (locus 1). But the cue did not improve correct prediction (locus 10); it raised sureness only. The cue also signals the robot's next move, not a change in rules. m3's carry-over, a cue placed before a change in governance, is an analogy this source does not test.
- **m1.** It supports staging indirectly and complicates it. The authors explain the null forethought effect by staging: "each animation was staged very clearly", so viewers could read the setting (the door) rather than the robot's cue (locus 13). Keyword accuracy varied far more by task (0.29 to 0.84) than by cue. That fits m1's weight on staging, but it is a post hoc explanation, not a test. Their "sureness" measure is confidence in one reading, not idempotence. Viewers could also watch clips repeatedly, and the authors say a single viewing would be better (§5.2, PDF p. 7).
- **m4.** Partly. The study tests how viewers actually read the robot, which is the receiver-side check m4 asks for, and the authors note "other unwritten animation principles and techniques" for showing thought (§6, PDF p. 8). But they apply the principles directly and offer no account of the viewer beyond their measures. The animator also departed from the robot's physical limits: "not all of the kinematic constraints were maintained, particularly the speed of the torso lift" (§2.1, PDF p. 3).
- **m2.** Thin. The authors want robot actions readable so that people can coordinate with the robot and see "robot intent" (locus 7). That is the robot making its own intent legible to the people around it. The model's m2 concerns a person specifying intent so that others can trace a result to it. The source says nothing about the person's side.

## Caution

- **How the authors define "readability".** They give no formal definition. The term has three glosses in the text: the abstract's (locus 1); the operational one, keyword match plus sureness (locus 9 and §3); and the conclusion's, "how quickly people can 'read' the robot, accurately predicting what the robot will do next" (§6, PDF p. 8; inner double quotes rendered as single). Their hypothesis H1 also counts liking ratings as readability evidence: forethought "will improve a robot’s readability as demonstrated through (a) written interpretations of the robot actions and sureness about those interpretations and (b) people’s subjective ratings of the robot" (§2, PDF p. 2). So "support for H1" in their discussion includes appeal and approachability, which are not readings. On accuracy, they report "mixed support for Hypothesis 1a" (§5, PDF p. 7).
- **Attribution of the principles.** The authors credit anticipation and follow-through to Thomas and Johnston, reference [21] (*The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation*, Hyperion, 1981). They credit anticipation as the key to readable action and the "thinking character" to Lasseter, reference [15] (SIGGRAPH 1987, pp. 35–44). Both are named in the text (loci 4–5). Lasseter's own words appear only as the authors quote him; I did not check those quotations against Lasseter. [Verifier, S6] Checked against the Lasseter (1987) PDF held for lasseter1987principles (sha256 b99cde55...): none of the three passages the authors quote from [15] occurs in it ("If a character were to move about in a series of unrelated actions…", "In creating a 'thinking character,'…", and the mouse-trap cheese passage). The nearest text there is "Without a thought process, the actions of a character are just a series of unrelated motions" (Lasseter, p. 43). Lasseter's "the eyes will usually lead the head in an action" sits in his §2.5 on follow through and overlapping action, citing Graham [12] (p. 39), not in his account of anticipation. The quotations may come from another Lasseter text; this card cannot say which.
- **Statistics as printed.** The authors give no effect sizes, only F, p, M and SE. The denominator degrees of freedom vary across tests on 273 respondents (255, 262, 265, 267, 271), and the paper does not explain the missing responses. The ratings use a Bonferroni cut-off of .0071 (= .05/7), and the reaction effect on confidence (p < .007) only just clears it. The no-reaction mean for "smart" (M = 3.86, SE = 0.07) is printed identically to the failure mean in the paragraph before; I report both as printed.
- **Design limits the authors state.** The clips were animations, not a physical robot. Forethought combined engagement, confidence and timing cues, so the study cannot say which element did the work ("we used a combination of dimensions rather than isolated ones", §5.2, PDF p. 7). The tasks differed in how much the setting gave away (the door).
- **Ethics.** The authors make no statement about deception, over-attribution or ethical risk. Respondents saw a debriefing paragraph (§2.3.2). Making a failing robot seem smarter is offered as a design benefit (locus 14; §6).
- **Elsewhere in this run.** Schulz, Torresen and Herstad (2019) summarise this study as making it "easier to understand" what the robot was doing (pp. 12:9–12:10) and as showing that forethought "increased its appeal" (p. 12:13). The second matches this paper. The first overstates it on accuracy (see schulz2019animation, Caution).
- **What I read.** The whole text, including Figures 4–7 captions and the reference list. The reading-order layer garbles the results pages around the figures, so I read PDF p. 6 in the layout `.txt` as well.
