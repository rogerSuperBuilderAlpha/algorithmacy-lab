## What it argues

Cameron and five co-authors (Sheffield, Northeastern, Cranfield) write about trust in human–robot interaction (HRI). They argue that the usual two-party model, user and robot, misses something: how far a user trusts a robot can depend on the user's relationship with, and opinion of, the person or organisation that deploys the robot. They call that party the Deployer and propose a Social Triad model of User, Robot and Deployer.

Their grounds are two. First, they borrow a three-way split from Clark and Fischer (their ref. 35), who say people see social robots as depictions and recognise three classes of agent: the character (shown in the robot), the audience (the users) and the authority (the often unseen people who deploy the robot). They add Vicente's five-level "Tech Ladder" (physical, psychological, team, organisational, political) to say where the Deployer acts. Second, they offer a case study across three robot experiments run with the same team and task: university volunteers, a non-unionised manufacturer, and a unionised aerospace firm. In the third, the study never started, because staff saw the firm's management as the sole Deployer and distrusted how it would use the data. They add a LEGO Serious Play focus group on care robots with eleven participants, where participants kept returning to who holds the data and the control.

Besides the User–Robot pathway the field already studies (which they call "Measured HRI"), they set out pathways from the Deployer to the Robot, from the Deployer to the User, and from the User and the Robot back to the Deployer (Fig. 1, pp. 1408–1409). They predict five effects, and list future work. They say the model "still remains to be formally tested."

### What the source counts as its third party

The author's Checkpoint B ruling asked for this record. I report what the paper says and take no side.

- **Does the Deployer have interests of its own?** Yes, as the paper describes it, but the interests are general and vary by case. The Deployer is "an interested external agent (such as a researcher, manager or corporation)" (p. 1408). The paper says there "will be a corresponding variety of motivations" (p. 1407), gives examples (a firm wanting to cut safety risk or to raise throughput, p. 1414; management monitoring staff, p. 1412), and holds that users may suspect the Deployer's motives (p. 1407, p. 1410). It does not name a single typical interest and it does not model conflict between the Deployer's interests and the User's. The Deployer is also defined by the user's view: it is "the agent who the User views as being responsible for the robot" (p. 1408). In the case study the research team changed roles by showing independence, and trust shifted "without a change in the HRI scenario itself" (p. 1410).
- **Does it act through the artefact on the user?** Partly. Pathways run from the Deployer to the Robot (setting goals, owning, controlling) and to the User (instructions, framing, p. 1408). The paper also says a robot's synthetic social behaviours "perhaps should be considered as indirect expressions from the Deployer rather than the robot itself" (p. 1415), and that a robot can become "another means by which the Deployer … can monitor behaviour" (p. 1412). It says social behaviours could be "manipulative or coercive actions to constrain users within the Deployer's framework" if the Deployer is distrusted (p. 1415). These are offered as readings and predictions. The Deployer's power is argued, not measured (p. 1413).
- **Is it modelled, or only named?** Named and sketched. Fig. 1 (p. 1408) draws pathways of communication, and pp. 1408–1409 describe them. The paper says it "has not addressed means by which to formally record and measure the relationship between Deployer and User, nor the influence of the Deployer on HRI" (p. 1414), and the evidence is a case study, not a test, plus a focus group it calls "too small to expect thematic saturation" (p. 1412). The five predictions (pp. 1414–1415) are hypotheses for later studies.
- **Where the triad comes from.** From Clark and Fischer's character, audience and authority (p. 1407) and from Vicente's Tech Ladder (p. 1407). I found no mention of Simmel in the text layer, the reference list included.

## What the paper uses it for

- **c3 (contrast).** The paper says the platform environment is triadic. Cameron et al. already propose a user–robot–deployer triad for trust in HRI (OUTLINE §3.2 flag). The source supports the idea that an interested party sits behind the artefact and shapes how users trust it. It does not show the triad is worked out: it is a proposed model with a case study.
- **c7 (contrast).** The paper's triad is viewer, character, animator. Cameron et al.'s User, Robot, Deployer maps onto it loosely, and their Clark and Fischer source compares social robots to "ventriloquist dummies or puppets" (pp. 1406–1407), which is close to an animation frame. Their triad is a trust model, not a claim about how the character is made or timed, and it has no counterpart to the animator's craft with frames.
- **c9 (contrast; the threat).** The paper says no prior work frames LLM interfaces as animation for decision design. This card shows that prior work frames a robot as a depicted character with an unseen authority behind it, and it adds a deployer-as-third-party claim. It does not cover LLM interfaces, animation, or design for decisions. c9 survives in that narrower form only. The Clark and Fischer paper (cited here as printed: Clark HH, Fisher K (2022) Behav Brain Sci) is the work to check next for the depicted-character move. The author rules on c9 at Checkpoint D.

## Caution

- Not Lindsey D. Cameron (algorithmic management; `cameron2024`, `cameron2024making`, `cameron2022expanding`). The byline here reads David Cameron, University of Sheffield (Information School). Crossref lists "David Cameron"; the triage file says OpenAlex gives "David S. Cameron". The article prints no middle initial except in the e-mail address.
- Year: online 13 September 2023, volume 16(6) in 2024. The card takes the volume year 2024 and sets `registered_year: 2023`.
- The Deployer is a trust variable here, not a design principle. The paper's concern is how researchers measure trust in HRI and how the Deployer biases those measures. It does not say designers should use the triad.
- The OUTLINE §3.2 sentence "Cameron et al. (2024, Social Triad Model) already propose a user–robot–deployer triad" is accurate as a description of the proposal. The author's remark at Checkpoint B ("Their triad isn't a real triad and certainly isn't as well examined") is the author's view and is not written into "What it argues." What the text supports on that point is in the four bullets above.
- Clark and Fischer is cited in the reference list as "Clark HH, Fisher K (2022) Social robots as depictions of social agents. Behav Brain Sci 33:e21". I report it as printed and did not check it. The author spelling and the volume and year pairing should be checked at source before it enters a reference list.
- The case study draws on the Online Appendix for outcome comparisons (footnote 3, p. 1409); I did not read it.
