## What it argues

Simone Natale argues that software has a discursive life as well as a material one: people make sense of programs through narratives, and the narratives shape governance and design. Because software is opaque, he says, it is 'prone to being translated into a plurality of narratives' (abstract). He proposes 'biographies of media', the bodies of narratives that follow a medium across its life, and tests the idea on Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA.

Two narratives grew around ELIZA. In the first, Weizenbaum built the program to show that machine intelligence is an illusion. He treated deception as the measure of success, gave ELIZA a script (the Rogerian therapist) so that it played a role, and likened it to an actress with 'nothing of her own to say' (pp. 14–15 of the PDF). In the second, which Weizenbaum did not foresee, readers took the same program as proof that computers think. Natale says Weizenbaum could not control or 'program' the narratives that grew from it (p. 17), and that the programme also worked as 'a narrative about conversational programs' (p. 15). Natale cites Turkle's reading that users sustain the illusion out of their own wish to 'breathe life into a machine' (p. 16). He concludes that narratives about software orient action (p. 23).

## What the paper uses it for

- c4 (an LLM interface is an animation; L. Stark; Silvio): Natale describes a chatbot as a performance, with a script, a role and a stage metaphor that Weizenbaum himself chose. The paper can cite this as an early, well-documented case of a conversational interface read as a character someone else voices. It stretches the source to call this animation. Natale never mentions animation; the theatre metaphor is Weizenbaum's and Natale's frame is narrative and deception.
- c6 (the viewer locks in; the user also animates): the quoted Weizenbaum passage that the sense and continuity 'is supplied largely by the person himself' (p. 15) fits the idea that the user completes the display. Natale reports it inside a story about deception, and he reports Turkle's alternative that users maintain the illusion 'because of their own desires to breathe life into a machine' (p. 16). The paper should take care not to turn a story about users being fooled into a claim about users co-creating.
