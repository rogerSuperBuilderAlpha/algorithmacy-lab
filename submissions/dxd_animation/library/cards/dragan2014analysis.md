## What it argues

Anca Dragan, Rachel Holladay and Siddhartha Srinivasa turn robotics' work on clear communication around. Where that work asks how a robot can convey true information, they study "communicating false information, or hiding information altogether" (locus 1). Their object is goal-directed motion, a robot moving toward one of a few candidate goals such as one of two bottles on a table, and they define deceptive motion as "motion that tricks the observer into believing that the robot is not moving towards its actual goal" (locus 2). They justify the topic by its uses (military applications, more engaging games, and "tricking patients into exerting more force during physical therapy", §I, p. 1) and by a defensive one: a robot that can generate deception can measure its accidental leakage and avoid deceiving by mistake (locus 3).

Five studies follow. In Study 1, six novices and "a senior animation designer", whom the authors treat as the expert, drew deceptive paths for a 2D robot character in 11 scenarios (locus 6; §II-A, p. 2). The novices most often exaggerated the motion toward another goal (67% of cases; locus 7). The authors map this to decoying in Whaley's theory of deception, a way of showing the false; smaller groups switched between goals (14%, "dazzling") or kept the goal ambiguous (5%, "masking", hiding the real) (§II-B, pp. 2–3). The animator first aligned the robot with the decoy goal "to clearly indicate its (deceptive) selection", moved toward it, and broke for the real goal at the last moment (§II-B, p. 3). The authors' mathematical model then lets the robot "take the observer’s perspective" (§III, p. 3). It assumes the observer expects efficient motion, estimates which goal the observer would infer from the path so far, and picks a path that minimises the probability the observer gives the actual goal (§III, pp. 3–4). Each human strategy becomes a different objective, and exaggeration wins because "the observer will not only be wrong, but will be confidently wrong" (locus 8).

Study 2 tested the paths on 234 Mechanical Turk workers (240 recruited, six failed a control question), between subjects, with four trajectories crossed with three time points (§IV-A, pp. 4–5). Viewers watched a clip, predicted the goal, and rated their confidence on a 7-point scale, counted negative when the prediction was right. All three deceptive paths (the novice's, the animator's, the model's) were significantly more deceptive than the straight-line baseline, "with p < .0001 for all three contrasts" (§IV-B, p. 5). The model came close to the animator, marginally equivalent by an equivalence test (locus 9), and one novice's "hovering" path beat both near the end. Study 3 carried the model to the 7-DOF arm of a mobile manipulator and again found a large effect of trajectory with 120 workers, F(1, 117) = 150.81, p < .0001 (§V-A, p. 6). In Study 4, three of six people deceiving with their own arms exaggerated as the model did (§V-B, p. 7).

Study 5 asked what deception does to the interaction. Twelve local participants played a game in which they had to anticipate which of two bottles the robot would grab and steal it first; after two predictable practice rounds, the robot deceived once (§VI-A, p. 7). Seven took the deception as intentional and five did not; two of those five thought a programmer had hand-made the motion (locus 11). Ratings of the robot as an adversary rose and trust fell, and both survived Bonferroni correction; engagement rose at p = .032 but did not survive it, and intelligence did not change significantly (p = .11) (locus 12; §VI-B, p. 8). Some users trusted the robot less although it "played by the rules", because they now knew it could trick them (locus 13). The authors close by placing deception opposite legibility: "the easier it is to be legible, the more extra energy it takes to be deceptive" (locus 14).

## What the paper uses it for

- **m5 (support).** The source gives the danger half of the model's craft a measured case. The techniques that make a goal readable, such as exaggerated motion toward a goal and the animator's alignment move, are the techniques that mislead, and the most effective of them leaves the observer "confidently wrong" (locus 8). Study 2 measured that confidence directly (confidence in a false prediction, Fig. 6, p. 5). This is the pattern m5 warns about, confidence without accuracy, produced on purpose. A senior animation designer made deceptive motion readily, and the model matched him (loci 6, 9). Two limits apply. The person here is an observer guessing a goal, not a decider bound by an intermediary. And the authors present deception as a capability to build, not as a misuse to guard against (loci 3, 5).
- **m3 (support, by analogy).** The bottle game is the nearest case in this run of a decision made on a cue before a move: participants act on their reading of the robot's opening motion, and that motion was built to mislead them (§VI-A, p. 7). `../MODEL.md` holds back "the surprise gag: an anticipation that misleads" on row 3, citing Chang and Ungar. Dragan et al. never use that term and cite no animation source for it; they supply the empirical case, not the warning. Study 5 also bears on governance. Trust fell even though the robot kept to the rules of the game, once users learned it could trick them (locus 13), and the authors restate it: "Even though the robot plays by the rules, the users become aware of its capability to deceive" (§VII, p. 8). For row 3 this means a cue before a rule change is trusted only as far as the cuer is believed unable to mislead. The authors tested one deception and say so: "Our evaluation studies only test that users are deceived once" (§VII, p. 8).
- **m1 (support).** People read the same motion as coming from three different sources: the robot deceiving them on purpose (7 of 12), a programmer who hand-made it (2), or the mechanics of the arm (3), as in "it went in that direction because it had to stretch its arm out" (locus 11; §VI-B, p. 8). That is idempotent interpretation's problem, one output and several possible producers, in a dyad. The authors flag the same thing as a "pitfall": "some users rationalize the robot’s behavior, not believing that a robot would be capable of intentional deception" (§I, p. 2).
- **m4 (support).** The model generates deceptive motion from an explicit account of how an observer infers a goal, built on efficient-motion expectations and the observer model of the authors' legibility work [11] (§III, p. 3), and it performs almost as well as the animator (locus 9). A receiver account here reproduces the animator's craft. The account is of goal inference, not of decision competencies.
- **G2 (checked; does not fill the gap).** The paper derives motion from a model of the observer and uses an animator only as a benchmark. It selects no animation craft for decision design, and its one decision setting is a two-person game against the robot.

## Caution

- **No ethics section.** The paper has none, so it cannot be cited for one. Its statements on ethics are four. It "echoes concerns raised in prior work about the effects of deception [20]" and answers at once that "deception is human nature", adding that "even the Turing Test is about deception" (locus 5; §I, p. 2). It cites the "Literature on the ethics of deception" only to motivate trust as a dependent measure (locus 10). It lists uses (military, games, physical therapy) without saying when deception is acceptable. And it offers the leakage argument, that a robot able to deceive can avoid deceiving by accident (locus 3). It says nothing about consent, disclosure or limits, and the authors call themselves "excited to contribute to a better understanding of this behavior" (§I, p. 2). Cite the paper for the measured effects, not for an ethical position. The "surprise gag" warning in `../MODEL.md` is Chang and Ungar's, not theirs.
- **How the authors define legibility.** This paper never defines it. It describes effective communication as clearly conveying "truthful information" and deception as its counterpart (§I, p. 1), sets deception against "clear, intent-expressive communication", and uses "legible" once (locus 14). It takes its observer model from Dragan, Lee and Srinivasa (2013, [11]) and its goal-inference formula from Dragan and Srinivasa (2012, [12]) (§III, p. 3). Legibility here is a robot's motion letting an observer infer its true goal. That is the robotics sense, a homonym of the legibility the author's ruling sets aside, a user reading the algorithm (`../MODEL.md` §3; see dragan2013legibility).
- **The Discussion overstates Study 5.** It says deception "can increase ratings of engagement, intelligence, and adversarial standing" (§VII, p. 8). Intelligence was not significant (p = .11), and with Bonferroni correction "only adversary and trust remain significant" (§VI-B, p. 8). The split by perceived intention leaves samples so small that the authors call those findings "very preliminary" (§VI-B, p. 8). Twelve participants, one deception, one game.
- **Printed inconsistency.** Exaggeration is "67% of the cases" in §II-B (p. 2) and "69% the user demonstrations" in §IV (p. 4).
- **Animation.** The paper names no animation principle and cites no animation text. Its link to animation is the "senior animation designer" in Study 1 and the motion-communication work it cites in §I, including Takayama et al. [30] and Gielniak and Thomaz [18] (§I, p. 1; references). The F12 placement rests on that.
- **What I read.** The whole text, the captions of Figures 1–10, and the reference list. The equations in §III come through the text layers garbled; I read them for structure only and quote none.
