## F1 Animation theory and the animator's craft (c4, c5, c7, c8)

No lab library holds an animation-theory card except this library's own `stark2024animation`, and the five
dxd_animator cards this facet upgrades were all written from abstract and catalogue record. So F1 starts almost
empty, and most of what it needs is closed: Silvio's article and book, Ngai, Bukatman and Furniss are books or
paywalled, and the author holds no books (Checkpoint A, ruling 4). Those become metadata or abstract cards and
go to `BROWSER_LIST.md`. Three gaps remain. First, the McLaren source: Sifianos (1995) still has no record on
Crossref or OpenAlex, so under the S3 rule it cannot be shortlisted, and §4.1's McLaren line has no confirmed
source (reserve R1). Second, F1 has no open text of animation theory that a card can quote; Cholodenko (2014)
and Ruddell (2008) are open and fill it, the second on limited animation, where `lamarre2009anime` is a closed
book. Third, F1 has no contrast. Maeda and L. Stark (2025) supply one: they describe "a network of distributed
human agents that collectively 'animate'" a chatbot (abstract), which strains §4.3's single "unseen
animator".

## F2 Apparent motion and film perception (c6)

F2 has three dxd_animator cards to upgrade and pointers in `spaces` for event boundaries (`zacks2009`,
`ongchoco2023`, `pitts2023` and others), but no card reads Wertheimer, the film-perception literature or the
persistence-of-vision dispute. CHECK F12 tells the author to call the lock-in "apparent motion", after
Wertheimer, and §4.2 carries the whole Perception section on that sentence. The searches show the sentence is
itself disputed. Steinman, Pizlo and Pizlo (2000) title their paper "Phi is not beta", which separates
Wertheimer's phenomenon from the motion seen in film. Hoerl (2015) argues against the claim "that apparent motion is the
psychological phenomenon that underlies motion experience in the cinema" (abstract). The shortlist carries both
as contrast, so the paper can say which apparent motion it means. For (b), Magliano and Zacks (2011) replace
Cutting (2005), whose chapter still has no record: they tie continuity editing directly to event segmentation.
