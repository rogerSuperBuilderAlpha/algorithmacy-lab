**Title:** Governed Without a Seat: Platform Co-optation and the Governance Work of the Governed

## Abstract
Economic sociology classifies governance by the relation it creates between parties. Hierarchies coordinate by authority, markets by price and contract, and networks by reciprocity and reputation. Platforms have strained this classification. One response treats them as a new form. Stark and Vanden Broeck put it in a verb: actors in hierarchies command, in markets contract and in networks collaborate, while on platforms they are co-opted. Vallas and Schor offer another image, the platform as a *permissive potentate* that holds concentrated power while ceding control over much of the work. This paper takes a third position. Platform co-optation is not a new logic of coordination. It is a governance arrangement with a specific problem, and that problem shifts the work of governance onto the people being governed.

The arrangement has four features. First, it is three-sided. The operator stands between two outside parties, such as a seller and a buyer, a hotel and its guests, or an app developer and its users, and employs neither. Second, evaluation passes between the parties. The operator enrols each side in rating the other, then fuses those ratings with its own ranking rule into a verdict that binds the rated side. Hotel accreditation shows the scale of the change. Anonymous reviews, aggregated by proprietary algorithms into rankings, displaced inspection-based accreditation such as the Michelin Guides and the AA star system (Orlikowski and Scott, 2023). Third, the mediator is opaque and not neutral. It is a party to the exchange it judges, and its rule is neither published nor stable. Fourth, the governed have no seat and a costly exit. Selznick's co-optation absorbed outsiders into the leadership of an organization in order to secure its stability. Platform co-optation reverses the direction: it projects evaluative authority over outsiders who have no part in setting the rule. Leaving is expensive because ratings earned on one platform do not transfer to another.

Each classical form gives the parties something to govern with. Employees can appeal to authority, contracting parties can bargain over price, and network partners can draw on reciprocity. Platform co-optation offers none of these. In Stark and Vanden Broeck's terms, the operator leverages the parties' agency rather than delegating to them, and accountability becomes distributed, deflected and denied. The result is a governance gap between the two rated parties. Neither can tell how much of a verdict came from the counterpart and how much from the rule. Neither can call the mediator to account in a forum that hears both. Neither can leave without losing standing.

The governed fill this gap in two ways, and the paper's contribution is to name both and show how they relate. The first is individual competence. Sellers, hosts and service workers learn to read an ambiguous score for its likely cause. They write messages that satisfy both a human counterpart and the evaluator, and they infer rule changes from shifts in their results. This competence is unevenly distributed, which makes the platform a new source of stratification among formally equal participants. It also has a perverse effect: the more skilfully the governed adapt, the less visible the mediator's authority becomes. The second is collective voice. Hirschman showed that easy exit weakens voice. Non-portable ratings make exit costly, which leaves voice as the remaining lever. Forums and worker organisations pool the evidence that no single participant can see, yet, as Johnston and Land-Kazlauskas report, many platform companies have been unwilling to bargain with workers directly.

Hotels show the arrangement most completely. A hotel and its guests are the two outside parties. Review platforms enrol guests in rating the hotel and rank hotels on an aggregate that the platform computes and does not publish. Scott and Orlikowski found that this user-generated content subverted the older hierarchical accountability in travel, in which complaints moved through internal lines of authority, and redistributed relations of accountability. A hotel cannot see how a guest's review entered its rank. It cannot take a disputed review to a forum where the guest is also heard, and it cannot carry its standing to a competing site: for properties listed on both Airbnb and TripAdvisor, the two sites' ratings correlate only weakly (Zervas et al., 2021). All four features are present, and so is the governance gap.

The paper builds this argument from published studies of three settings: online marketplaces, hotel rating platforms, and app stores and other complementor ecosystems. It reads each for the four features and for the two responses. Three implications follow for economic sociology. First, the analysis of platforms should treat the relation between the two rated parties as a site of governance, not only the relation between operator and provider. Second, research on digital inequality should measure competence in addressing an evaluator, not only access and skill with devices. Third, regulation that targets transparency alone will miss the problem. A published rule does not give the governed a seat, a shared forum or a portable record. Those three goods are the objects of the technopolitics the mini-conference asks about. They are also what the governed most lack.

**Keywords:** platforms; co-optation; governance; evaluation; voice; digital economy
