# Argument: Platform co-optation as a governance arrangement

[DRAFTED by Claude, 26 Oct 2026. For the author's ruling.]

**P1.** Economic sociology describes governance by the relation it sets up between parties: authority in hierarchies, price and contract in markets, reciprocity and reputation in networks. Platforms are often read as a hybrid of these forms.
- *Sources:* `lima_pdw/literature/cards/powell1990.md` (secondary summary only, see FLAGS); `lima_pdw/literature/cards/vallas2020.md` ("permissive potentates").

**P2.** Platforms govern two outside parties through evaluation that passes between them. The operator enrols both in rating and ranking, fuses their judgements with its own opaque rule, and binds each to the result. It does this while holding neither as employee nor agent, and while ratings stay non-portable.
- *Sources:*
  - `dxd_foundation/literature/library/stark2020algorithmic.md` (pp. 48, 53, 61)
  - `lima_pdw/literature/cards/starkvandenbroeck2024.md` (p. 14)

  - `lima_pdw/literature/cards/orlikowskiscott2023.md` (p. 3)
  - `hospitality_phygital/library/cards/zervas2021reputation.md`
  - `coordinative_sovereignty/literature/library/cards/cutolo2021platform.md`

**C.** What is new about platform co-optation is not a fourth logic of coordination. It is a governance problem: an interested, opaque mediator holds evaluative authority over parties who have no seat, no shared forum and a costly exit. The arrangement therefore shifts the work of governance onto the governed. They need individual competence to read and address the mediator, and collective voice to contest it.
- *Warrant for the step:* this follows from P2's four features (interest, opacity, no seat, costly exit), set against P1's forms, each of which gives the parties either authority, a price or reciprocity to work with.
