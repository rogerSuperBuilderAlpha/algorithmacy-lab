## What it argues

The journal article's published summary: "The power of large internet platforms to amplify or silence certain voices at a scale that can alter major political outcomes poses a grave threat to democracy. Approaches to reducing this power have thus far fallen into four main categories: 1) using antitrust legislation to break up companies such as Facebook and Google; 2) government regulation of content; 3) data portability, or empowering users to move their data between platforms; and 4) applying privacy legislation to limit how platforms can use the data they collect. Yet each of these four approaches is inadequate in its own way. A more promising solution lies in using both technology and regulation to outsource content curation from the dominant platforms to a competitive layer of 'middleware companies.'"

The report is the fuller statement. It defines middleware as "software, provided by a third party and integrated into the dominant platforms, that would curate and order the content that users see. Users would choose among competing middleware algorithms, selecting providers that reflect their interests and have earned their trust, and thereby would dilute the platforms' editorial control over political communication" (p. 3). Middleware "would interconnect with Facebook, Amazon, Apple, Twitter, and Google APIs and allow consumers to shape their feeds and influence the algorithms that those dominant platforms currently employ," and "would make editorial judgments that are currently provided (usually without transparency) by the platform" (p. 5). Offerings could include "fact-checking services, news rankings, relevance priorities, information filters," adjusted Amazon results favouring "domestic production and eco-friendly products," and "fine-grained choices on Facebook's privacy settings dashboard" (pp. 5–6). The political framing is the gun on the table: "The growing political power of the current major digital platforms is like a loaded weapon sitting on the table in front of us... The question for American democracy is whether it is safe to leave the gun on the table" (pp. 3–4). The report is candid about openness: "we offer here only a conceptual outline" (p. 7). Three questions follow. The division of labour between platform and middleware runs from the platform as "little more than a neutral pipe" to middleware as "no more than... a supplemental filter"; "It is unlikely that either of these extreme arrangements would be satisfactory" (p. 8). A business model would probably need "revenue sharing arrangements," set by regulators if the parties cannot agree (pp. 8–9). And a technical framework must handle three classes of content, of which "content that is not public" the middleware "will not have access to" (p. 9). Mandates are envisaged: platforms "allow users to choose among third-party filters," possibly with middleware as the default users must opt out of, since "a default option that allows users to never consider installing middleware would severely limit its uptake" (p. 7). The closing line is the promise: "With middleware, platform users would be handed the controls over what they see. They—and not some invisible artificial intelligence program—would determine their ultimate online experience" (p. 12).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8. Sorted: **counter-delegation proper** — the only mainstream policy proposal that places an agent, chosen by the user, inside the platform's coordination to act on the user's behalf in real time, and so the nearest existing name for Principle I. Three features matter. First, the report already contains the spectrum the paper needs: at the "neutral pipe" end the platform becomes a moderator (Φ = 0 on the construct's terms) and middleware is the whole intermediary; at the "supplemental filter" end the platform keeps its determinations and middleware only labels them. The report rejects both ends and wants the middle, which is exactly the region where a counter-agent and the platform's own objective coexist — the construct's Φ > 0 case with a second agent added. Second, the report's user is a *reader* of content, not a party being matched with another party; nothing in it contemplates that the item being re-ranked is a determination about someone else (a driver, a seller, a co-worker). The transfer to the construct's triad is the paper's, and the report's silence on the second human is a limit to state. Third, the report's honesty about what is unbuilt (business model, API access, private content) is the reason Keller's rejoinder (keller2021.md) has force; the pair should be cited together, the report for the mechanism and Keller for the obstacles. CUTS AGAINST nothing in the paper directly, but its closing promise — users "would determine their ultimate online experience" — is the literacy paradigm's remedy-in-the-subject restated with a new tool, and the paper should say why a counter-agent that only expresses preferences over a feed is not yet parity in a coordination.

## Caution

The journal article itself was not read; do not quote it beyond the summary above, and check the body before attributing to it any claim found only in the report. The report is a policy outline with no data, no prototype and no evaluation; its authors say so. Its examples are American platforms and its remedy is American regulation (a new specialized agency, congressional legislation). "Users" throughout means consumers and voters; the report never models labour platforms or two-sided matching, so its relevance to the construct rests on the mechanism, not the setting.
