# Le Dantec, C. A., Poole, E. S., & Wyche, S. P. (2009). Values as lived experience: Evolving value sensitive design in support of value discovery. *Proceedings of CHI 2009*, 1141–1150.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/1518701.1518875 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-deposited camera-ready at ledantec.net; page numbers below are absent from that copy, so quotes are located by section) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top venue (CHI); critique plus three formative photo-elicitation studies · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** designer–stakeholders · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

VSD, the authors say, "has gotten it backwards. What is needed is more prescription in methods that inform value-centered investigations, and less prescription in the kinds of values considered" (Introduction). Three coupled complaints follow. The twelve "values of ethical import" act as a classification that "privileges a discursive definition of values over values that may be discovered or encountered through investigation," and classifications embedded in working infrastructure "risk getting black-boxed" (A Critique of VSD, citing Bowker and Star). VSD gives no guidance on which empirical instruments fit, so researchers "are left with poor tools for engaging locally expressed values." And the conceptual-first ordering means "iterating over a known classification will not reveal something not already expressed by that classification"; the canonical VSD cases (web cookies, the video window, UrbanSim) present values "ex post facto," after deployment. The remedy is to move empirical investigation to the front and treat values as "local phenomena, expressed in a local vocabulary." Three photo-elicitation studies illustrate: homeless adults in a US city, where a transit system's switch from tokens to a debit card removed the ability to know one's remaining fares and so undercut "independence"; public perceptions of RFID, where participants withheld objections for fear of seeming not "knowledgeable" or not "normal" — "any concerns the participant might raise would be ill-founded or easily dismissed"; and older homemakers' oral histories, where values of work ethic, quality and togetherness surfaced rather than sustainability. The paper closes by asking for designs "that reflect the values of the people they are meant to serve rather than the values of the system designers."

## Relation to the argument

Two uses for RQ5. First, the paper poses the adjudication question and leaves it: "As tensions between values of designers and clients emerge, whose values take precedence? ... whose values we are being sensitive to" (A Critique of VSD). The authors answer with a population to serve, not with a rule for the case where the commissioning client is the operator and the served population is being coopted; the frame stays consensual. Second, the RFID finding is the most useful empirical fragment in this cluster for the construct itself: contestation has a competence precondition and a social cost, since people who cannot read the system decline to object. That supports placing the interpretive capability before "contest what it commits," and it supports the process/product complement — a locally discovered value decides what the seam should disclose, an affordance decides how. The paper does not cut against the four affordances, but it does imply a generic seam cannot be specified in advance of value discovery.

## Caution

Three formative interview studies, sample sizes not reported in the paper, all in the United States, none producing or testing a design; the argument for reordering VSD rests on a reading of published VSD case studies, and Borning and Muller (2012) reply that VSD prescribes no order and that the homelessness study itself began with a conceptual choice about whom to interview. The transit and RFID examples are one-sided human–technology cases; no two-party mediation appears.
