# Vallor, S. (2015). Moral deskilling and upskilling in a new machine age: Reflections on the ambiguous future of character. *Philosophy & Technology*, 28(1), 107–124.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/s13347-014-0156-9 · **Read depth:** full_text (Springer version of record, PDF mirrored at bhaven.org; pagination 107–124 matches the journal) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, the field's main philosophy-of-technology journal; conceptual argument, no empirical study · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology–world (soldier–weapon–target, carer–robot–patient, user–media–interlocutor); the intermediary is never modeled as interpreting both sides · **Remedy locus:** subject — cultivate the skill; design and participatory methods "will not take us all the way" (p. 122) and a cultural shift is asked for, but the thing to be repaired is the person's judgment · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-19

## What it argues

Vallor takes the sociology-of-labour concept of deskilling (Braverman 1974) and applies it to moral skill. Moral skills, on the Aristotelian skill model she adopts from Annas, are "acquired quasi-perceptual sensitivit[ies] to the morally salient features of particular circumstances" (p. 109), and they are "typically acquired in specific practices which, under the right conditions and with sufficient opportunity for repetition, foster the cultivation of practical wisdom and moral habituation" (p. 109). She gives a test: "if it is challenging to practice towards the right people, at the right times and places, and in the right manner, then it is a moral skill, not merely a reflex, attitude, belief, or value" (p. 111). Skills are "necessary (while not sufficient) conditions of genuine moral virtue or practical wisdom" (p. 111), so a technology that removes the occasions for practising them interrupts the path to character even if outcomes improve.

The criterion for when technological assistance deskills is stated on p. 113: the tools of ethics amplify our capacities and do not diminish us, "yet we could be, if these tools somehow obviated the need for us to employ our own skillful moral judgment, rather than aiding us in its successful exercise." Principles like the categorical imperative still demand interpretive effort; "an automated system that produces reliable and situation-specific moral judgments in a particular domain by employing its own perceptive and interpretative capacities, with minimal human input, is an entirely different story" (p. 113). Three cases follow. Autonomous weapons: even if robots fought more ethically than humans, humans "on the loop" could not supervise them, because "a supervisor, in order to be a legitimate authority, must have more experience and practical wisdom than the supervisee" and will not "share with them a comparable decision architecture" (p. 115). Media multitasking: attention is a moral skill (p. 117); the lockout apps (Freedom, AntiSocial, Self-Control) are "an imperfect solution" — "Why must we choose between tools that debilitate us and digital lockboxes to keep us away from these tools?" (p. 118). Carebots: van Wynsberghe's patient-lift example — a nurse lifting with a machine "is hardly engaged in skillful caring if the nurse's attention throughout must be devoted to monitoring the machine controls and not the patient—even if the end result is a safe and efficient lift"; an exoskeleton that keeps eye contact possible is the better design (p. 120). The recurring question is "what they may do *with* us" rather than "for us" (pp. 118–119; "for us" alone also at pp. 112–113). The diagnosis of why the bad potentials win is cultural instrumentalism rather than markets as such: "Let us avoid the lazy answer: 'Because capitalism'" (p. 121); "It is quite another to let the free market determine what quality of moral beings we are, or shall become" (pp. 118–119).

## Relation to the argument

This is the paper RQ3 turns on, and two things in it matter for the construct. First, Vallor's criterion is a formation criterion, not an objective criterion, and it does not reduce to "whose objective the friction serves." The lift example is decisive: the machine's objective (a safe, efficient lift) is the nurse's and the patient's own, no third objective is in the loop, and the design still deskills because it captures the attention that caring practice requires. Conversely the lockout apps serve the user's own objective and she still counts them as a poor answer. So "whose objective" is neither necessary nor sufficient for her verdict; what decides is whether the arrangement keeps the human exercising the judgment in question. Second, p. 115 cuts against the definition's "contest what it commits." Vallor argues that once judgment is delegated, the capacity to evaluate the delegate's calls decays, because that capacity is cultivated only in the practice being delegated. Algorithmacy as a standing competence therefore presupposes some friction that keeps the user practising the underlying judgment — which is her criterion, not the cooptation reading. The two are complementary: Φ and the objective axis say when a third objective is present; Vallor says which frictions, present or absent, form or deform the person who must contest it. Her market pages (118–121) come closest to the objective axis, but she explicitly refuses to locate the problem in whose objective the market serves.

## Caution

The argument is entirely conceptual; the only empirical material is second-hand (Wang and Tchernev 2012 on multitasking, Coeckelbergh 2013 on drone pilots). "Practice" here is Aristotelian via Annas, Hursthouse and Borgmann's focal practices; MacIntyre is not cited anywhere in the paper, so the RQ's phrase "a practice in MacIntyre's sense" cannot be attributed to this text. The word "friction" does not appear; the friction reading is the essay's, not Vallor's. The cases are dyadic-with-a-world (soldier, carer, user), never a mediated triad in which the intermediary interprets both parties, and the paper offers no design procedure — it ends by asking for a shift in cultural values and says "values by design" and "participatory design" "will not take us all the way" (p. 122).

## Relation to the moderator/mediator pivot (cluster B pass, 2026-09-18)

RQ2 asks whether the technomoral virtues presuppose a subject with time to practise, and the 2015 paper answers yes in its own terms: skills are "acquired in specific practices which, under the right conditions and with sufficient opportunity for repetition, foster the cultivation of practical wisdom" (p. 109), attention is itself a moral skill (p. 117), and the lockout-app passage (p. 118) is the attention-bandwidth point stated as a complaint about the design space ("Why must we choose between tools that debilitate us and digital lockboxes"). What the paper does not say is who sets the tempo. Its three cases are a soldier, a carer and a media user, and in each the technology is on the person's own side of the relation — the drone is the soldier's, the lift is the nurse's, the feed is the user's — so the opportunity for repetition is lost to a tool the person holds, not to a party that interprets two people at once and decides in seconds. The remedy locus follows from that framing: cultivate the subject, shift the culture, and treat design as a partial aid. The pivot's objection is therefore not that Vallor is individualist (p. 122 refutes that) but that her remedy is addressed to the one party in the relation who does not control its cadence. Winner (1977, pp. 304–305; see winner1977.md) made the same objection to the cultivate-the-subject remedy a generation earlier — nourishing "moral sensibility" is idle if the "organizational situation ... mocks the very idea of responsible conduct" — and Vallor's own 2024 book (pp. 88–91) imports Winner's reverse adaptation for AI-set tempo, so the objection can be run inside her own sources. One caution the book adds: the 2015 Caution's "friction is the essay's word, not Vallor's" is still true of this paper, but the 2016 book uses "communicative friction" as a term of art at pp. 161–163 (see vallor2016.md); cite the friction reading to the book.
