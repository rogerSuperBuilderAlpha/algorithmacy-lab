## What it argues

From the abstract: the paper deploys "the 'script' concept, indicating how technologies prescribe human actions," in "a normative setting" after a decade of descriptive use; when technologies co-shape action "they give material answers to the ethical question of how to act," so engineers are "doing 'ethics by other means': they materialize morality." It "first augments the script concept by developing the notion of technological mediation," then "investigates how the concept of mediation could be made fruitful for design ethics," and "elaborates two methods for anticipating technological mediation in the design process: performing mediation analyses and using an augmented version of constructive technology assessment." The 2008 chapter (verbeek2008b) restates the augmentation as mediation in two directions, pragmatic (Latour, Akrich) and hermeneutic (Ihde), and cites this paper as the home of "mediation analysis" (p. 100) and of "materialize morality" and "doing ethics by other means" (p. 99). The 2015 magazine piece cites *Moralizing Technology* rather than this paper for "designers materialize morality" (p. 31). I could not verify any page number inside 361–380, the paper's treatment of Ihde's embodiment and hermeneutic relations, or the CTA proposal in its own words.

It is Verbeek's own text, but a pre-publication one: its abstract reads "'ethics with other means'" where the VoR abstract reads "'ethics by other means'," it names the first method "'anticipation by imagination' in the form of a 'mediation analysis'," its reference list has twelve entries against the VoR's fifteen (the VoR adds Verbeek 2002, 2005 and the forthcoming "Acting artifacts"; the preprint cites Verbeek 2000, *De daadkracht der dingen*), and its note 1 says the mediation section reworks a chapter forthcoming in Verbeek and Slob, "Technology and Behavior (provisional title)." Page numbers below are **typescript pages 1–28**, not journal pages 361–380; every quotation should be re-located in the VoR before it enters the paper, and the wording may differ.

**Structure and how much CTA occupies.** §1 Conceptualizing the moral dimension of technologies (the script concept, Akrich and Latour; pp. 2–7); §2 technological mediation (Ihde's embodiment and hermeneutic relations, Latour's translation, Figure 1 and the Figure 2 "vocabulary for technological mediation"; pp. 7–13); §3 design ethics: §3.1 Moralizing technologies (pp. 14–16), §3.2 Designing mediations (pp. 16–18), §3.3 Anticipation by imagination: 'Eternally Yours' (pp. 19–21), §3.4 Augmenting Constructive Technology Assessment (pp. 22–25); §4 Conclusion (pp. 25–26). CTA is one subsection of four typescript pages plus two sentences in §3.2 and one paragraph of the conclusion; the Eternally Yours example (Sigrid Smits's couch, Sven Adolph's ceramic heater) gets as much space.

**Where CTA comes from and why it needs augmenting.** §3.1 sets up the problem: Achterhuis's plea to "moralize" technology drew two objections, that steering behaviour by design attacks freedom and dignity, and that it is technocratic, since "laws come about in a democratic way, and the moralization of technology does not" (p. 16). Verbeek's answer to the second is the hinge for CTA: "If technologies are not moralized explicitly, after all, the responsibility for technological mediation is left to the designers only. Precisely this would amount to form of technocracy. A better conclusion would be that it is important to find a democratic way to 'moralize technology'" (p. 16). §3.2 then gives two ways to make "an informed prediction of its future mediating role and a moral assessment of this role," which he calls "a 'mediation analysis'" (p. 18): "prediction by the imagination of the designer" (p. 18), and "an augmentation of the existing design methodology of Constructive Technology Assessment in such a way, that it becomes an instrument for a democratically organized moralization of technology" (p. 19).

**The augmentation, in his words (§3.4).** CTA is cited to "Schot 1992; Rip, Misa and Schot 1995" (p. 22). "CTA creates a link between the contexts of design and use in a practical way: it aims to involve all relevant stakeholders in the design of technologies. In order to make use of the CTA methodology within the context of technological mediation, it needs to be augmented, though" (p. 22). CTA "is a method to employ this nexus in a systematical way, by feeding back assessments of the technology-on-design by all relevant actors -- like users, pressure groups, designers, companies et cetera -- into the design process. It does so by organizing meetings of all relevant actors in which the aim is to reach consensus about the design of the technology that is 'constructively assessed'" (pp. 22–23). "Besides this, CTA can be seen as a democratization of the designing process. When a CTA design methodology is followed, not only designers determine what a technology will look like, but all relevant social actors. Following this method, therefore, could take away the fear for technocracy that was discussed above" (p. 23). The limitation: "CTA primarily focuses on human actors, and pays too little attention to the actively mediating role of the nonhuman actor that is at the center of all activity: the technology-in-design" (p. 23); by analysing only the dynamics of development "the black box of technology is only opened half way. It reveals how technologies emerge from their design context, but their role in their use context remains blackboxed. Therefore, organizing a democratically, domination-free discussion between all relevant actors is not enough to lay bare all relevant aspects of the technology in question" (p. 23). The fix: "participants in the CTA process should not only be invited to integrate assessments of users and pressure groups in product specifications, but also to anticipate possible mediating roles of the technology-in-design. The vocabulary for analyzing mediation, as presented in section 2 of this paper, could be a helpful for doing this" (p. 24); Figure 4, "Mediation and the nexus between design and use," draws the nexus as "anticipation of mediation • by imagination • augmenting CTA" (p. 24). "When the CTA method is augmented in this way, the method of 'anticipation by imagination' is given a more systematic character. Creating space for all relevant stakeholders to anticipate the possible mediating role of the technology-in-design enhances the chance that as many possible mediating roles are taken into account" (pp. 24–25), though this "does not guarantee that all mediating roles of the technology in design will be predicted" and "cannot possibly cover all 'emergent' mediating roles" (p. 25). The conclusion restates it: designers "carry out a mediation analysis, together with all stakeholders that are involved with the technology-in-design, and decide in a democratically organized debate how to feed back the outcomes of this analysis into the design process," which "could also take away the fear that deliberately designing behavior-steering technology would lead to a technocratic society, since the inevitable mediating role of technology is then made subject to democratic decision-making" (p. 26).

**What this settles for the draft.** The two open questions in RESEARCH_PLAN_TERMINOLOGY.md §1.2 are answered. (1) The augmentation reaches the user as a *participant*: users are named first among the "relevant actors" whose assessments are fed back (p. 22), and the augmentation's content is that those participants, not only the designer, anticipate mediations (p. 24). (2) The draft's sentence — Verbeek's constructive remedies "presume a participatory, democratic design context where designers and users collaboratively negotiate the moral mediation of an artifact" — is accurate to Verbeek's own account, not a gloss borrowed from Rip and Schot: "democratization of the designing process," "all relevant social actors," "democratically organized debate," and "subject to democratic decision-making" are his phrases. One word is not his: the CTA process aims "to reach consensus" (p. 23), and "negotiate" is Schot and Rip's (schotrip1997.md, pp. 253, 262); the draft can keep "negotiate" if it cites both. What the draft cannot say is that the paper considers a use context the participants cannot inspect: Verbeek's whole worry is that CTA leaves the use context "blackboxed" (p. 23) and his remedy assumes the stakeholders can be put in a room with the technology-in-design and a vocabulary. A proprietary model that is optimised after deployment defeats the nexus the augmentation is built to create, so the draft's "collapse" claim is a fair extension, and a stronger one now that the mechanism is on the table. Proposed wording is in schotrip1997.md's Relation section.

**Second-party note.** The prior card's caution stands: the preprint models designer, user, and technology (Figure 3, "Sources of mediation," p. 18: user, designer, technology; Figure 4 adds "Environment"). No second human user appears anywhere, and "all relevant stakeholders" is a category of assessors, not of coordinated parties.

## Relation to the protect/promote question (2026-09-19, F3 pass)

Telos promotive, instrument promotive, beneficiary singular — the same shape as verbeek2009.md and verbeek2011.md. The preprint's §3.1 takes up Achterhuis's plea to "moralize" technology and answers the freedom and technocracy objections rather than retreating from the plea (p. 16); §3.2 asks designers to make "an informed prediction of its future mediating role and a moral assessment of this role" (p. 18) and, in the 2009 restatement of this paper, to "deliberately 'inscribe' or 'build in' specific forms of mediation" (verbeek2009.md, p. 256), which is promotion by design. Its democratic instrument, augmented CTA, distributes the decision about which mediations to build across "all relevant stakeholders" (p. 22), so the promoted good is not the designer's alone — a correction to RESEARCH_PLAN_FLOURISHING.md §6's "the designer's conception of the good." But the sources of mediation are "user, designer, technology" (Figure 3, p. 18); the user is one; and the assessors in the CTA room are a category of judges, not two parties whose interests the artefact will trade. Who delivers the promotive part: the design process, feeding stakeholder anticipation of mediations back into an artefact built once. For the matrix this is the process-side statement of the same locus verbeek2009.md gives on the subject side; neither is the relation. Remedy locus added today.
