## What it argues

Abstract: "This paper tracks a debate that occurred, first, within the field of Ubiquitous Computing but quickly spread to CHI and beyond, in which design scholars argued that seamlessness had long been an implicit and privileged design virtue, often at the expense of seamfulness. Seamless design emphasizes clarity, simplicity, ease of use, and consistency to facilitate technological interaction. Seamful design emphasizes configurability, user appropriation, and revelation of complexity, ambiguity or inconsistency. Here we review these literatures together and argue that, rather than rival approaches, seamful and seamless design are complements, each emphasizing different aspects of downstream user agency. Ultimately, we situate this debate within the larger, perennial discussion about the strategic revelation and concealment of human and technological operations, and therein the role of design."

## Relation to the argument

This is the bridge between Chalmers and Galani's infrastructural seams and Ehsan et al.'s seamful XAI, and Ehsan cites it for the term. Two phrases in the abstract do work for the paper even without the body. "Downstream user agency" names what both seamless and seamful design are for, which is the register in which the construct's competence claim should be placed: affordances serve agency, and the question is whose. "Strategic revelation and concealment of human and technological operations" is the phrase that turns a seam from an honest gap (Chalmers) into a *choice made by someone* — which is where the objective axis enters the seams lineage. If the intermediary's designers decide what to reveal, then a seam is a determination like any other, and a seam revealed to one party but not the other is a strategic concealment from the second. The paper's framing therefore supports the "asymmetric seamfulness" point (see chalmersgalani2004.md) and pushes it one step further: revelation is itself a move in the game, not a view from outside it. CUTS AGAINST: the review treats revelation and concealment as *design* choices about *operations*, with a single downstream user; nothing in the abstract suggests a case where the operation concealed is the system's objective toward a second user.
