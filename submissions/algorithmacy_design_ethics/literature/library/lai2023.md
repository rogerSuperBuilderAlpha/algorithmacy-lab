## What it argues

The field needs common frameworks before it can accumulate knowledge. The authors screened CHI, CSCW, FAccT, IUI, EMNLP, ACL and NAACL from 2018 to 2021, started from over 130 candidates, kept "over 80 in-scope papers" (the abstract says "over 100"), and coded each on three design choices: decision task, AI model and assistance elements, and evaluation metrics. For tasks they build a domain table (law and civic, medicine, finance, education, leisure, professional, artificial, generic) and propose four characteristics that govern generalizability — risk, required expertise, subjectivity, and AI for emulation versus discovery — noting, with Jacovi et al., that trust can only be studied where the decision-maker is vulnerable, and that most studies use laypeople on tasks that require experts. For assistance elements they distinguish predictions, information about predictions (uncertainty, explanations), information about the model (training data, performance), and elements affecting agency. For metrics they find home-grown, unvalidated scales, a bias toward efficacy over efficiency and satisfaction, and a habit of reading reliance as trust when "many other factors besides trust can influence reliance, such as required efforts, perceived risk, self-confidence, and time constraints" (§5). The summary calls for replication, shared reporting templates, and frameworks that "make explicit otherwise latent or disregarded factors."

## Relation to the argument

RQ4, and the sharpest answer in this cluster to whether the reliance literature admits a second human. It does not, and this survey says so twice. The inclusion rule: "The task must involve and focus on studying human decision makers, thus we exclude papers on AI automation or other AI stakeholders (model developers, ML practitioners)" (§2). The closing recommendation: the field should consider "what matters for different stakeholders instead of just the decision-makers (e.g., people whose life will be impacted by the decision)" (§6). Between those two sentences lies the whole space this essay's construct occupies. The survey's design space has one human on the near side of the AI and treats the person on the far side — the defendant whose recidivism is predicted, the applicant whose loan is scored — as the content of the task rather than a party to it. That is a documented, quotable absence, and it is the survey's own diagnosis rather than the essay's imputation. The four task characteristics are also useful: they show that "risk" in this literature means the decision-maker's exposure, never the decision subject's.

## Caution

A survey of 2018–2021 work in seven venues, weighted toward NLP and HCI; it predates the generative-AI wave and the essay's platform cases. Counts differ between abstract and method (over 100 vs over 80). I read the arXiv v1, not the FAccT paper, whose title differs and whose tables may have been revised; cite the DOI only after checking the ACM page. The survey does not itself run a study, so it establishes what the literature omits, not what happens when the omission is repaired.
