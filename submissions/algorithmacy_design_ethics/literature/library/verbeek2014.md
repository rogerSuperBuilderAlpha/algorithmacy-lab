## What it argues

Verbeek answers the objection he says he heard "at least once a month" while writing *Moralizing Technology*: "So you really think we should blame cars for traffic accidents?" (p. 75). The misunderstanding is to read "technologies are involved in moral agency" as "technologies are moral agents." Mediation theory, he says, "studies technologies as mediators between humans and reality," in which "technologies are not merely silent 'intermediaries' but active 'mediators' that help to constitute the entities that have a relationship 'through' the technology" (p. 77). Ultrasound is again the paradigm: it "'translates' the unborn child into a possible patient" and "expecting a child into choosing for a child" (p. 78), and even the decision not to test "has become a moral decision now," which the wrongful-life suits show (p. 79). Against Pitt (instrumentalism) and Illies and Meijers (technology as mere context), he claims "moral agency needs to be understood as a fundamentally hybrid affair" (p. 77), while insisting, against Peterson's mountain objection, that his claim "is simply that, in mediating how humans are directed at reality, technologies help to shape moral intentions" (p. 82). Two passages matter for design. First: "Use practices can be enriched by equipping users with the ability to 'read' how the technologies they use help to shape their actions and decisions, so that they can deal with these mediations in creative and responsible ways" (p. 78). Second, on freedom, using Berlin's positive liberty: "it is the denial of the mediating role of technologies in human freedom that makes people not free. Only when making the mediations explicit, we can develop a free relation to it" (p. 84). He also marks the limit of design: "all technologies are multistable, in Don Ihde's words (1990) ... their mediating power is hardly predictable" (p. 86). A separate passage distinguishes persuasive and ambient technologies from the ultrasound case: with the "persuasive mirror," intentions "are not so much 'mediated' as they are 'induced' ... technologies use human beings here to do their work" (p. 83). He closes by rejecting Latour's symmetry in favour of "interaction and mutual constitution," keeping the distinction between humans and things while dropping their separation (p. 86).

## Relation to the argument

RQ2 is where this card earns its place. Page 78 states the competence the construct calls algorithmacy, minus the second party: users equipped to read how the technology shapes their decisions. Page 84 is the licence for seamful disclosure in mediation-theory terms, since making mediations explicit is what produces a free relation, and it is also the reason a seam cannot be neutral: explicitness is itself a mediation, so there is no peripheral vision from nowhere. Page 86 on multistability is the ground for the prediction that one seam means different things to the two parties. RQ1 and the objective axis: page 83 is the point at which Verbeek's own vocabulary reaches a technology with a programme of its own ("technologies use human beings here to do their work"), which is the cooptation reading in one clause, yet the case is still one human and a mirror. The intermediary/mediator contrast on p. 77 is rhetorical rather than diagnostic: he asserts there are no silent intermediaries, which cuts against the construct's channel/party threshold as plainly as verbeek2015 does.

## Caution

A reply to critics, so it restates rather than argues the theory, and the examples (ultrasound, speed bumps, DBS, the persuasive mirror) are the same handful used across all of Verbeek's work. "Reading" mediations is proposed in one sentence with no method or evidence that users can do it. The DBS case (Leentjens et al. 2004, a Dutch medical-journal report) is a single patient. Nothing here models two humans; the "other" in every example is the world, a fetus, or a mirror.
