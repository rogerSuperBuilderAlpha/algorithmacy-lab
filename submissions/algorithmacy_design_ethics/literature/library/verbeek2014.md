## What it argues

Verbeek answers the objection he says he heard "at least once a month" while writing *Moralizing Technology*: "So you really think we should blame cars for traffic accidents?" (p. 75). The misunderstanding is to read "technologies are involved in moral agency" as "technologies are moral agents." Mediation theory "studies technologies as mediators between humans and reality," in which technologies are "active 'mediators' that help to constitute the entities that have a relationship 'through' the technology" (p. 77 [GB]). Ultrasound is the paradigm: it "'translates' the unborn child into a possible patient" and "expecting a child into choosing for a child" (p. 78), and "Morality appears to be a coproduction of humans and nonhumans" (p. 78 [GB]). Against Pitt and against Illies and Meijers he holds that moral agency is a "hybrid affair; it cannot be located exclusively in things, but not in humans either. Each in their own way – distinct, but not separated – humans and things contribute to moral actions and decisions" (p. 80 [GB]).

Three passages carry the design argument. First, on use: "Use practices can be enriched by equipping users with the ability to 'read' how the technologies they use help to shape their actions and decisions, so that they can deal with these mediations in creative and responsible ways" (p. 78 [GB]), which follows a call to "anticipate the mediating roles of technologies" (p. 78 [GB]). Second, on design: the theory enables "designers and users to anticipate, assess, and design moral mediations in technology," though "some moral mediations emerge without the explicit intention of any human agent," ultrasound again (p. 82 [GB]). Third, on freedom, with Berlin's positive liberty: "Only when making the mediations explicit, we can develop a free relation to it – understood as positive, not as negative freedom. A free relation to technology does not require the absence of its influence, but the presence of the..." (p. 84 [GB]).

Page 83 is the passage this pass went looking for. "Current technological developments" (p. 82) bring technologies that "merge with our physical environment and with our own bodies. Ambient Intelligence technologies, for instance, result in 'smart environments', that actively register and monitor events, and react and intervene accordingly. Smart beds in geriatric hospitals are good examples here; they detect if patients fall out of bed or step out of their beds. When such smart environments are explicitly designed to influence people's decisions and behavior, as is the case with so-called 'persuasive technologies', they embody a truly new form of moral significance" (p. 83 [GB]). The example is the "persuasive mirror," "in fact a flat screen monitor with a built-in camera," which persuades "by presenting them with an image of how they will look in the future if they would stick to their current pattern of living (Knight 2005)" (p. 83 [GB]). The intentions of its users are "'induced'. They are not the result of 'using' a technology – rather, technologies use human beings here to do their work. At the other extreme, technologies do not merge with our environment but with our bodies" (p. 83 [GB]); the spectrum runs from "induced intentions to the fully-fledged hybrid intentions of human-technology assemblies like people with brain implants" (p. 84 [GB]). The limit of design is multistability: "all technologies are multistable, in Don Ihde's words (1990). Technologies often end up in different relations with human beings than their designers expected, and therefore their mediating power is hardly predictable. But this unpredictable character of technological mediations does not make human beings entirely powerless. Industrial Designers, for instance, have developed various methods to make an educated guess about possible..." (pp. 85–86 [GB]). He closes against Latour's symmetry: "interaction and mutual constitution. Things are not symmetrical to humans, but together, humans and things constitute myriad 'hybrid entities'" (p. 86 [GB]).

## Relation to the argument

RQ1, the adaptivity axis, and the answer is that this chapter is fixed. The in-volume search finds no occurrence of "adapt," "adaptive," "learn" (except humans who "learn to orient themselves," p. 82), "feedback," "update," "algorithm," "software," or "profile" anywhere in pp. 75–88. The ambient case on p. 83 is the nearest miss and it reads one way: smart environments "register and monitor events, and react and intervene." The bed detects a fall and responds; the mirror reads a face and projects a future one. Nothing in the passage has the environment change what it is on the strength of what it reads. Multistability (pp. 85–86) is likewise interpretive openness across users and contexts, "different relations ... than their designers expected," and not run-time re-fitting; its remedy is the designer's "educated guess" made before deployment. So "static script" was the wrong charge, since Verbeek's mediators are active, but a narrower charge survives on the text: his vocabulary reaches technologies that read a person and act on the reading, and stops there. It has no term for a mediation re-fitted to the person it mediates.

The same volume makes that silence conspicuous. Chapter 11 is the Floridi and Sanders reprint, which defines an agent by interactivity, autonomy and adaptability and glosses adaptability as "learning, the research area in computer science that studies adaptability" (p. 195 [GB]), with a bot that "adapts its behaviour to our preferences" and must "learn the user's preferences" (p. 198 [GB]); see floridisanders2004.md. Chapter 3, Introna's, analyses Turnitin, a detector that "updates itself at a rate of 60 million pages per day" (p. 44 [GB]) and stands between teachers and students. Verbeek co-edited both into the book and his own chapter takes up neither the criterion nor the case.

On the objective axis, p. 83 remains the point where his vocabulary reaches a technology with a programme of its own ("technologies use human beings here to do their work"), still with one human and a mirror. The p. 77 intermediary/mediator line is asserted, not tested, and cuts against the construct's channel/party threshold as verbeek2015 does. For RQ2, p. 78 states the competence the construct calls algorithmacy minus the second party, and p. 84 is the licence for disclosure in mediation-theory terms. Remedy locus is the subject: what is enriched is the user's ability to read, with design anticipation as the designer's share (p. 82); no remedy is placed in a relation between two mediated people, because no such relation appears.

## Caution

A reply to critics, so it restates rather than argues the theory, and the examples (ultrasound, speed bumps, DBS, the persuasive mirror) recur across all of Verbeek's work. This pass re-verified by snippet, not by re-reading the chapter whole: the first-pass quotations from pp. 75, 78 ("translates"), 79 and 82 (Peterson) were not re-located and stand on the earlier read. The FutureLearn URL is still unrecorded. "Reading" mediations is proposed in one sentence with no method. The vocabulary census in the Relation section rests on Google's index of the Springer scan, which can miss hyphenated or OCR-damaged tokens; treat it as strong evidence, not proof (the verbeek2011 card says the same of its census). Nothing here models two humans; the "other" in every example is the world, a fetus, or a mirror.
