# Endsley, M. R., & Kiris, E. O. (1995). The out-of-the-loop performance problem and level of control in automation. *Human Factors*, 37(2), 381–394.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1518/001872095779064555 · **Read depth:** abstract_only (verbatim abstract from the Consensus/Semantic Scholar index; article paywalled at SAGE; not among the PDFs archived from SA Technologies' publications directory, and no other deposit found) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, flagship HF journal; laboratory experiment · **Evidence basis:** secondary · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (one operator, one expert system) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The abstract names the out-of-the-loop performance problem as "a major potential consequence of automation" that "leaves operators of automated systems handicapped in their ability to take over manual operations in the event of automation failure," and attributes it to "a possible loss of skills and of situation awareness (SA) arising from vigilance and complacency problems, a shift from active to passive information processing, and change in feedback provided to the operator." The experiment "studied the automation of a navigation task using an expert system and demonstrated that low SA corresponded with out-of-the-loop performance decrements in decision time following a failure of the expert system." Two conclusions: "Level of operator control in interacting with automation is a major factor in moderating this loss of SA," and "the shift from active to passive processing was most likely responsible for decreased SA under automated conditions." Later work by the same group (Endsley & Kaber 1999; Kaber & Endsley 2004, abstracts seen in the same search) builds a level-of-automation taxonomy on this result, finding intermediate levels best for recovery after failure.

## Relation to the argument

Structural axis, as the classic statement that being out of the loop is a competence loss with a mechanism — passive processing — and a remedy — intermediate control. That is the nearest HF antecedent to the construct's "adapt as it changes": if the operator has been passive, the takeover fails. On this pass's question: dyad. One operator, one expert system, one navigation task; no second human appears in the abstract, and the loop in "out of the loop" is the control loop between the operator and the process, not a channel between two people. The paper's world is one where being in the loop is available by design choice; the construct's irreducible case is one where the direct channel does not survive at any level of control, which is outside this paper's design space.

## Caution

Abstract-only. Sample, task details, the SA measure (presumably SAGAT, but unverified here), and effect sizes are unread. The 1995 result is on decision time after a single failure of an expert system in a navigation microworld; do not generalise beyond that without the body. The level-of-automation programme it seeded has been contested (see the Parasuraman 2000 card).
