## What it argues

The opening sentence sets the frame the protect/promote question needs: "Ever since the Enlightenment, ethics has had a humanist character. Not 'the good life' but the individual person now has a central place in it, taken as the fountainhead of moral decisions and practices" (p. 241). Verbeek's target is the "humanist metaphysics behind contemporary ethics," which "needs to be overcome in order to include the moral dimension of objects and their mediation of the morality of subjects" (p. 243); he keeps humanism's values — "self-determination, integrity, pluriformity and responsibility" (p. 243) — and drops its ontology. Deontology and consequentialism each sit at one pole of the subject–object split (pp. 245–247); against both he sets "classical and medieval virtue ethics. Here, not the question of the right action was central but the question of the good life. This question does not depart from a separation of subject and object, but from the interwoven character of both" (p. 247). Obstetric ultrasound is the running case: it "helps to constitute the unborn as a possible patient and its parents as decision-makers about the life of their unborn" (p. 253), so "moral agency should not be seen as an exclusively human property; it is distributed over human beings and non-human entities" (p. 253). Sloterdijk's "taming" and "breeding" are re-read so that technologies count as "material media" that tame us (p. 252).

The constructive part (§11.5) has two lines. *Ethics of design:* "Beside developing lingual frameworks for moral judgement, ethics then also consists in designing material infrastructures for morality. When matter is morally charged, after all, designing is the moral activity par excellence, but simply 'by other means'. Designers materialize morality" (p. 254). "The designers of these technologies, consequently, have the responsibility to help to shape these mediating roles in desirable ways" (p. 255); once the subject–object split is overcome, "ethics can move beyond the fear that non-human objects will start to suffocate human subjects and direct its attention to the moral quality of associations of subjects and objects" (p. 255). Achterhuis's moralization proposal and the technocracy objection are answered as in verbeek2006.md (p. 255). Then the qualification: "the metaphor of 'inscribing' morality suggests too much of a central steering role of the designer. Moralizing technology can never be the work of a 'prime mover' (cf. Smith, 2003), but comes down to establishing connections between three 'agents': the designer ... the user ... and the artefact itself, which can give rise to 'emergent' mediations" (p. 256), so that "the source of ethics needs to shift from the decision-making processes of autonomous individuals to well-reflected and well-designed alliances of humans with non-human entities" (p. 256). Coercive, persuasive and seductive technologies are distinguished (pp. 256–257), with Midden's feedback devices — "Users preserve the freedom to act as they think right, but are offered extra considerations which might lead them to change their behaviour" (p. 257) — and Eternally Yours (p. 257). *Ethics of use:* Foucault's late ethics makes the subject's "subject-ion" the place of ethics (p. 258); "Not an autonomous subject is the pivot of ethics here, but a mediated subject that finds a relation to this mediation" (p. 259); "Ethics of technology then consists in carefully assessing and experimenting with technological mediations, in order to explicitly help shape the way in which we are constituted as technologically mediated subjects" (p. 259). The ultrasound options — date-only scans, risk-only scans, all tests as an explicit choice, or refusal — are the worked "moral self-practices" (pp. 259–260). The sentence that carries the good-life telos into the use side: "Moral self-practices in a technological culture, in which human beings attempt to give a desirable shape to the technological mediation of their subjectivity, offer plenty of space for the virtue-ethical aspiration to the good life, the deontological ambition to meet moral norms, and the utilitarian goal to reach a preponderance of positive effects over negative effects" (p. 260). The conclusion reads technologies as *res publica*, Latour's "things" that "gather people around themselves; they are places where humans discuss the quality of the ways in which these things help to shape their lives" (p. 261), and closes: "In order to cultivate humanity, we need to take seriously how technologies also help to cultivate us" (p. 261).

## Relation to the protect/promote question (2026-09-19, F3 pass)

Verbeek's telos is promotive and he says so in his first sentence: the humanist ethics he rejects is the one that displaced "the good life," and the ethics he wants back is the virtue-ethical one whose question is the good life (pp. 241, 247). His instruments are promotive too. Designers are to shape mediations "in desirable ways" (p. 255) and to build "material infrastructures for morality" (p. 254); subjects are to give "a desirable shape" to their own mediation (p. 260). So the paper cannot list him as a protective account, and RESEARCH_PLAN_FLOURISHING.md §6 was right to call him the biggest risk to that version of the claim. What the chapter settles is the reformulated claim. Both of Verbeek's instruments are addressed to a single relation, the one between a subject and the artefact that mediates it: the design side works on "associations of subjects and objects" (p. 255) and "alliances of humans with non-human entities" (p. 256), and the use side works on "the technological mediation of *their* subjectivity" (p. 260, emphasis added). His three agents are designer, user and artefact (p. 256); the user is singular throughout; Midden's feedback devices inform "users" of their own consumption (p. 257); and the one case with two people in it, the expecting parents, treats them as one decision-maker facing one machine (p. 253) — the second party is the unborn, constituted by the technology as a patient, not a party whose interest the mediation trades against the parents'. Nothing in the chapter assigns the promotive work to the design of a relation between two people mediated by a third. That is the §6 verdict on the text: Verbeek's promotion is for one subject's good life, materialized by the designer and appropriated by that subject, and the "all five" claim survives in its reformulated form — "whatever the account's telos, none of the five assigns the promotive work to the design of the relation between the two coordinated people."

One correction this chapter forces on the plan's own wording. §6 describes Verbeek's promotion as "the designer's conception of the good, materialized once." The chapter explicitly denies the first half — inscription "suggests too much of a central steering role of the designer," and moralization "can never be the work of a 'prime mover'" (p. 256) — and verbeek2006.md's CTA augmentation democratizes the design decision. The paper should say "the designer–user–artefact alliance's conception of the good," or simply "one user's good," and keep "materialized once," which the adaptivity finding on verbeek2011.md supports (the artefact is designed, appropriated, and may produce emergent mediations, but no rule in it learns the person). A second correction is to verbeek2011.md's paraphrase of 2011 p. 84 — see that card's 2026-09-19 note.

## Caution

A 2009 chapter is not the 2011 book. The correspondence to chapters 2 and 4 rests on one near-verbatim snippet at 2011 p. 84, a Foucault snippet at p. 80, the chapter titles, and Verbeek's own citation chain (Krisis 2006 → this chapter → the book); the book may add material — the 2011 p. 84 snippet promises "'techniques of technology use'" in "the next chapter," a phrase this chapter does not use — and may revise wording. The Sloterdijk discussion (pp. 248–251) is the bulk of the chapter and is not summarized here beyond what the ethics needs. The chapter has no algorithmic system, no adaptive artefact, no platform, and no case in which one technology mediates between two people with separate interests; the ultrasound case is the closest and it is not that. Page numbers come from the mirrored PDF's running heads and were not checked against a printed copy.
