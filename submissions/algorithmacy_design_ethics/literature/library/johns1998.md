## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. The verified pages settle the direction of the book: fixity is Eisenstein's "most important" attribute (p. 10), and Johns's charge is that her account "neglects the labors through which success was achieved" (p. 19) and that fixity "was far rarer and harder to discern ... than most modern historians assume" (p. 36); the book's positive programme moves "from fixity to credit" (p. 28), i.e. from a property of the object to a judgement made by readers on the basis of conventions and reputations. On the working hypothesis, then: Johns supports the reading that the competence to live with print was built — by the Stationers' Company's conventions, by the patents regime's identification of "virtuous people with veracious printing," by practices of proofing, registration and licensing — and he would resist the alternative that institutions merely stabilized an affordance the press supplied, because for him the affordance was not reliably there to stabilize ("rarer and harder to discern"). The one qualification, stated more precisely in the 2002 reply (p. 120), is that he grants standardization "on first pass" as a consequence of printing; what was achieved was its reliability and its credit. That is the shape the paper's design principles need: credit, not fixity, is what a reader had to be competent to assess, and the institutions that made credit assessable are the analogue of counter-agents, refusal rights and bounded contracts. The book is entirely in the institutional register; it has nothing to say about cognition and nothing about literacy rates.

The scaffolding essay's use. Chapter 5's title, "The Cultural Construction of the Printing Revolution," and Johns's 2002 gloss that the concept of a printing revolution arrived c. 1800 (johns2002, p. 122), contradict the essay's "Print shattered that monopoly" as a fifteenth-century fact. The book's subject is how print became credible among the literate through institutions; it gives no support to "stripped of agency," and its account of the patents regime — "gentlemen patentees ... could produce works of higher quality and fidelity than would be possible in an environment of ruthless competition" (p. 258) — shows print's authority being *re-monopolized* by privilege, not a monopoly being shattered.

Only two pages were read in full and five in fragments; the Introduction and chapter 1 remain unread as wholes, and the quotations from pp. 5, 33, 258, 323 and 636 are Johns's own quotations of himself in 2002.
