# Suchman, L. (1994). Do categories have politics? The language/action perspective reconsidered. *Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW)*, 2(3), 177–190.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/BF00749015 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-deposited PDF at lancaster.ac.uk/fass/doc_library/sociology/Suchman_categories_1994.pdf, Kluwer page images with journal pagination; Springer page paywalled) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, the field's own journal; a critical essay, no empirical study · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** two humans + mediator (the Coordinator between coworkers), with the designer/manager as a fourth party who sets the categories · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Suchman takes the Winograd–Flores language/action perspective and its trademarked product, THE COORDINATOR, as the exemplary case of a class of CSCW systems "aimed at using system design as a mechanism for the prescription of a priori forms of social behavior" (p. 177), and argues that "the adoption of speech act theory as a foundation for system design, with its emphasis on the encoding of speakers' intentions into explicit categories, carries with it an agenda of discipline and control over organization members' actions" (p. 178). The paper opens under Winner's 1986 epigraph on technical things that "embody specific forms of power and authority," and its title copies his. The mechanism she describes is precise. Speech act theory after Searle needs an analyst to classify each utterance's illocutionary force, which conversation analysis (Schegloff, Goodwin, Bowers and Churcher) shows cannot be done from the utterance alone because intent is shaped interactionally as the hearer responds. The Coordinator "dispenses with this problem by enrolling speakers themselves in categorizing their utterances with explicit illocutionary tags" (p. 180): a user "performs a speech act using THE COORDINATOR by: selecting the illocutionary force from a small set of alternatives ... indicating the propositional content in text; and explicitly entering temporal relationships to other (past and anticipated) acts" (Winograd and Flores 1986, p. 159, quoted p. 180). The system then tracks the conversation-for-action state machine (Winograd and Flores' Figure 5.1, p. 64–65, reproduced as her Fig. 1), warns of breakdowns, and keeps "a record that can subsequently be invoked by organization members in calling each others' actions to account" (p. 181). Via Sacks's "Hotrodder" lecture and Foucault's disciplines, she reads the category scheme as a control device: "the machine thus becomes the instructor, the monitor of one's actions" (p. 181), and "specific occasions of conversation are no longer open to characterization in any other terms" once reduced to the diagram (p. 185). Two textual findings carry the argument. Flores et al. (1988, p. 173) say the Coordinator "has been most successful in organizations in which the users are relatively confident about their own position and the power they have within it," from which she concludes it is "a tool for the reproduction of an established social order" rather than "a tool for the collaborative production of social action" (p. 186). And the field reports she cites (Johnson et al. 1986; Bullen and Bennett 1990) show users "selectively appropriate and ignore aspects of the system in an ad hoc fashion," so its success may owe more to "practical adaptability" than to "theory-driven coherence" (p. 186). She closes by naming such systems "technologies of accountability ... in the sense represented by the bookkeeper's ledger" (p. 188) and by asking "who are 'we'? From what position do we claim or are we granted rights to design change?" (p. 187).

## Relation to the argument

This is the closest pre-platform case to the construct, and it is the single most important finding of this pass that it satisfies the **structural axis** and the **objective axis** only in a weaker form than a platform does — which is exactly what makes it useful. Structural axis: the Coordinator sits between two coworkers, interprets both (it holds each party's tagged illocutionary force and the temporal commitments), and commits determinations that bind both (the conversation state, the open-commitment ledger, the breakdown warning, the record later invoked "in calling each others' actions to account"). But the direct channel survives: coworkers can and, per the field reports she cites, do talk around it, and the system "enlists" the parties to do the interpretive coding themselves rather than inferring it. So it is a channel that formats rather than a party that interprets — the interpretation is delegated to the humans, the commitment is inscribed by the machine. Objective axis: Suchman's whole argument is that the system pursues an objective neither coworker set — a managerial one, "the reproduction of an established social order," administered through "standardized, universalistic categories ... implemented on the desktop" (p. 188). Her 1995 reply sharpens this: "there is no single structure of interactions or measure of effective coordination within an organization," so the designer's "effectiveness" is itself a third objective. On both axes, then, the Coordinator is an *early, partial* instance: two humans, a mediator with a script neither wrote, but with Φ arguably near zero because the humans do the interpreting and can bypass it. This is valuable as a boundary case for RQ9 and RQ6. It also shows that Winner's title format was already in CSCW thirty years ago, aimed at a *category scheme* rather than an object — Suchman's 1995 clarification that "the politics lie" in "who produces artifacts for, or on behalf of, whom, on what premises and with what stated objectives" (1995, p. 90) is a structural-relational reading of "politics," which is what RQ6 needs to move Winner from object to form. CUTS AGAINST in one respect: she attributes the politics to the *designer's categories and the managerial voice*, not to any irreducibility of the triad. On her account the remedy is participatory category-making and a recognition of "discordant voices," not a new user competence; the paper contains no notion that the co-worker on the other side needs a tactical skill to read what the mediator is doing with both of them. The construct's competence claim has no ancestor here.

## Caution

A textual critique, not a field study: she reads Winograd and Flores (1986) and Flores et al. (1988) and explicitly did not observe the Coordinator in use (Suchman 1995, p. 88). Winograd's reply disputes the reading (see winograd1994.md), and Suchman herself concedes in 1995 that she is "deeply undecided" whether artifacts "have politics" or are merely "used politically" (1995, p. 91). The Coordinator is a 1980s email-structuring tool: no learned model, no inference about the parties, no optimization target. Do not present it as an algorithmic intermediary; present it as the earliest CSCW instance of a mediator with a script neither party wrote.
