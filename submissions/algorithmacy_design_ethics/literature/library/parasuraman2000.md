# Parasuraman, R., Sheridan, T. B., & Wickens, C. D. (2000). A model for types and levels of human interaction with automation. *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics — Part A: Systems and Humans*, 30(3), 286–297.

## What it argues

Automation "is not all or none" but applies at a level, from manual to full, to each of four functions modelled on a four-stage account of human information processing: information acquisition, information analysis, decision and action selection, and action implementation (p. 287; Fig. 2, p. 288). Decision automation takes Sheridan's ten-point scale (Table I): at level 4 the computer suggests one alternative, at level 6 it gives the human "a limited time for a veto," and a GPWS sits at level 4 while a proposed automatic ground-collision avoidance system sits at level 7 (pp. 287, 289). The primary evaluative criterion is human performance — workload, situation awareness, complacency, skill degradation — and the secondary criteria are reliability and the cost of decision/action outcomes (pp. 290–292). The recommendation that follows is asymmetric: high levels of acquisition and analysis automation "can be pursued and implemented if the resulting system can be shown to be reliable," but for decision and action automation "high levels should be implemented only for low-risk situations" (p. 293). The opening examples are an anesthesiologist, an air-defence operator and "a securities analyst given various financial data who must judge whether to buy a large block of stocks" (p. 286). The paper flags that reliability figures such as ".997" conceal variance and that failures often arise "because the assumptions that are modeled in the automation by the designer are not met in a given operational situation" (p. 291).

## Relation to the argument

Structural axis, and this is the one classic source that puts a second human on the far side of the automation, though only for a paragraph. Discussing the interdependence of decision and action automation, the authors write that "currently an air traffic controller issues a verbal clearance to a pilot, who acknowledges and then executes a flight maneuver," but with datalink "the clearance (which itself may be a computer choice) could be uplinked and loaded in the aircraft's flight management system (FMS) automatically" and "the aircraft could then carry out the maneuver, without pilot intervention" (p. 293). That is controller → automation → pilot, with the automation choosing the clearance: interprets both, binds both. The model's response is telling. It does not ask whose objective the computer choice serves; it recommends keeping action automation low so that the pilot stays "actively pressing a button" — an "error-trapping" mechanism against "mindless acquiescence in computer-generated solutions" (p. 293). The triad is collapsed back into a dyad by treating the pilot as the last-stage check on *the automation's* error, and the controller drops out. The paper also records, in its situation-awareness cost, that "humans tend to be less aware of changes in environmental or system states when those changes are under the control of another agent (whether that agent is automation or another human)" (p. 291) — the other human and the automation are interchangeable loss-of-awareness sources, not co-principals. The stakeholder who sets the objective appears only as the designer who "automate[s] every subsystem that leads to an economic benefit for that subsystem" (p. 294), which is Parasuraman and Riley's abuse restated.

## Caution

A qualitative framework the authors call "not an exact science" and "a simple guide," with no data of its own; the empirical claims are cited from others. The ATC-datalink passage is one illustration inside a discussion of error trapping and the authors do not develop it as a multi-party structure, so citing it as a triad is our reading of their example, and it should be presented that way. The "securities analyst" example is a single decision-maker over data, not a market intermediary. Adaptive automation is explicitly set aside (p. 289). Levels of automation have since been contested (Endsley 2018 defends them; Dekker & Woods 2002 attack "MABA-MABA" allocation), and the four-stage model is itself called "almost certainly a gross simplification" (p. 287).
