**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/19312458.2021.1968361 (Crossref; open access per Unpaywall, but the Taylor & Francis PDF refused every download attempt and no repository copy was located — the VU Amsterdam record lists no file) · **Read depth:** secondary — not opened; the definition and the scale's structure are taken verbatim from Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025), pp. 684 and 691, who quote and describe it, and the abstract-level summary in the coordinative_sovereignty library (dogruel2022development.md) from Crossref and Semantic Scholar · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed methods journal; a two-phase scale-validation study (per the prior card: 331 then 1,041 German internet users, item response theory) · **Evidence basis:** secondary · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (an internet user and "algorithmic operations") · **Remedy locus:** subject (a measured literacy) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

As quoted by Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum, algorithm literacy is "being aware of the use of algorithms in online applications, platforms, and services, knowing how algorithms work, being able to critically evaluate algorithmic decision-making as well as having the skills to cope with or even influence algorithmic operations" (Dogruel et al. 2021: 4, in Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum 2025, p. 684). The validated instrument covers only the first two: it is "currently the most comprehensive attempt to measure literacy, capturing the dimensions of algorithmic awareness and algorithmic knowledge. Algorithmic awareness is measured using binary statements about whether a variety of communication technologies (e.g. Internet browsers) use algorithms to function. Algorithmic knowledge uses true/false statements to measure more nuanced aspects of algorithms, such as 'The use of algorithms which deliver personalized content can mean that the content you find is mostly consistent with your pre-existing opinions' and 'I can influence algorithms with my Internet usage behavior'" (Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum 2025, p. 691, citing the scale's online supplement). The reviewers note it "still breaks into two sub-scales that together do not measure algorithmic literacy as one cohesive construct" (p. 691), and that "measures of algorithmic literacy lie mostly in the cognitive dimension" (p. 695).

## Relation to the argument

RQ11, rival construct. The value of this card is the gap between the four-part *definition* (awareness, knowledge, critical evaluation, skills to cope with or influence) and the two-part *measure* (awareness, knowledge): the field's most cited scale operationalises the cognitive half and leaves the tactical half — the half nearest algorithmacy — unmeasured. The paper can use this to say that where the literacy field does name influence over an intermediary, it has not built an instrument for it, and that even the named influence runs from one user to "algorithmic operations" with no second party. The sovereignty chapter's audit (E8) already corrected a misdescription of this scale as measuring "skill"; the paper should not repeat it. CUTS AGAINST the supersession claim only in the definition's "influence" clause, which shows the field is aware that literacy might include acting on the system; the scale's own limits show it has not measured that.
