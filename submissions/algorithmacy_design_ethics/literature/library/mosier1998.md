# Mosier, K. L., Skitka, L. J., Heers, S., & Burdick, M. (1998). Automation bias: Decision making and performance in high-tech cockpits. *International Journal of Aviation Psychology*, 8(1), 47–63.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1207/s15327108ijap0801_3 · PMID 11540946 · **Read depth:** abstract_only (PubMed record, paraphrased by the fetch tool rather than returned verbatim; article paywalled at Taylor & Francis, no deposit found) plus secondary detail from Skitka, Mosier & Burdick (1999, pp. 994–995) and Parasuraman & Manzey (2010, pp. 392–393, 396), both read in full · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed aviation-psychology journal; simulator study with professional pilots · **Evidence basis:** secondary · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (one pilot, cockpit automation) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The study coined the omission/commission framing of automation bias and gave it its most-quoted numbers. As Skitka et al. (1999, p. 994) describe it, 25 pilots flew a part-task simulation with B747-400-like displays from Los Angeles to San Francisco and on to Sacramento. Four events offered omission errors — an altitude-clearance misload, a heading change that loaded correctly but executed wrongly, a frequency-change misload, and a tracking-automation failure — each detectable on traditional gauges; one event offered a commission error, an EICAS engine-fire message "contradicted by normal engine parameters and the absence of any other indicators," with pilots reminded in training that five other indicators would accompany a real fire. The omission rate was 55%, related to flight hours such that "increased experience decreased the likelihood of catching automation failures"; "virtually all participants shut down the engine," a 100% commission rate, though the same pilots said afterwards that an EICAS message alone would not justify a shutdown; and 67% "had a false memory of at least one additional cue that was not actually present" (pp. 994). Parasuraman and Manzey (2010, p. 396) add that the paper observed omission errors varying with how accountable pilots felt for the automated tasks, and the PubMed abstract says that the experimentally manipulated accountability demands "did not significantly impact performance," while post hoc analyses found that pilots reporting "an internalized perception of 'accountability'" were "significantly more likely to double-check automated functioning against other cues and less likely to commit errors."

## Relation to the argument

Objective axis, as the strongest evidence in the classic corpus that experts follow an automated verdict against their own training and against contradicting raw data, and reconstruct memory to fit it — the interpretive failure the construct's "infer what the intermediary is doing" is meant to name. On this pass's question the answer is a pure dyad: one pilot, one automation, a simulated aircraft, no counterpart. The follow-up crew study (Mosier et al. 2001, 48 pilots, reported in Parasuraman & Manzey 2010, p. 397) found that adding a second crewmember made no difference, and that second crewmember shares the pilot's goal. Nothing in the design lets the automation serve anyone but the crew.
