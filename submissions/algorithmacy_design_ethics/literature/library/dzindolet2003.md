# Dzindolet, M. T., Peterson, S. A., Pomranky, R. A., Pierce, L. G., & Beck, H. P. (2003). The role of trust in automation reliance. *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*, 58(6), 697–718.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/S1071-5819(03)00038-7 · **Read depth:** abstract_only (verbatim abstract from the Consensus/Semantic Scholar index; article paywalled at Elsevier, no deposit found) plus secondary mentions in Lee & See (2004, pp. 66–67, 69) and Parasuraman & Manzey (2010, p. 392), both read in full · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed HCI journal; three laboratory experiments · **Evidence basis:** secondary · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (one participant, one decision aid) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Three experiments "with Cameron University students" in which "participants viewed slides of Fort Sill terrain and indicated the presence or absence of a camouflaged soldier" with the help of an automated decision aid. The abstract's findings: "Participants initially considered the automated decision aid trustworthy and reliable. After observing the automated aid make errors, participants distrusted even reliable aids, unless an explanation was provided regarding why the aid might err. Knowing why the aid might err increased trust in the decision aid and increased automation reliance, even when the trust was unwarranted." The authors frame this inside Parasuraman and Riley's misuse/disuse and call for "valid and reliable self-report measures of trust in automation." Lee and See (2004, p. 67) describe the companion Dzindolet et al. (2002) framework as combining "cognitive, social, and motivational processes" to explain reliance, and (p. 69) cite the group's finding that "initial expectations of perfect automation performance can lead to disuse when people find the automation to be imperfect"; Parasuraman and Manzey (2010, p. 392) report from the 2002 paper that most participants expected an automated aid to outperform a human aid.

## Relation to the argument

Objective axis, through explanation. The abstract's last clause is the sharp one: an explanation of *why the aid might err* raised trust and reliance "even when the trust was unwarranted." That is a finding about explanations as trust levers rather than as calibration aids, and it bears on any transparency affordance — telling a party how the intermediary works can raise reliance without raising appropriateness. On this pass's question: a dyad. The comparison with a human aid belongs to the sister 2002 paper, and there the human is the alternative trustee, not a co-principal on the far side of the automation. The Fort Sill target-detection task has one observer, one aid and no counterpart whose interests the aid could also serve.

## Caution

Abstract-only; the 2002 framework findings are reported from later reviews, not from the 2003 paper. Sample sizes, reliability levels, the form of the "explanation" manipulation and the trust measures are all unread. Students on a military target-detection task; the aid's objective is the participant's own.
