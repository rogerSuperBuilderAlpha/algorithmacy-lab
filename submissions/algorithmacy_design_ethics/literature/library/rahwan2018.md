# Rahwan, I. (2018). Society-in-the-loop: Programming the algorithmic social contract. *Ethics and Information Technology*, 20(1), 5–14.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/s10676-017-9430-8 (Crossref: vol. 20, issue 1, pp. 5–14, March 2018) · **Read depth:** full_text of the author's preprint (arXiv:1707.07232v2, 26 July 2017, "This version: 20 July 2017," headed "To appear in 'Ethics of Information Technology' – manuscript No. TBD"); the Springer version of record was not opened (not open access), so section numbers below are the preprint's and quotations should be checked against the published pagination · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed journal; a conceptual essay, no empirical study · **Evidence basis:** direct_read (preprint) · **Parties modeled:** designer–stakeholders — "various human stakeholders" whose values a governing algorithm must embody, versus a "human controller" in HITL; the SITL figure explicitly draws "Stakeholders with conflicting interests and values" facing one "AI System with contested function" · **Remedy locus:** adoption / relation — the remedy is a social contract negotiated among stakeholders and monitored by society, layered over the system; not a competence and not an artifact property · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

"I argue that we need tools to program, debug and maintain an algorithmic social contract, a pact between various human stakeholders, mediated by machines... I propose an agenda I call society-in-the-loop (SITL), which combines the HITL control paradigm with mechanisms for negotiating the values of various stakeholders affected by AI systems, and monitoring compliance with the agreement. In short, 'SITL = HITL + Social Contract'" (abstract). Human-in-the-loop, from Sheridan's supervisory control, puts one operator over one system with "uncontested and common goals." The move to SITL "raises a fundamentally different problem: how to balance the competing interests of different stakeholders, including the interests of those who govern through algorithms? This is, traditionally, a problem of defining a social contract" (§3). Figure 3 contrasts the two: in HITL "a human controller ensures that the AI system fulfills uncontested and common goals on behalf of societal stakeholders"; in SITL "society must agree on two aspects": "tradeoffs between the different values that AI systems can strive towards" and "which stakeholders would reap which benefits and pay which costs" (§5). Citing Citron and Pasquale, "[n]ew algorithmic decisionmakers are sovereign over important aspects of individual lives," so the aim is "to build institutions and tools that put the society-in-the-loop of algorithmic systems" (§§5, 8). The "SITL gap" has three parts: articulating values across the humanities–engineering divide, "quantifying externalities & negotiating tradeoffs," and "verifying compliance" (§6). Bridges include crowdsourcing values (the Moral Machine, "over 30 million decisions"), computational social choice and contractarian algorithms for negotiation, and compliance by "people watching algorithms" and "algorithms watching algorithms" (§7). On transparency: "this does not mean having access to computer source code... Transparency must, therefore, be about the external behavior of algorithms. Indeed, this is how we regulate the behavior of humans—not by looking into their brain's neural circuitry, but by observing their behavior" (§7.3). Auditing faces a "generalized 'defeat device'" problem — an audited algorithm "may attempt to trick the algorithm doing the auditing" — so monitoring must be continuous and in the wild (§7.3). The Etzionis' "oversight programs" and browser plug-ins that audit ad platforms are the "algorithms watching algorithms" examples (§7.4). SITL "operates at different time-scales than HITL. It looks more like public feedback on regulations and legislations, than feedback on frequent micro-level decisions" (§8).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8, closing the lead that tsamados2022.md flagged. Rahwan is the ethics-side author who already writes the intermediary as a party among parties — a "pact between various human stakeholders, mediated by machines," with "conflicting interests" and an objective that is "contested" — so the paper cannot say the field lacks a multi-party frame; it must say what SITL leaves out. Sorted: **grounds the why, not an instrument** — SITL is a governance layer at legislative time scale, explicitly "not feedback on frequent micro-level decisions," and its human is a citizen in a polity, not a party in a coordination. The construct's algorithmacy operates at exactly the micro level Rahwan sets aside: the moment of a determination that binds two people, where no social contract clause reaches. Two of his sub-points carry over as support for Principle I. His behavioural-transparency argument (regulate by observing conduct, not by reading circuitry) is the same ground on which mechanistic_interpretability_method.md finds the Context Engine misplaced, stated by an AI-governance author. And "algorithms watching algorithms" — oversight programs and the ad-audit plug-ins — is the nearest thing in this essay to a counter-agent, though his auditor works for society, not for a party. CUTS AGAINST the paper if it claims novelty for the idea that the mediator's objective is negotiable among those it binds; Rahwan says that in 2017. The paper's distinct claim must be the *locus*: the negotiation happens once, in the contract, for Rahwan; it happens continuously, in the relation, for the construct.

## Caution

A programmatic essay with no cases beyond illustrations (autonomous vehicles, news filtering, credit scoring); the only data cited are the author's own Moral Machine counts. The preprint, not the version of record, was read. "Stakeholders" is left undifferentiated — passengers and pedestrians, citizens and governments — so the two-humans-plus-mediator triad is present only as a special case of a many-party polity. The essay predates large language models and platform-labour scholarship, and its examples of "algorithms watching algorithms" are 2016 advertising audits.
