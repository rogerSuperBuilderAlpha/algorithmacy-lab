## What it argues / what it does

Mechanistic interpretability discloses the internal computation of a trained neural network to someone who holds its weights. Olah and colleagues state the programme as three "speculative claims": features — "directions" in a layer's activation space, often single neurons — "are the fundamental unit of neural networks"; features "are connected by weights, forming circuits," where a circuit is "a computational subgraph of a neural network"; and "analogous features and circuits form across models and tasks." What the method yields is concrete and small. In InceptionV1, a vision classifier, they show curve detectors assembled from earlier line detectors, high–low frequency detectors, a pose-invariant dog-head detector built from eye, snout and fur features, and a car detector reading wheels at the bottom of its window and windows at the top. Their own summary of the payoff is exact: "Once you understand what features they're connecting together, the individual floating point number weights in your neural network become meaningful! You can literally read meaningful algorithms off of the weights."

Elhage and colleagues carry the same programme to language models. They restrict themselves to "transformers with two layers or less which have only attention blocks," rewrite the forward pass so that the residual stream becomes a "communication channel" that heads read from and write to, split each attention head into a query-key circuit (which token attends to which) and an output-value circuit (what gets copied), and identify "induction heads," which appear only at two layers and implement a copy-the-pattern form of in-context learning. The stated purpose is safety: reverse engineering "the detailed computations performed by transformers, similar to how a programmer might try to reverse engineer complicated binaries into human-readable source code," so as to give "a more systematic approach to explaining current safety problems, identifying new ones."

Bereska and Gavves define the field in the same terms for their TMLR review: "reverse engineering the computational mechanisms and representations learned by neural networks into human-understandable algorithms and concepts to provide a granular, causal understanding." Their Definition 1 makes features "the fundamental units of neural network representations that cannot be further decomposed into simpler independent factors." The review organizes the field around features, circuits, motifs, and the linear-representation, superposition and universality hypotheses, then assesses relevance to safety under understanding, control and alignment, with capability gains and dual use as the risks.

To whom the disclosure runs is unambiguous in all three texts. Olah and colleagues frame the audience as "the interpretability community" and propose "natural science" as its evaluation paradigm: "neural networks are an object of empirical investigation, perhaps similar to an organism in biology." The reader who benefits is the person at the microscope. Elhage and colleagues mention users exactly once, and as people who are surprised: "both creators and users routinely discover model capabilities – including problematic behaviors – they were previously unaware of." In the 216,000-character text of the Bereska and Gavves review, *stakeholder*, *end-user*, *consumer*, *company*, *regulator* and *third party* occur zero times; *user(s)* occurs twice, both inside a glossary entry on sycophancy; *deploy* occurs once, in a glossary entry on "microscope AI"; *audit* occurs once, in a bibliography entry. The field's own review does not have a vocabulary for the parties around the model.

## Relation to the argument

Mechanistic interpretability does not address the triadic problem the construct names, and cannot, for three reasons that are structural rather than a matter of maturity.

First, its object is the wrong thing. The method discloses what a network computes — which directions in activation space respond to what, and how weights connect them. The construct's intermediary "pursues an objective neither of them set," and that objective lives in the training signal, the ranking or matching rule, and the deploying organization's product decisions, not in the forward pass. Circuits can show that a model has an engagement-prediction feature; they do not show that a firm chose to rank by it, or why. Olah and colleagues are explicit that the method's rigour comes precisely from restricting scope: "the cost of this rigor is that statements about circuits are much smaller in scope than overall model behavior."

Second, the second human is not in the frame. Nothing in the method represents "the other party's interests" as an object that could be read out. Every diagram in the three texts has one investigator and one model. The construct's defining feature — a determination that binds two people at once, run through a third objective — has no counterpart in the method's ontology, which contains features, weights and circuits and nothing else.

Third, and most damaging to the Gemini report's proposal, the access model runs the wrong way. Reading circuits requires white-box access to weights. That access sits with the intermediary's owner. Even if mechanistic interpretability could recover an objective from weights, it would disclose it to the one party who already set it. Neither coordinating human can run it, and neither is the reader the method addresses. An "AI Context Engine" built on this method is therefore a dyadic instrument — one auditor, one model — and its proposed use as a remedy for a person coordinating with another person through an opaque intermediary misapplies it. The method is a microscope; the construct needs something closer to a right of cross-examination.

The verdict is not that mechanistic interpretability is weak. It is that it answers a different question — what does this model compute — for a different reader — whoever holds the weights. If the harness's other passes confirm that the design-ethics and human-factors literatures also assume a dyad, this card supplies the technical half of the finding: the remedies proposed for AI opacity are dyadic all the way down.

## Caution

The three texts read here are foundational and definitional, not the frontier. Later Anthropic work (attribution graphs, "circuit tracing," 2025) extends the method to production-scale models, and I did not read it for this card; it changes scale, not the party structure. Olah and colleagues studied a vision model, and Elhage and colleagues studied toy attention-only transformers of at most two layers, disclaiming any application to large models in the same paper. Bereska and Gavves is a review; its term counts show what the field talks about, not what it forbids. Nothing here rules out an organization commissioning an interpretability audit and publishing the result; that would be a governance arrangement layered on the method, and it is not what the method or any of these texts proposes. The card also does not claim the three texts oppose a triadic reading — they simply never raise it.
