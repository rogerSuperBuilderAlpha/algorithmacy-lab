## What it argues

The note reports design lessons from CORE-MI, a system that transcribes recorded motivational-interviewing sessions and scores the therapist against fidelity codes learned from prior human ratings, and it names "contestability" as "a new principle for designing systems that evaluate human behavior" (Abstract). Three design issues structure it. Incentives: how to get clinicians to correct transcripts and ratings without gamifying professional work. Legibility rather than transparency: deep models are "inherently obscure," so the aim becomes a system that "can be deciphered and interpreted," via Höök's "black box in a glass box" and "sandboxing techniques that allow users to probe models with various inputs to investigate their effects on predictions" — for example letting a therapist alter talk-time ratios or edit a transcript to watch the efficacy score move (From Transparency to Legibility). Contestation: counsellors already object to being "judged by a machine," and "unlike a human supervisor, a trainee cannot inquire about the ratings, nor engage the evaluator about their assessment." The authors then relocate the problem: correction works for discrete errors, but "there will also be a need for more nuanced and substantial argumentation ... particularly important when the user cannot simply register disagreement with the system, but rather, must make arguments to powerful actors whose decisions are informed by those systems. Think, for example, of a mental health provider whose clinical supervisor relies on automated assessment technologies to evaluate performance, or an insurance company that employs machine learning algorithms in determining whether to cover an addiction treatment program" (Facilitating Contestation). Four strategies follow: improve accuracy through phased deployment with students; make models legible with explanations, confidence scores and the ability to "unpack aggregate measures, tracing system predictions all the way down to the transcript level"; build training modules with sandboxing; and stay vigilant, "providing mechanisms for users to ask questions and record disagreements" and watching for aggregate effects "that may not be apparent to individual users," citing Sweeney's ad-delivery study (Design for Contestability). They invoke Verbeek's mediation theory to say the technology "can shape relationships between therapists, supervisors, insurance providers, patients," under "significant financial and organizational pressures" to "rationalize" mental health care.

## Relation to the argument

Contestability enters the design literature here already in the construct's shape. The evaluated party does not argue with the machine; she argues through it to a second human whose decision the machine's score informs — the intermediary interprets one side, commits a determination that binds her before the other, and does so under an objective (rationalised care) neither of them set. The strategies map onto the affordances unevenly: sandboxing is Counterfactual/Branching Exploration, tracing scores to transcript lines is Modular Visual Chunking joined to Seamful Boundary Disclosure, and confidence scores are a weak Cognitive Forcing Function. The remainder — phased deployment, training modules, aggregate monitoring "on the behalf of vulnerable users" — is process and institution, not interface, which is the point RQ5 needs: contestability-by-design starts as an interface principle and immediately overflows it. The note also supplies the construct's anticipatory component from the operator's side, since the authors design against a future misuse they expect. It does not cut against any affordance, but it shows that "contest what it commits" requires a channel to the other human that no interface product alone provides.

## Caution

A design note about one system in one domain with no evaluation ("a formal user evaluation is currently underway"); the contestation concerns are reported from information sessions, not from a study, and the authors say the misuse concern "is somewhat speculative." Three of the five authors are co-founders and equity holders of Lyssn.io, the company commercialising CORE-MI. The therapist is an expert user who is also the decision subject; nothing is said about the patient, who is the party most indirectly bound.
