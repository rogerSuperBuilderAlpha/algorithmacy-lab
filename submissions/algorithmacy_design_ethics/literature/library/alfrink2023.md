# Alfrink, K., Keller, I., Kortuem, G., & Doorn, N. (2023). Contestable AI by design: Towards a framework. *Minds and Machines*, 33(4), 613–639.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/s11023-022-09611-z · **Read depth:** full_text (final published version deposited in the TU Delft repository, open access CC-BY; the extracted text carries no page numbers, so quotes are located by section) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed journal (Minds and Machines); systematic literature review synthesised into a design framework · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** designer–stakeholders, with an actor model of developers, human controllers, decision subjects and third parties · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Contestable AI systems are "open and responsive to human intervention throughout their lifecycle: not only after an automated decision has been made, but also during its design and development" (§1). From a Scopus search of 2016–2021 papers on design, contestation and AI, plus snowballing, the authors extract five system features — built-in safeguards, interactive control over decisions, explanations, human review and intervention requests, tools for scrutiny — and six development practices — ex-ante safeguards, agonistic ML development, quality assurance during and after deployment, risk mitigation, third-party oversight (§6). The conceptual spine comes from four sources: Hirsch et al.'s "humans challenging machine predictions"; Vaccaro et al.'s contestability as "a deep system property" and a form of procedural justice; Almada's ex-ante "contestability by design," which Hildebrandt's "agonistic debate" about "data and modeling choices made to represent decision subjects" underwrites; and Sarra's demand for "an articulate act of defense" inside a "procedural relationship," so that "a 'human in the loop' is insufficient if there is no possibility of a 'dialectical exchange'" (§2). The feature descriptions are the usable part: "Reliance on out-of-system mechanisms for contestation is insufficient"; explanations should shift "from explaining the computational rules to the decision rules," should include "a justification as well" to avoid "the self-production of norms," and may use "sandboxing" so users "manipulate inputs and see how these affect outputs"; controllers who answer intervention requests "should have the authority and capability to actually change previous decisions"; contestation "cannot be handled in a fully automated fashion, because a dialectic exchange is not possible in a meaningful sense between humans and machines," since "computational logic can only offer an answer to the 'how'"; and mechanisms "should allow for collective action, because harms can be connected to group membership" (§6.1). The discussion states the ethic: "avoid attempts to at all cost resolve disputes up front before they arise using some form of compromise or consensus-seeking. Instead, we accept that controversy is at times inevitable ... Stakeholders do not need to agree on every decision ... However, stakeholders do need to agree on procedures by which such disagreements will be resolved" (§7).

## Relation to the argument

This is the design tradition that already owns "contest," and its stance answers RQ5's second question: contestability-by-design is agonistic where VSD is consensual. It does not ask whose values win; it asks that the losing party retain an arena and a procedure, which is a different relation to cooptation than adjudication — it presumes the operator's objective may be contested rather than settled. Against the four affordances the map is partial. Interactive control and explanations with sandboxing, confidence levels and traceable decision chains correspond to Counterfactual/Branching Exploration, Seamful Boundary Disclosure and Modular Visual Chunking; the framework then places those products inside a lifecycle whose other features — intervention requests, tools for scrutiny — and all six practices are procedural and organisational. So the affordances are contained by this framework, not rivalled by it, and the framework says in its own words why interface-level products cannot finish the job: the "why" requires a human on the other end. The actor model still has no co-determined counterpart; the dyad is controller and subject, with third parties acting for subjects. Under cooptation the human controller who must have authority to reverse decisions is an agent of the party whose objective is in dispute, and the framework's only answer to that is third-party oversight and ex-ante agreement on procedure. That gap is where the Φ structural reading adds something this literature lacks.

## Caution

A synthesis "developed based on a small sample of academic papers," most of them "not based on empirically validated interventions" and "still 'context-free'"; the framework is offered for validation that "was not part of this paper." The authors restrict it to significant-impact, low time-pressure decisions and to settings like OECD local government, and exclude lethal autonomous weapons and shared vehicle control. The volume and page numbers above follow the TU Delft cover sheet; the article itself carries an online-first DOI and the "13" running marks of a Springer proof.
