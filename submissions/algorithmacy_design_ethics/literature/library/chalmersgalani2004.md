# Chalmers, M., & Galani, A. (2004). Seamful interweaving: Heterogeneity in the theory and design of interactive systems. *Proceedings of the 5th Conference on Designing Interactive Systems (DIS '04)*, 243–252. ACM.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/1013115.1013149 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-deposited PDF, University of Glasgow eprints 3457; not paginated to the proceedings, so citations are by section) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed conference (ACM DIS); theory paper grounded in one mixed-reality user trial · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology–world, extended to several co-present/remote humans coordinating through heterogeneous media (three museum co-visitors) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

"A narrow design focus on one tool or medium as primary may clash with the way that everyday activity involves the interweaving and combination of many heterogeneous media. Interaction may become seamless and unproblematic, even if the differences, boundaries and 'seams' in media are objectively perceivable. People accommodate and take advantage of seams and heterogeneity, in and through the process of interaction" (abstract). The paper takes Weiser's ideal of the tool that is "literally visible, effectively invisible" (attributed to Weiser's 1994 UIST talk "Creating the invisible interface," their ref. 35) and Dourish's embodied interaction, and argues both "swing from one extreme to the other," neglecting that "ready-to-hand embodied interaction and present-at-hand objectification are interdependent" (§ Heterogeneity and ubiquity): breakdown, learning, and "the task 'is' the tool itself" all make the tool present-at-hand, and "we have to expect that a new technology will be to some degree present-at-hand, no matter how well the designer aims towards embodied ... interaction." The Mack Room trial (Equator City project): three co-visitors to a Mackintosh exhibition — one on-site with an ultrasonically tracked handheld showing a map, one on the web with a 2D map, one in a 3D VR — shared audio and each other's positions, with content and spatial representation "deliberately heterogeneous." Visitors built "hybrid objects" out of mismatched representations, coined shared terms the designer had not supplied ("boomerang" for the time-line wall), and "did build up a shared understanding of these 'seams' in the infrastructure and how to handle them"; small content differences "led to greater engagement with the exhibition." Design guidelines: precise positioning is not always needed; "overlap in content and presentation should be substantial but not necessarily total"; make past activity across media a resource; and "designers may consider selectively and carefully revealing differences and limitations of systems, in ways that support social interaction i.e. seamful design. We can show a person's sensed position as a spatial extent rather than as a point" — "seamful systems whose underlying infrastructural mechanisms are 'literally visible, effectively invisible', in that everyday interaction does not require attention to these mechanisms' representations — but one can selectively focus on and reveal them when the task is to understand or even change the infrastructure." Conclusion: "accommodation and appropriation [is] a process that designers contribute to by selectively revealing system structures, and affordances for their potential use, but it is users who through their interactions with our system and with each other choose what to use and why."

## Relation to the argument

This is the design-side origin of the seams vocabulary that Ehsan et al. (2024) inherit through Inman and Ribes, and reading it corrects two things the later lineage blurs. First, the seam here is *infrastructural* (GPS accuracy, wireless coverage, ultrasonic tracking error), and its purpose is to let users *understand or change the infrastructure*; Ehsan's seam is an *assumption mismatch* revealed for contestability. The construct's fourth affordance sits closer to Ehsan, but Chalmers and Galani supply what Ehsan lacks: a demonstration that seams are handled *socially* — the Mack Room visitors repaired positional mismatch by talking to each other. That is the triadic point the RQ2 brief anticipated as "asymmetric seamfulness": a seam revealed to one co-visitor became a shared resource *because a direct channel (audio) existed*. Remove the audio and each visitor's seam is private; the paper's own finding is that seamful design works through the direct channel, which a Φ>0 intermediary has by definition removed. That is a usable argument that seamful disclosure in the triad needs a counterpart channel or it degrades into the dyadic XAI case. Second, their closing line assigns the *choice* of what to use to users, and the *revealing* to designers — a division that matches the construct's competence claim on the user side but leaves the designer as the party who decides which seams exist. CUTS AGAINST: the paper is about heterogeneity among media and among *peers*; the "system" has no objective and no interpretive stance toward the visitors, and it never commits a determination binding them. Their seams are honest gaps in sensing, not strategic concealments (Inman and Ribes add that word later). Nothing here supports reading a platform's disclosure as a seam in their sense unless the disclosure is of an infrastructural limit.

## Caution

One trial, three participants per session, museum setting, no quantitative results; the excerpts are illustrative conversation fragments. The "literally visible, effectively invisible" quotation is attributed to a 1994 invited talk, not to the 1991 *Scientific American* paper (see weiser1991.md), and the phrase "beautiful seams" does not appear in this paper. Their earlier "Seamful Design: Showing the Seams in Wearable Computing" (IEE Eurowearable 2003) is the first use of the term and was not read.
