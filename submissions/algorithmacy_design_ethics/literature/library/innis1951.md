## What it argues

Innis's unit of analysis is the medium and the institution that monopolizes it, not the mind. The programmatic statement: "A medium of communication has an important influence on the dissemination of knowledge over space and over time and it becomes necessary to study its characteristics in order to appraise its influence in its cultural setting. According to its characteristics it may be better suited to the dissemination of knowledge over time than over space, particularly if the medium is heavy and durable and not suited to transportation, or to the dissemination of knowledge over space than over time, particularly if the medium is light and easily transported. The relative emphasis on time or space will imply a bias of significance to the culture in which it is imbedded" (p. 33). And: "We can perhaps assume that the use of a medium of communication over a long period will to some extent determine the character of knowledge to be communicated and suggest that its pervasive influence will eventually create a civilization in which life and flexibility will become exceedingly difficult to maintain and that the advantages of a new medium will become such as to lead to the emergence of a new civilization" (p. 34).

The operative concept is the "monopoly of knowledge," which is explicitly a matter of who controls a script and its institutions. "A complex system of writing becomes the possession of a special class and tends to support aristocracies. A simple flexible system of writing admits of adaptation to the vernacular but slowness of adaptation facilitates monopolies of knowledge and hierarchies. Reading in contrast with writing implies a passive recognition of the power of writing. Inventions in communication compel realignments in the monopoly or the oligopoly of knowledge" (p. 4). "Monopolies of knowledge to an important extent dominated by priestly organization and protected by complex types of script such as the cuneiform and the hieroglyphic checked the growth of political organization. Escape from these monopolies came from the fringes of Babylonian and Egyptian civilizations in which new languages among primitive peoples demanded simplicity" (p. 38). Greece is his counter-case: "No energy was lost in learning a second language and monopolies of knowledge could not be built around a complex script" (p. 41); the oral tradition "bent the consonantal alphabet to its demands" (p. 40); yet "The written tradition had brought the vitality of the oral tradition to an end" (p. 44). On the medieval-to-print sequence his account is institutional throughout: "Parchment became the medium through which a monopoly of knowledge was built up by religion. This monopoly of knowledge invited the competition of a new medium, namely paper" (p. 50); "Monopolies of knowledge controlled by monasteries were followed by monopolies of knowledge controlled by copyist guilds in the large cities. The high price for large books led to attempts to develop a system of reproduction by machine and to the invention of printing in Germany which was on the margin of the area dominated by copyists" (p. 53); "Commercialism of the publisher began to displace the craft of the printer. The vernacular offered new authors and new readers" (p. 53). Print, for Innis, is not the end of monopoly but a new one: "The monopoly of knowledge centring around the printing press brought to an end the obsession with space and the neglect of problems of continuity and time" (p. 60); "The monopoly of knowledge which emerged with technological advances in the printing industry and insistence on freedom of the press checked this development" (p. 80); "The printing press supported the Reformation and destroyed the monopoly of the church over time" (p. 72). One sentence bears directly on competence as screening: "The spread of writing widened the base by which the screening of ability could take place. The feudal hierarchy of Greece was weakened by an emphasis on writing which became a type of intelligence test. A writing age was essentially an egoistic age" (p. 9). His normative plea is for the oral tradition against print's bias: "a plea for consideration of the role of the oral tradition as a basis for a revival of effective vital discussion" (p. 32).

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. Innis is the cleanest institutional-register source in the set. His claims are about which class holds a script, which organization is built on a medium, and how a new medium breaks or replaces a monopoly; he makes no claim that a medium rewires cognition. He therefore strengthens the analogy at exactly the level the paper needs: "monopoly of knowledge" is a description of a coordination order, and "inventions in communication compel realignments" (p. 4) is the medium-shift thesis without the great-divide freight. Two features complicate the paper's use. First, Innis's monopolies are broken from the margins, by force and by cheaper media, not by teaching the excluded the incumbent competence — the Greeks escaped Egyptian priestly monopoly because they had no complex script to learn (p. 41). That favours the paper's structural remedies over a literacy-style pedagogy. Second, print for Innis produces its own monopoly (pp. 60, 80), so the sequence oracy → literacy → algorithmacy would, on his account, be a sequence of monopolies, each of which must be counterbalanced rather than one being replaced by an emancipation.

The scaffolding essay's use. Innis half-supports the essay's "clerical/scribal monopoly" framing and then breaks it. He does describe medieval parchment as a monopoly of knowledge "built up by religion" (p. 50), which the essay can cite. But he inserts a stage the essay omits — the "copyist guilds in the large cities" (p. 53) — and he denies that print ended monopoly; it created a new "monopoly of knowledge centring around the printing press" (p. 60) run by publishers and later by newspapers. Nothing in Innis supports "stripped of agency"; his interest is in which institution holds the medium, not in the individual reader's standing. The essay's "Print shattered that monopoly" should read, on Innis's evidence, "print displaced one monopoly with another."

## Caution

These are lecture-essays that move across five millennia in sixty pages with dated sources (Childe 1946, Kroeber 1946, Trevelyan 1942); the historical specifics (dates for the Egyptian calendar, the Hyksos, Solon) are not reliable at the level of detail and should not be cited as facts. Innis writes no history of literacy rates or of the illiterate. "Monopoly of knowledge" is used loosely and never defined. The essay's closing bias toward the oral tradition is a normative position, not a finding. Pagination confirmed from the scan's running heads; the reprint keeps the 1951 pagination.
