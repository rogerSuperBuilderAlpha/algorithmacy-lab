**Identifier:** Commission newsroom doc_id=60419 (made public 8 April 2019) · **Read depth:** full_text (41 PDF pages, downloaded on the author's request 2026-10-25; quotations checked word for word against the PDF text; printed page = PDF page − 2) · **Source-tier:** policy guidance (soft law; "Nothing in this document shall create legal rights nor impose legal obligations towards third parties," p. 6) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** developers, deployers, end-users and "other impacted groups (those who may not purchase or use an AI system but for whom decisions are made by an AI system, and society at large)" (p. 23) · **Remedy locus:** institution and artifact, with rights addressed to the person (contest, redress, explanation on demand); the override sits with the humans operating the system · **Relation last checked:** 2026-10-25

## What it argues

Trustworthy AI is lawful, ethical and robust. Four principles (respect for human autonomy, prevention of harm, fairness, explicability) become seven requirements: human agency and oversight; technical robustness and safety; privacy and data governance; transparency; diversity, non-discrimination and fairness; societal and environmental well-being; accountability, "Including auditability, minimisation and reporting of negative impact, trade-offs and redress" (p. 14). Procedural fairness "entails the ability to contest and seek effective redress against decisions made by AI systems and by the humans operating them," which requires that "the entity accountable for the decision must be identifiable" (p. 13). Where a system has "a significant impact on people's lives, it should be possible to demand a suitable explanation" (p. 18). Human-in-command includes "the ability to override a decision made by a system" (p. 16). The document warns that "transparency cannot prevent non-discrimination or ensure fairness" and asks, for scoring, for opting out "without detriment" or else "mechanisms for challenging and rectifying the scores," especially "where an asymmetry of power exists between the parties" (p. 34). "Certification can however never replace responsibility" (p. 23).

## Relation to the argument

The text Floridi and Cowls ([`floridicowls2019.md`](floridicowls2019.md)) say inherits the five principles, read for itself as that card asks. It goes further toward the third party than the principles papers: it names the person "for whom decisions are made" without being a user (p. 23) and the business-consumer asymmetry (p. 12). The rights it gives that person are to ask: to contest, to demand an explanation, to seek redress. The controls (override, oversight, audit) sit with the operator (p. 16). The person is the addressee of a right, not the holder of an instrument. That is the census pattern in its strongest current form, and its opt-out "without detriment" (p. 34) is the nearest the establishment texts come to a counter-delegated control. Mentxaka et al. ([`mentxaka2026.md`](mentxaka2026.md)) map onto these requirements; cite the guidelines rather than their paraphrase.

## Caution

Soft law: it obliges no one (p. 6). The opt-out passage is limited to scoring and hedged with "ideally" (p. 34). Membership includes Floridi, Coeckelbergh, Google, IBM and SAP; Microsoft is not listed (p. 39).
