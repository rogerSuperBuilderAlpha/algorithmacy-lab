# Mittelstadt, B. (2019). Principles alone cannot guarantee ethical AI. *Nature Machine Intelligence*, 1(11), 501–507.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1038/s42256-019-0114-4 · **Read depth:** full_text (the arXiv:1906.06668 preprint, 19 pp. including references and two tables, extracted with pdftotext and read whole; the VoR is paywalled; page numbers are the preprint's) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed Perspective; a single-authored argument by analogy to medical principlism, no empirical work · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** developers, users and "affected parties" / "data subjects," with the medical client–practitioner relation as comparator; a developer–subject dyad, no mediated pair · **Remedy locus:** institution — profession, licensing, regulation, "organisational ethics" — and explicitly not design ("Pursue ethics as a process, not technological solutionism," p. 10) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-19

**Not to be confused with `mittelstadt2016.md`** — Mittelstadt, Allo, Taddeo, Wachter & Floridi (2016), "The ethics of algorithms: Mapping the debate," *Big Data & Society*, five authors, a six-concern taxonomy. This is a different paper: single-authored, three years later, about whether a principled approach can work in AI at all. He cites the 2016 paper as his ref. 39.

## What it argues

The abstract's claim, verbatim: "According to recent meta-analyses, AI Ethics has seemingly converged on a set of principles that closely resemble the four classic principles of medical ethics … Compared to medicine, AI development lacks (1) common aims and fiduciary duties, (2) professional history and norms, (3) proven methods to translate principles into practice, and (4) robust legal and professional accountability mechanisms. These differences suggest we should not yet celebrate consensus around high-level principles that hide deep political and normative disagreement" (p. 1). The meta-analysis he leans on is Jobin, Ienca & Vayena (his ref. 3, "at least 84" initiatives, p. 1); the resemblance finding is AI4People's (ref. 8), "subsequently endorsed by the OECD and the European Commission's High Level Expert Group" (p. 2); the principlism he compares against is Beauchamp & Childress (ref. 12, the 2009 edition). Industry initiatives "have been characterised as mere virtue signalling intended to delay regulation and pre-emptively focus debate on abstract problems and technical solutions" — "This view is difficult to dismiss" (p. 1). The phrase "ethics washing" does not occur in the preprint.

§2.1, "Common aims and fiduciary duties" (pp. 2–4), is the part the draft needs. "Medicine is broadly guided by a common aim: to promote the health and well-being of the patient" — the interests of patients and practitioners "remain aligned at some fundamental level," and "By some accounts, practitioners have a moral obligation to advocate for their patient's interests against institutional interests" (p. 2). "Comparable solidarity cannot be taken for granted in AI development. AI is largely developed by the private sector … The fundamental aims of developers, users, and affected parties do not necessarily align" (p. 2). "Unlike medicine, AI development does not serve the equivalent of a 'patient' whose interests are granted initial primacy in ethical decision-making. This lack of a common goal transforms ethical decision-making from a cooperative to a competitive process" (p. 3). Formal professions "are distinguished from other vocations by fiduciary duties derived from the client-practitioner relationship"; "AI development is not a formal profession. Equivalent fiduciary relationships and complementary governance mechanisms do not exist for private sector AI developers … Companies and their employees have principal fiduciary duties towards their shareholders. Public interests are not granted primacy over commercial interests" (p. 3). "A unified regulatory framework does not yet exist for AI which establishes clear fiduciary duties towards data subjects and users" — the footnote for such a framework is Balkin's information fiduciaries (ref. 28) — and "The absence of a fiduciary relationship in AI means that users cannot trust that developers will act in their best interests when implementing ethical principles in practice" (p. 3). "It is unacceptable that users and affected parties must rely on the personal convictions of developers, fear of reputational damage, or public outcry for their vital interests in privacy, autonomy, identity, and other areas to be taken seriously" (p. 4).

§2.2 supplies a history the §1.3 decision turns on: the AMA's first code (1847) "emphasised a paternalistic duty to maximize benefits and minimize harms to patients. Over time the profession's standards have shifted away from this myopic focus on beneficence and professional conduct, and towards other duties owed to patients, most notably a growing respect for autonomy" (p. 4). §2.3 says of value-conscious design, naming Value-Sensitive Design: such methods "are also largely procedural, not functional … They do not, however, allow for particular values to be 'injected' into system design, and struggle to capture the degree to which the resulting artefact reflects particular values" (p. 7). §3 closes on five recommendations, of which the fourth — "Shift from professional ethics to organisational ethics": current initiatives "steer debate towards the transgressions of unethical individuals, and away from the collective failure of unethical organisations and business models" (p. 10) — and the fifth — "It is foolish to assume that very old and complex normative questions can be solved with technical fixes or 'good' design alone … Ethics is a process, not a destination" (p. 10) — bear on this paper.

## Relation to the argument

The one point the plan asked for holds, with a boundary the draft must respect. Mittelstadt does not write the sentence "beneficence is rarely defined because no fiduciary duty is owed"; that link is this paper's inference. What he supplies are its premises, verbatim: medicine's common aim *is* beneficence ("to promote the health and well-being of the patient"), that aim has a beneficiary built into the relation (the patient, "whose interests are granted initial primacy"), AI development "does not serve the equivalent of a 'patient'," and the only fiduciary duty a private developer owes runs to shareholders. From those the draft can say that beneficence in AI guidance names no one to whom the benefit is owed, which is why Jobin et al. find it "often mentioned" and "rarely defined" — citing Mittelstadt for the premises and owning the conclusion. `delacroix2019.md` reaches the same place from trust law: "a fiduciary obligation towards data subjects is incompatible with the data controllers' responsibility towards shareholders." Mittelstadt hopes regulation will one day impose the duty on the developer (Balkin); Delacroix & Lawrence show why it must instead be held by a separate party — which is Principle I's separate agent, and the reason the two cards belong side by side in "Protection Without Promotion."

Two further transfers. His three roles — "developers, users, and affected parties" whose aims "do not necessarily align" — are as close as the guideline literature comes to the triad, and he never models the users and affected parties as coordinating with one another; the mediated pair is absent even here. And his §2.3 verdict that VSD is "procedural, not functional" is an outside confirmation, from a critic of principlism, of what `manders2011.md` and `borning2012.md` say from inside the tradition, useful to F3.

**Cuts against, and must be answered.** His remedy locus is the institution, and he is openly hostile to design as the locus: "technical fixes or 'good' design alone" cannot solve "very old and complex normative questions." A paper that proposes three design principles has to say why they are not the solutionism he attacks: they specify the *relation* — who stands inside the coordination, what may be refused, what envelope is declared — not a technical encoding of "fairness" or "dignity," and they are the kind of "binding and highly visible accountability structures" his own first recommendation asks for (p. 9), delivered where he does not look. His "organisational ethics" recommendation is also the operator named, and the paper should take it rather than argue with it.

## Caution

An argument by analogy with no data; the comparison class is Western biomedicine and the author says reducing AI development "to a single vocation or type of expertise would be an oversimplification" (p. 5). The preprint may differ in wording from the VoR (pp. 501–507); quotations here are preprint-located and unverified against print. The paper predates the EU AI Act and any licensing regime and should not be cited for the current state of accountability mechanisms. Nothing in it concerns interface design, explanation, or adaptivity. His history of medical ethics (beneficence first, then autonomy) is a two-paragraph sketch with secondary citations, not a reading of Beauchamp & Childress.
