## What it argues

Per the abstract (paraphrased except where quotation marks appear; the verbatim fragments below were checked against the Springer record on 2026-09-18): the paper outlines an approach to CSCW systems design based on the concept of coordination mechanisms, developed as a "generalization of phenomena described in empirical investigations of the use of artifacts for the purpose of coordinating cooperative activities in different work domains"; on the basis of empirical studies it outlines a theory of artifact use for coordination in cooperative work settings, derives requirements for computational coordination mechanisms, and sketches the architecture of Ariadne, a CSCW infrastructure for constructing and running "such malleable and linkable computational coordination mechanisms." The standard gloss of the concept in the CSCW literature — a coordination mechanism is a *protocol* (a set of procedures and conventions stipulating articulation of interdependent activities) coupled with an *artifact* (a permanent symbolic construct in which the protocol is objectified) — is how the paper is usually summarized, but this pass could not verify that formulation against the text and does not quote it. The design emphasis visible in the abstract is on *malleability* and *linkability*: mechanisms that the cooperating actors themselves can modify and compose.

## Relation to the argument

For RQ9 the paper matters because it is CSCW's own vocabulary for something that coordinates *between* people through an artifact, and its answer to Suchman (1994) — Schmidt and Simone co-edited the ECSCW'93 proceedings where her paper first appeared — is flexibility: mechanisms should be "malleable" by the people they coordinate. Whether the framework has any language for a mechanism with an objective of its own cannot be settled from the abstract; the emphasis on malleability suggests it does not, since a mechanism the parties can rewrite cannot pursue an objective they did not set. That would place coordination mechanisms firmly on the *channel* side of the construct — an artifact that objectifies a protocol both parties can inspect and change — and would make the platform intermediary a coordination mechanism that has lost its malleability. If the paper is read in full, the question to answer is whether the protocol/artifact pair is always assumed to be owned by the cooperative ensemble, or whether the framework admits a mechanism owned by a third party. CUTS AGAINST the construct if the former: it would show that CSCW already had a theory of artifact-mediated coordination that simply presupposed co-ownership, and the construct's novelty would lie in dropping that presupposition rather than in noticing mediation.
