## What it argues

As quoted through Redaelli: humans have become "technological beings" (p. 4); postphenomenology is a "philosophical analysis of the structure of the relations between human beings and their lifeworld" (p. 7) without foundational claims (p. 15); Latour is credited with "nonhuman forms of agency" (p. 17); "things can be seen as part of the moral community in the sense that they help to shape morality" (p. 42); intentionality as "directedness of human beings toward reality" grounds the capacity to form intentions (p. 55); technologies have "a directing role" (p. 57) and "moral decision making is a joint effort of human beings and technological artifacts" (p. 58); "technologies also contribute to the moral responsibility of human beings ... But [...] this does not imply that technologies should be held morally accountable for their mediating roles" (p. 108); there are "emergent forms of mediation" (p. 127); and the definition, "when this 'directedness' of technological devices is added to human intentionality, a composite intentionality comes about" (p. 145), with technology developed "to reveal a reality that can be experienced only by technologies" (p. 146) (redaelli2022, pp. 161–169). The preface, again through Redaelli, records the ultrasound case that opens the book: "the mere availability of testing possibilities had made us feel responsible for not testing" (p. VII). Verbeek's 2014 reply confirms that the book's paradigm example is obstetric ultrasound and that its design proposal is to "anticipate the mediating roles of technologies" in design and to equip users to "read" mediations (verbeek2014, p. 78). On the plan's note: Verbeek does use "script," but as Akrich and Latour's term for the pragmatic side of mediation; his own term is mediation, and the Achterhuis material (the "moralization of technology" proposal and the technocracy and moral-laziness objections to it) is documented in verbeek2008b, pp. 97 and 101, not verified here.

## Relation to the argument

RQ2: this is the book behind "designers materialize morality" (verbeek2015, p. 31), and its chapter "Morality in Design" is, by title and by the 2008 chapter of the same name, where anticipating and designing mediations is argued. What the secondary quotations establish for the paper is the shape of the licence: mediations are unavoidable, emergent (p. 127) and composite (p. 145), so the designer's choice is between reflective and unreflective mediation, and a seam is a reflective one. The passage at p. 108 is useful against a misreading the paper must avoid: Verbeek does not hold the artifact accountable, so an algorithmacy argument that treats the platform as the accountable party is not Verbeek's position and needs its own ground (the objective axis, which he lacks).
