# Green, B., & Chen, Y. (2019). The principles and limits of algorithm-in-the-loop decision making. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, 3(CSCW), Article 50, 24 pages.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3359152 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-hosted camera-ready PDF from benzevgreen.com, carrying the ACM header and "50:n" pagination) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top CSCW venue; large pre-registered-style MTurk experiment with IRB and NACJD approval · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology–world — one crowdworker, a risk assessment, and a decision subject (defendant or loan applicant) who is the object of the prediction but not a participant · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

People given an algorithm get more accurate and no more reliable or fair. The authors state three desiderata for "algorithm-in-the-loop" decisions — accuracy (do better with the model than without), reliability (evaluate one's own and the model's performance and calibrate reliance to it), and fairness (interact with the model without bias by race or gender) — and test them on 1,156 MTurk workers predicting pretrial violation and 732 predicting loan default, each seeing 40 profiles under one of six conditions: baseline, a numeric risk score, the score pre-filled as a default, "update" (predict first, then see the score and revise), an explanation of which features drove the score, and outcome feedback. The models were gradient-boosted trees with AUC 0.66 (pretrial, comparable to COMPAS and the PSA) and 0.71 (loans). Every treatment except feedback beat baseline; update was best (gain 0.603 vs 0.464 for the plain score in pretrial, 0.821 vs 0.682 in loans), and feedback was worst because binary outcomes pushed people to binary predictions. Presentation alone moved accuracy as much as a 3.4–4.3-point change in model accuracy would have. On reliability, no treatment let people judge their own or the model's accuracy, and calibration was significant in the wrong direction in three pretrial conditions. On fairness, every treatment showed disparate interactions: when the score exceeded the baseline guess, its influence on predictions about Black defendants was 0.50 against 0.12 for white defendants under the plain score; update cut that disparity by 81.5% and the deviation disparity by 73.9%, without removing it. The discussion turns the finding on *State v. Loomis*: courts assume the human will "exercise discretion," and "such behavior requires people to evaluate the quality of predictions and to calibrate their decisions based on these evaluations — abilities that our findings indicate people do not reliably possess" (50:19).

## Relation to the argument

RQ4, both axes, and the closest this cluster comes to the essay's triad. The person on the far side of the model is real here — a felony defendant, a loan applicant — and the paper's fairness desideratum measures how the model's mediation shifts the decision-maker's treatment of that person by race. So the model does interpret both sides in the essay's sense (it scores the subject and shapes the decider), and its determination binds the subject. What the paper does not do is give the subject any channel: the defendant cannot see, contest, or adapt to the score, and the study measures only the decider's behavior. The limitations section names this: due process means "defendants must have the right to hear and challenge claims against them," and "these considerations were not captured by our experimental task" (50:21). That sentence is the essay's opening: the literature knows the second party has a claim and has no instrument for it. On the objective axis the paper is also useful: risk assessments are adopted "with the explicit goal of reducing detention rates" and fail to, because judges override release recommendations (50:4) — a case where the mediator's built-in objective, the institution's objective, and the decider's objective diverge, though the paper treats this as background rather than as a variable.

## Relation to Principle I (added 2026-09-18)

This card is the **negative anchor** for Principle I, and its function changes with the thesis pivot. The 50:21 sentence — due process means "defendants must have the right to hear and challenge claims against them," and "these considerations were not captured by our experimental task" — is no longer only evidence that the triad is on the page and unmeasured; it is the literature's own statement that the second party *has a claim* and *has no instrument*. Green and Chen give the defendant a right and give the study no channel through which she could exercise it; every remedy the paper's six accounts propose (a literate judge, an explained score, an adopted or refused tool) is a remedy on the decider's side of the model. Principle I's counter-delegation asks what would stand on the defendant's side: an agent that reads the score's interaction with her case as the study read it for the crowdworkers, and contests it before the decision binds. The paper should quote 50:21 as the gap and cite the fairness result (influence 0.50 vs 0.12 by race) as the reason the gap has a cost the subject cannot see. CUTS AGAINST a purely client-side Principle I in one way: the disparity was reduced most by changing the *decider's* interface (update condition), not by anything the subject could do — an argument that parity may need the intermediary's cooperation, which Keller (keller2021.md, p. 171) shows platforms have litigated and threatened against (Power Ventures sued "out of existence," Facebook's threat to NYU's ad-observer extension).

## Caution

MTurk workers, not judges or loan officers; the authors say so and offer field evidence that judges behave similarly. Two settings, forty predictions each, a Brier-score bonus, models built by the authors. The reliability tests rest on exit-survey self-reports regressed against performance. The subject is present only as a profile; the paper does not model the subject's knowledge, options, or perception of the score. Use it to show that the triad is on the page and unmeasured, not as evidence about what a subject facing a risk score can do.
