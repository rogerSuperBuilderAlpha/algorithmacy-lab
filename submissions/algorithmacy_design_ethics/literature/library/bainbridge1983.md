# Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of automation. *Automatica*, 19(6), 775–779.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8 (DOI not printed on the 1983 PDF; verified against the Crossref record for *Automatica* 19(6), 1983) · **Read depth:** full_text (Pergamon PDF, 5 pages, pagination 775–779 confirmed) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed brief paper in the IFAC control-engineering journal; a review-and-argument piece, not a study · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology–world (operator–automatic controller–process plant) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Bainbridge's thesis is that "the more advanced a control system is, so the more crucial may be the contribution of the human operator" (p. 775), because the designer who treats the operator as unreliable "still leaves the operator to do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate" (p. 775). Two deskilling mechanisms follow. Manual skills decay when unused, "particularly the refinements of gain and timing," so "a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one" (p. 775). Cognitive skills decay too, since process knowledge "develops only through use and feedback about its effectiveness" (p. 775), and the operator's working picture of plant state takes "quarter to half an hour" to build, which a take-over in an emergency does not allow (p. 776). Monitoring is the worst of it: vigilance cannot be sustained "for more than about half an hour" (p. 776); the automatic system was installed because it outperforms the operator "but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working effectively" (p. 776); and when a computer applies more dimensions and finer criteria than a human can, "there is therefore no way in which the human operator can check in real-time that the computer is following its rules correctly. One can therefore only expect the operator to monitor the computer's decisions at some meta-level ... The human monitor has been given an impossible task" (p. 776). The deskilled job is "very boring but very responsible, yet there is no opportunity to acquire or maintain the qualities required to handle the responsibility" (p. 776).

Her remedies are deliberate inefficiencies. If the operator must follow the computer's decisions, "it is necessary for the computer to make these decisions using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which the operator can follow, even when this may not be the most efficient method technically" (p. 777). Operators should get "hands-on control for a short period in each shift," or failing that simulator practice (p. 777). Automatic systems "should fail obviously" (p. 777). Advice-giving carries its own cost: "when following advice the operator's reactions will be slower ... and he is getting no practice in being 'intelligent'" (p. 777). Ephrath (1980) found "system performance was worse with computer aiding, because the operator made the decisions anyway, and checking the computer added to his workload" (p. 777). Two closing ironies: "it is the most successful automated systems, with rare need for manual intervention, which may need the greatest investment in human operator training" (p. 777), and a display so compatible that the operator "does not have to think about it to take it in" may teach him nothing about the process (p. 778).

## Relation to the argument

Bainbridge supplies, forty years before Vallor, the human-factors version of both halves of RQ3. Her p. 776 argument is a structural claim about the definition's "infer what the intermediary is doing": once the intermediary decides faster and on more criteria than the human can follow, real-time inference is impossible and only meta-level judgement remains — which is what algorithmacy asks for. Her p. 777 remedy is the friction criterion the RQ wants: pace and criteria the human can follow are worth their efficiency cost because without them contestation is empty. That is a justification by the structural axis (the human cannot check what she cannot follow), and it needs no third objective; the controller in a process plant pursues the operator's own goal. So Bainbridge, like Vallor, shows a friction that earns its cost without any cooptation in play. She also anticipates Buçinca: "checking the computer added to his workload" (p. 777) is the cost users resent. On the objective axis she is silent; the plant is dyadic and the automation has no goal of its own. Her observation that "the human being must know which tasks the computer is dealing with and how. Otherwise the same problems arise as in human teams in which there is no clear allocation of responsibility" (p. 778) is the earliest statement I have of Seamful Boundary Disclosure as a human-factors requirement.

## Caution

A five-page argument drawing on 1970s process-control and flight-deck studies, several of them conference reports and one a "verbal presentation" (Ephrath 1980); no data of her own. The setting is a single skilled operator supervising a physical process, not a lay user coordinating with another person through a system, and the operator's competence is technical, not moral. Her deskilling is skill decay from disuse; Vallor's is failure to acquire character in the first place. Do not cite as evidence about consumer interfaces or generative AI.

## Relation to Principle III — bounded outputs, not mechanistic interpretability (cluster F pass, 2026-09-18)

**Remedy locus:** artifact (the computer's methods, criteria and rate are to be designed so the operator can follow them).

Bainbridge's p. 777 remedy is Principle III's earliest form, and her p. 776 argument is its justification. The remedy: if the operator must follow the computer's decisions, "it is necessary for the computer to make these decisions using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which the operator can follow, even when this may not be the most efficient method technically." Read with the surrounding pages, that is a constraint on *outputs and their pacing*, not on what the operator is shown of the computer's internals: she asks that the decisions themselves be of a kind, and arrive at a speed, the human can track. The justification: once a computer applies more dimensions and finer criteria than a human can, "there is therefore no way in which the human operator can check in real-time that the computer is following its rules correctly," and "the human monitor has been given an impossible task" (p. 776). That sentence is the human-factors refutation of mechanistic interpretability as a remedy for the coordinated party — the interpretability would be delivered to someone who, by her argument, cannot use it in real time — and it is why Principle III bounds what the system does rather than exposing how. Two of her other remedies belong to the same family. Automatic systems "should fail obviously" (p. 777) is a regime-disclosure requirement, the ancestor of Sarter and Woods's mode awareness and of the ODD's "fallback." And "the human being must know which tasks the computer is dealing with and how. Otherwise the same problems arise as in human teams in which there is no clear allocation of responsibility" (p. 778) is the operational, not mechanistic, sense of "how": which tasks, under what allocation. What she adds to Muir (1994) is the time dimension: predictability is not only consistency of behaviour but a *rate* the human can follow, which Chen, Zaharia and Zou's (2024) version-to-version drift violates in a way no single snapshot shows. The caution from the original relation section stands: her controller pursues the operator's own goal, so she supplies the structural half of Principle III and nothing on the objective axis; and her operator is a trained professional, so the "rate which the operator can follow" is a higher rate than a lay party's.
