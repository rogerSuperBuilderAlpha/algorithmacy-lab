# Livingstone, S. (2004). Media literacy and the challenge of new information and communication technologies. *The Communication Review*, 7(1), 3–14.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/10714420490280152 (Crossref: vol. 7, issue 1, pp. 3–14, January 2004) · **Read depth:** full_text of the author's final manuscript in LSE Research Online (eprints.lse.ac.uk/1017, deposited May 2007), which states "Some differences between this version and the publisher's version remain"; the Taylor & Francis version was not opened, so quotations are located by the manuscript's section headings · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed journal (an invited essay); the standard citation for the four-component definition of media literacy, and the one Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) take as the root of the algorithmic-literacy line · **Evidence basis:** direct_read (author manuscript) · **Parties modeled:** human–technology — a reader/viewer/user and a mediated text; Livingstone's own move is to insist the medium is a party to the relation, but no second human appears · **Remedy locus:** subject (skills), with the essay's own critique that skills alone are not enough · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The canonical definition is quoted and adopted: "in a key conference a decade ago, a clear, concise and widely adopted definition emerged: media literacy — indeed literacy more generally — is the ability to access, analyze, evaluate and create messages in a variety of forms (Aufderheide, 1993; Christ and Potter, 1998). These four components — access, analysis, evaluation and content creation — together constitute a skills-based approach to media literacy" ("What is media literacy?"). Livingstone accepts it as "necessary but not sufficient." Each component is then read against new media. Evaluation is where the essay bites: "critical evaluation rests on a substantial body of knowledge regarding the broader social, cultural, economic, political and historical contexts in which media content is produced"; where print and broadcasting had "key filters operating to select material," "Now that almost anyone can produce and disseminate internet contents, with fewer — and different kinds of — filters, the basis of critical literacy must alter" ("Evaluation"). The essay's argument proper is "Beyond a skills-based approach": "to focus solely on questions of skill or ability neglects the textuality and technology that mediates communication. In consequence, it unwittingly supports a universalist, cognitive framework"; instead "we must develop an account of literacies in the plural, defined through their relations with different media rather than defined independently of them," a shift "from an exclusive focus on the viewer to a focus on the interaction between text and reader." Hence "Literacy, by extension, cannot be conceived solely as a feature of the user but must also be seen as medium-dependent, a co-production of the interactive engagement between technology and user" ("Conclusion"). Media education has always taught that technology is not neutral — "the favorite exam question being, 'television is a window on the world: discuss'" — and the same critical work on the web "[is] just beginning" ("Changing literacies"). The institutional point, via Hartley: literacy "is ideologically and politically charged — it can be used as a means of social control or regulation, but also as a progressive weapon"; UK policy that devolves "responsibility for media use from the state to individuals" can be read "either as 'empowering' or, more critically, as part of a Foucauldian shift from centralized government to individual governance" ("Individual and institutional uses of literacy"). The conclusion names three processes "no one of which is sufficient alone": representation, "the diffusion of interpretative skills and abilities across a (stratified) population," and "the institutional, especially, the state management of the power that access to and skilled use of knowledge brings."

## Relation to the argument

RQ11, the canonical statement of the media-literacy paradigm the paper names as misapplied — chosen over Buckingham (2003) because the field's own reviews (oeldorfhirsch2025.md) route the lineage through this definition, and because Livingstone states the paradigm and its limit in one essay. Three things follow. First, the four verbs — access, analyze, evaluate, create — all take a *message* as their object; there is no verb for a determination made about two people, which is the paper's core distinction and can be drawn straight from the definition. Second, Livingstone already argues that a skills-in-the-subject model is insufficient and that literacy must be "medium-dependent, a co-production" — so the paper cannot say the literacy tradition never looked past the subject; it must say that even her relational turn stops at *text and reader*, with no third party and no co-determined counterpart. Third, her Foucauldian warning — that promoting literacy can be a way of shifting responsibility from institutions to individuals — is the strongest version of the objection the paper must survive: is "algorithmacy" one more devolution of the institution's burden onto the user? The paper's answer (Principle I places an agent in the relation, not a skill in the subject) should be stated against this passage. CUTS AGAINST the paper's supersession claim to the extent that "critical evaluation" of production contexts already covers knowing whose interests a filter serves; the paper's distinct content is the binding of the second party, which no component of the definition touches.

## Caution

An invited, argumentative essay with no data; the author says research at the time was "mainly analytic." The author manuscript, not the published version, was read; page numbers for the 3–14 pagination are not available from it. The definition Livingstone adopts is Aufderheide's 1993 conference wording, so a citation to Livingstone is a citation to her endorsement of it, not to its origin. The essay is about media and ICT in 2003 — web pages, email, chat — and says nothing about algorithms, platforms or automated intermediaries.
