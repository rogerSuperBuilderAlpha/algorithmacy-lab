# Gray, C. M., Kou, Y., Battles, B., Hoggatt, J., & Toombs, A. L. (2018). The dark (patterns) side of UX design. *Proceedings of the 2018 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, Paper 534, 1–14.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3173574.3174108 · **Read depth:** full_text (NSF Public Access Repository accepted manuscript, PAR 10057203, paginated 1–14 as "Paper 534") · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top HCI venue (CHI full paper); qualitative content analysis · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** designer–stakeholders (designer, shareholder, end user) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The authors take the practitioner term coined by Harry Brignull in 2010 and give it an academic definition: dark patterns are "instances where designers use their knowledge of human behavior (e.g., psychology) and the desires of end users to implement deceptive functionality that is not in the user's best interest" (p. 1), a "co-opting of human-centered values in the service of deceptive or malicious aims" (p. 1), where "user value is supplanted in favor of shareholder value" (abstract). They collected 118 artifacts — 45 from social media, 40 from practitioner blogs, 19 from news outlets, 10 from their own product use, 4 from darkpatterns.org (p. 4) — and, after finding Brignull's eleven-type list mixed "context, strategy, and outcome" (p. 3), re-coded the corpus into five designer strategies: nagging, obstruction, sneaking, interface interference, and forced action (p. 5). Obstruction is defined as "impeding a task flow, making an interaction more difficult than it inherently needs to be with the intent to dissuade an action" (p. 5), with Brignull's "Roach Motel" (Stamps.com requiring a phone call in business hours to cancel) and "Price Comparison Prevention" nested under it. Nagging examples include Uber prompting drivers who try to stop for the day with an arbitrary income target (p. 5). Two discussion points carry the weight. First: "Paradoxically, some instances of dark patterns test well from a usability perspective (e.g., forced action, nagging), but do so at the expense of user choice" (p. 8), and shareholder-focused evaluation criteria will not detect the harm. Second, the remedy they propose is not a code but the designer's character, via Nelson and Stolterman's "guarantor of design": "we propose a redirection back to the designer's character" (p. 9), with Hanlon's razor marking the practitioner line between dark patterns (malice assumed) and anti-patterns (lack of skill) (p. 9).

## Relation to the argument

Gray et al. are the design-side statement of the cooptation reading: the criterion that sorts a dark pattern from a design choice is whose value the interface serves — user or shareholder — and obstruction is friction that fails that test while frictionlessness (preselection, forced continuity) fails it just as often. That gives RQ3 its cleanest evidence that friction has no valence of its own: the same obstruction that is a Roach Motel when it blocks cancellation would be a microboundary (Cox et al. 2016) if the user had installed it. The p. 8 paradox is the mirror image of Buçinca et al. (2021): there, users dislike friction that helps them; here, users pass friction-free designs that harm them. Felt preference fails in both directions, which is the RQ's premise. The paper also cuts against a purely objective-axis account in one respect: its remedy is virtue, but virtue in the designer, not competence in the user. Nobody in this paper expects the user to infer or contest the pattern; the authors' own question "Do they blame themself for not understanding the system, or do they understand that they are being explicitly deceived or manipulated?" (p. 9) is left open. Algorithmacy proposes the user-side competence this literature does not.

## Caution

A corpus of practitioner-shared screenshots is a sample of what practitioners found egregious, not of interfaces in general; the authors say so (p. 10). No users were studied, so nothing here measures harm, preference, or detection; the "test well from a usability perspective" claim is the authors' inference, not a result. The five strategies are inductive labels from grounded coding, since refined in Gray's later work and in regulatory taxonomies (FTC 2022, EU DSA), so cite the 2018 categories as origin rather than current standard. E-commerce and consumer apps only; no AI intermediaries, no coordination between two users.
