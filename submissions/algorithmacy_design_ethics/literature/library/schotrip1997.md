## What it argues

CTA "originated in the mid-1980s in the Netherlands," in the 1984 Policy Memorandum on TA and the 1987 NOTA background study (p. 251); the paper is a retrospective and a programme. The family of TA approaches shares a philosophy, "to reduce the human costs of trial and error learning in society's handling of new technologies, and to do so by anticipating potential impacts and feeding these insights back into decision making, and into actors' strategies" (p. 251). CTA's own thrust is "to broaden the design of new technologies (and the redesign of old technologies). Feedback of TA activities into the actual construction of technology is crucial, and strategies and tools contributing to such feedback make up CTA. Such strategies and tools can range from dialogue workshops and social experiments, to technology forcing programs and platforms" (p. 252). The abstract's compression: "CTA shifts the focus away from assessing impacts of new technologies to broadening design, development, and implementation processes. Explicit CTA has concentrated on dialogue among and early interaction with new actors" (p. 251).

The definition the draft needs is on p. 255: "CTA is not simply a management tool. CTA can be seen as a new design practice (which includes tools) in which impacts are anticipated, users and other impacted communities are involved from the start and in an interactive way, and which contains an element of societal learning." The worked examples are Dutch and Danish: NOTA's telecommunications programme, which showed "that developers of technology enter discussions with other concerned parties in society during the actual designing process" (p. 253); SWOKA's "Future Images for Consumers," "a process of iterative meetings in which room is created for discussions and negotiations on design criteria and visions of both producers and consumers" (pp. 253–254); Digital City Amsterdam, where "citizens experiment with e-mail and Internet, build user competence, and provide input for further technology development" (p. 254); and Danish consensus conferences and dialogue workshops (p. 254).

The authors are candid about the limit. Dialogue and agenda-building have been achieved, "However, it turns out to be difficult to organize feedback of such societal agendas into actual technological development. There often is no special effort at feedback other than publicizing results and hoping that technology actors will respond" (p. 255). They also decline to assume symmetry: actors "are co-producers of impacts. (This should not be read to imply that we believe all actors have equal access and power in various stages of technology development.)" (p. 257). The theoretical core is that "Assessment occurs all the time, and it is a modulation of ongoing processes of assessment (and feedback) which is in order. This, we claim, is the thrust of CTA" (p. 257); "Command and control approaches do not work, and modulation is the best one can do" (p. 258). Three meta-level criteria guide modulation: anticipation, learning that is "broad and deep" (first- and second-order), and reflexivity (p. 257). Three "generic strategies" follow: technology forcing (pp. 258–261), strategic niche management (pp. 261–262), and "loci for alignment," which to work "should function as negotiation spaces" (p. 262).

The closing sections put CTA in politics. The Luddites "judged machines on their contribution toward the quality of labor and society as a whole, as if they were constructive technology assessors avant la lettre"; "Their interest, we could say, was not in the machines, but in the loss of access to and negotiation power about new technologies" (p. 263). Modern societies run a "two-track regime" that separates promotion from control, so that "promotion actors (engineers and others) need not realize that when they are engineering technology they are also engineering society" (p. 264). Government's residual role is "to ensure a balance in the access and voice of various constituences involved in technical change" (p. 264). The conclusion leaves representation open: "Who represents actual or potential users, victims or impactees in CTA activities, and how?" (p. 265), and allows that "opposition to technology, including contestation and harassment, is useful if it contributes, directly or indirectly, to the improvement of technology in society" (p. 266).

## Relation to the argument

The draft sentence this card bears on (DRAFT.md, Verbeek section): "Verbeek's constructive remedies—anticipatory design and Constructive Technology Assessment (CTA)—presume a participatory, democratic design context where designers and users collaboratively negotiate the moral mediation of an artifact. This model collapses under the conditions of proprietary artificial intelligence."

The card was made to check the draft's gloss against the term's origin. Three findings. First, "participatory" and "negotiate" are Schot and Rip's own words for CTA: users "involved from the start and in an interactive way" (p. 255), "negotiations on design criteria" (p. 253), loci that "function as negotiation spaces" (p. 262). "Democratic" is not their word; it is Verbeek's addition (see verbeek2006.md, full-text addition), and the draft should attribute it to him. Second, the draft's "collapse" is half-anticipated by the founders: they report that feedback into design is the step CTA had not secured ("hoping that technology actors will respond," p. 255) and that technology actors are "always the target" (p. 256) with unequal power (p. 257). So the draft can say, accurately, that CTA's own authors located its weak point exactly where proprietary AI breaks it, at the feedback from stakeholders into a design the technology actor controls. Third, Schot and Rip's remedy for that weakness is not more dialogue but leverage — technology forcing by regulators, insurers and standard-setters (pp. 258–261) and "sociotechnical criticism" of the Luddite kind (pp. 263–266) — which is closer to this paper's collectively won standing than to the designer-side anticipation the draft attributes to the CTA tradition. Proposed replacement wording:

> Verbeek's second remedy borrows constructive technology assessment from Dutch STS, where it names "a new design practice ... in which impacts are anticipated, users and other impacted communities are involved from the start and in an interactive way" (Schot & Rip, 1997, p. 255), and augments it so that stakeholders anticipate mediations as well as impacts, in what he calls "a democratization of the designing process" (Verbeek, 2006). Both presume that the stakeholders' assessment can be fed back into the design. Schot and Rip already reported that step as the one CTA had failed to secure — "hoping that technology actors will respond" (p. 255) — and proprietary, continuously optimised AI removes it altogether: the user is neither in the room nor able to read the artifact that is assessed.

The draft's next sentence, "The user does not 'co-constitute' reality with the model," should not be attached to this card; Schot and Rip say users co-produce impacts (p. 257), which is the opposite claim, and the draft's point is that platform AI removes that co-production.

## Caution

A programme statement by two of CTA's founders, written from inside Dutch policy institutions (NOTA/Rathenau, SWOKA, the Ministry of Economic Affairs) whose projects it evaluates; the retrospective is not independent. The examples predate the web platform (Digital City Amsterdam is the one internet case, p. 254), and nothing in the paper anticipates a technology actor whose artifact is opaque to the participating users or changes after assessment. "Managing technology in society" refers to Rip, Misa and Schot's 1995 Pinter volume, which this card has not read; Verbeek's 2006 paper cites that volume and Schot's 1992 STHV article as its CTA sources, not this one, so the draft should cite this paper for the term's definition and Verbeek for his augmentation.
