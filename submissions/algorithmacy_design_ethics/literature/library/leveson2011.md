## What it argues

The book's target is the assumption that safety is reliability. "Reliability in engineering is defined as the probability that something satisfies its specified behavioral requirements over time and under given conditions — that is, it does not fail" (p. 10); "Safety, in contrast, is defined as the absence of accidents, where an accident is an event involving an unplanned and unacceptable loss. To increase safety, the focus should be on eliminating or preventing hazards, not eliminating failures. Making all the components highly reliable will not necessarily make the system safe" (p. 11). Hence "Safety is a system property, not a component property, and must be controlled at the system level, not the component level" (p. 14), and "New Assumption 1: High reliability is neither necessary nor sufficient for safety" (p. 14). The conceptual spine is the distinction between requirements and constraints: "Requirements are derived from the mission or reason for the existence of the organization. The mission of the chemical plant is to produce chemicals. Constraints represent acceptable ways the system or organization can achieve the mission goals. Not exposing bystanders to toxins and not polluting the environment are constraints on the way the mission (producing chemicals) can be achieved" (p. 11). Where safety is a constraint rather than the mission, "The best way to ensure the constraints are enforced in such a system may be not to build or operate the system at all. Not building a nuclear bomb is the surest protection against accidental detonation" (p. 12; the sentence introducing it, "in others safety is not the mission but instead is a constraint on how the mission can be achieved," runs across the p. 11–12 break). Chapter 3 grounds this in systems theory: emergent properties "are related to constraints upon the degree of freedom of those components" (p. 63); "Safety, on the other hand, is clearly an emergent property of systems: Safety can be determined only in the context of the whole" (p. 64); "Safety depends on the enforcement of constraints on the behavior of the components in the system, including constraints on their potential interactions" (p. 64). And so "Safety then can be viewed as a control problem. Accidents occur when component failures, external disturbances, and/or dysfunctional interactions among system components are not adequately controlled" (p. 67). STAMP follows: "The most basic concept in STAMP is not an event, but a constraint. Events leading to losses occur only because safety constraints were not successfully enforced" (p. 76). Chapter 4 then separates two kinds of control. "Passive controls are those that maintain safety by their presence — basically, the system fails into a safe state or simple interlocks are used to limit the interactions among system components to safe ones," like a railway semaphore weighted to drop to *stop* if its cable breaks; "active controls require some action(s) to provide protection: (1) detection of a hazardous event or condition (monitoring), (2) measurement of some variable(s), (3) interpretation of the measurement (diagnosis), and (4) response," and "The failure modes for the active control system are greatly increased over the passive design" (pp. 76–77).

## Relation to the argument

RQ10, and this is the engineering-discipline statement of what Principle III's "hard guarantees" are. Leveson's distinction between a requirement (the mission) and a constraint (an acceptable way of pursuing it) is the distinction the paper needs between the mediator's objective and the bounds on its behaviour: Principle III does not ask the mediator to pursue the parties' goals — that is Principle II's business — it asks that whatever objective it pursues, its outputs stay inside an enforced envelope. Her insistence that safety is "an emergent property" enforced "at the system level" is Floridi and Sanders's level-of-abstraction point restated by an engineer: predictability of a component (a model's weights) is not predictability of the system (what the coordinated party experiences), and the guarantee has to be stated at the level where the loss occurs. The passive/active distinction is the sharpest tool here. Bounded outputs are a passive control — an interlock that "maintains safety by its presence" and does not depend on anyone diagnosing anything — whereas mechanistic interpretability is an active control in her exact sense: detect, measure, *interpret*, respond, with "greatly increased" failure modes and a human in the loop who, as Bainbridge showed, cannot keep up. That gives Principle III's preference for bounded outputs over interpretability an engineering rationale rather than a merely epistemic one, and it links to Sarter and Woods: what the operator needs is the regime (which interlocks are engaged), not the diagnosis. Two further points. "High reliability is neither necessary nor sufficient for safety" is the answer to the objection that a well-benchmarked model is a predictable one; Chen, Zaharia and Zou's (2024) drift is a reliability story, and Leveson says reliability is the wrong axis. And her remark that the surest way to enforce a constraint "may be not to build or operate the system at all" is the bridge from Principle III to Principle II — refusal is the limiting case of constraint enforcement. CUTS AGAINST: her controllers pursue the mission the organisation set, and her hazards are physical losses. A mediator's "unpredictable" determination is not an accident in her sense unless the paper defines the hazard — what loss to the coordinated party counts — and it must do so before it can borrow "safety constraint." Her hierarchy also assumes that the top level (regulator, management) wants the constraint enforced; under cooptation the operator is the one choosing the constraints, which is a control structure her model can describe but not remedy.

## Caution

Roughly 70 pages of a 560-page book were read closely, and the STPA/CAST methods that are the book's practical contribution were not. Page numbers are inferred from the running headers of the OAPEN text and should be checked against a print copy. Leveson's examples are chemical plants, spacecraft, aircraft and a ferry; there is no software-as-mediator case, no platform, and no notion of an objective that diverges from the operators'. The 2011/2012 date discrepancy is copyright page versus print date; cite 2011 with the Crossref DOI.
