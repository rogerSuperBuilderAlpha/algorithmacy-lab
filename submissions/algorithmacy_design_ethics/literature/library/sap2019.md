# Sap, M., Card, D., Gabriel, S., Choi, Y., & Smith, N. A. (2019). The risk of racial bias in hate speech detection. *Proceedings of the 57th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics*, 1668–1678.

**Identifier:** doi:10.18653/v1/P19-1163 · **Read depth:** full_text of the ACL Anthology PDF (aclanthology.org/P19-1163.pdf, paginated 1668–1678; Florence, July–August 2019) · **Affiliations (p. 1668):** Sap, Gabriel, Choi and Smith, Paul G. Allen School, University of Washington; Card, Machine Learning Department, Carnegie Mellon; Choi and Smith also Allen Institute for AI · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, ACL main conference · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** none — a corpus and classifier audit plus an annotation experiment; no coordination modeled · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The abstract (p. 1668): "We investigate how annotators' insensitivity to differences in dialect can lead to racial bias in automatic hate speech detection models, potentially amplifying harm against minority populations. We first uncover unexpected correlations between surface markers of African American English (AAE) and ratings of toxicity in several widely-used hate speech datasets. Then, we show that models trained on these corpora acquire and propagate these biases, such that AAE tweets and tweets by self-identified African Americans are up to two times more likely to be labelled as offensive compared to others. Finally, we propose dialect and race priming as ways to reduce the racial bias in annotation, showing that when annotators are made explicitly aware of an AAE tweet's dialect they are significantly less likely to label the tweet as offensive." Figure 1 (p. 1668) shows Jigsaw's PerspectiveAPI scoring "Wussup, n*gga!" at 90% toxicity against "What's up, bro!" at 7%, and "I saw his ass yesterday" at 95% against "I saw him yesterday" at 6%. The motivation: "automated removal of such content risks further suppressing already-marginalized voices" (p. 1668). The finding (§3, p. 1671): in the Demographic16 corpus, "AAE tweets are more than twice as likely to be labelled as 'offensive' or 'abusive' (by classifiers trained on DWMW17 and FDCL18, respectively)," and in UserLevelRace18 "tweets by African American authors are 1.5 times more likely to be labelled 'offensive'." The verdict: "existing approaches to toxic language detection have racial biases, and... text alone does not determine offensiveness" (p. 1669); "our work shows how insensitivity to dialect can lead to discrimination against minorities, even without explicit identity mentions" (p. 1672).

## Relation to the argument

The earlier, pre-LLM anchor for the draft sentence in "Moral Hazards," bullet 3, on AAVE. It supports "penalize" in one specific domain: toxicity classifiers, trained on crowd-annotated corpora, mark AAE as offensive at up to twice the rate — and the mechanism is annotator bias that the model acquires and propagates. That is an "evaluative" system in the content-moderation sense, not an educational one, and the draft should say which. Proposed wording: "toxicity classifiers trained on crowd-labelled corpora flag tweets in African American English as offensive up to twice as often as other tweets, 'suppressing already-marginalized voices' (Sap et al. 2019, p. 1668)." The phrase in quotation marks is the source's own and is the exact harm the bullet's heading, "erasure," names.

The remedy the authors test is a change at the labelling stage — priming annotators with the tweet's dialect — not a literacy for the speaker whose tweet is removed. That places the fix upstream, in the intermediary's pipeline, and the paper can cite it as a case where the field itself locates the repair away from the affected party's reading skill. Hofmann et al. (hofmann2024.md) cite this paper as a "known case of language technology putting speakers of AAE at a disadvantage," which is the lineage the draft should follow: Sap 2019 for classifiers, Hofmann 2024 for language models.

## Caution

Twitter corpora only; AAE is inferred by the Blodgett et al. (2016) dialect model rather than self-reported, except in the UserLevelRace18 corpus; the illustrative examples turn on reclaimed in-group terms; the classifiers are the authors' own, trained on two public datasets, plus PerspectiveAPI; "up to two times" is the top of a range. A 2019 result about classifiers, not about generative models, and nothing about grading or education.
