# Sigg, D., Hardt, M., & Mendler-Dünner, C. (2025). Decline Now: A combinatorial model for algorithmic collective action. *Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '25)*, 1–17. ACM.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3706598.3713966 (Crossref: CHI '25, pages 1–17, issued/published online 25 April 2025, print date 26 April 2025, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 open access) · **Read depth:** full_text of the arXiv preprint (arXiv:2410.12633v1, 16 October 2024, 17 pages plus appendices); the ACM version of record was not opened (dl.acm.org refused the download), so section numbers below are the preprint's and the CHI text may differ · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top HCI venue; a formal model with simulations, instantiated on a Working Washington worker-pay report, plus a documented-case table · **Evidence basis:** direct_read (preprint) · **Parties modeled:** two humans + mediator — drivers, customers who place orders, and a dispatch algorithm that assigns and re-prices; the customer is present only as the source of orders and has no strategy · **Remedy locus:** relation (a coordinated strategy that changes what the intermediary's rule yields, without changing the rule) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

DoorDash drivers "started a campaign, #DeclineNow, to purposefully decline orders below a certain pay threshold. For each declined order, the platform returns the request to other available drivers with slightly increased pay" (abstract). The authors build "a first combinatorial model to study the strategic interaction between workers and the platform": N workers, one order per time step assigned at random to an idle worker at base pay r; a declined order is re-offered with pay raised "by an extra δ" and "randomly among the idle workers – including the worker who just declined the order," and "This random reassignment of the order happens until the order is accepted" (§3; the intro's phrasing is "until a worker accepts the order," §1); participants (fraction α) follow h_τ, "decline, if pay < τ; accept otherwise," non-participants "accept an order at any pay" (§3). Utility is expected hourly income net of a per-order cost cm. The key variable is the "degree of oversupply," deg := N/b, workers relative to the busy time needed to serve the stream (Definition 2). Results: "the gain of collective action is positive" for any α > 0 and any supply level (Theorem 1); under undersupply "the benefit of freeriding is 0" and "it is individually rational to participate" (Theorems 2–3); under oversupply participation pays only when deg is small and α large (Theorem 4). The instantiation uses the movement's actual threshold (τ = $7), a $0.25 increment "as it is often reported in forums such as Reddit," and "a report from Working Washington [2020]" that "collected over 200 data points from DoorDash drivers" (§4; the paper calls it a report, not a survey, and counts data points, not drivers). The recommendation is organisational: "organizing in shifts as a means to reduce oversupply and empower collectives" (abstract; §6). The paper also assembles "30 cases of algorithmic collective action" (Table 1), sorted into "collective information sharing" (Turkopticon, WeChat driver groups, Worker Info Exchange, Driver's Seat Cooperative) and "collective strategizing" (Uber surge clubs, Amazon Flex phones in trees, Instagram engagement pods, AdNauseam, Twitter screenshotting) (§2.1), and defines the term: "We refer to technology-specific efforts of collectives in algorithm-managed work spaces as algorithmic collective action" (§2). It contrasts itself with Hardt et al. (2023): here "a platform that acts according to a fixed mechanism rather than a learned rule," where "The rigidity of the allocation rule naturally gives the collective fewer levers to turn" (§1.1). The discussion reports a driver-run experiment: acceptors earned "$0.68 per mile with a gross hourly wage of $12.41," selective decliners "$1.26 per mile with a gross hourly wage of $21.15" (§7), and a Deliveroo case where a refused job's offer rose "by 52% from £6.04 gross to £9.20."

## Relation to the argument

RQ8. Sorted: **collective standing**, not counter-delegation proper — the instrument is a shared rule that many humans follow by hand; no software acts for the driver, and the paper's own closing turn is toward "tools and apps for organizing participants" as future work (§7). Three points for the paper. First, this is the cleanest formal statement in the library of the construct's structural claim that the intermediary's determination is not the driver's to change but is *reachable* through the coordination: "by declining orders a driver cannot influence the overall pay level, they can only influence the pay for the subsequent driver on the same order... it must be a collective effort" (§2.2). Second, the oversupply result is a general constraint on Principle I that the paper has not yet stated: any counter-move by one party is discounted by how easily the intermediary can substitute another party, and the authors show the regime where individually rational participation collapses. A counter-agent that declines on a driver's behalf faces the same arithmetic. Third, Table 1 is a ready-made census of real client-side counter-moves, and AdNauseam (Brunton & Nissenbaum) is the only entry among the thirty that is a browser-side agent acting automatically on the user's behalf against the intermediary; the other software entries are data-collection or analytics services (WeClock, Driver's Seat Cooperative), an obfuscation tool for facial lookup (FoggySight) and user-built Waze bots, and the rest are manual practices — evidence, though not quite a null result, for the memo's finding that the counter-agent family is thin. CUTS AGAINST the paper in one respect: the customer, the second human, is modelled as an order stream with no interest and no strategy, and the paper's triad needs her to be a party; the model shows how the field collapses her even when she is on the page.

## Caution

A stylised model: one order per step, random assignment, constant busy time, a fixed increment, and parameters partly guessed ("We could not find documented information" on orders per hour, §4). The CHI version of record was not read; pin section numbers to it before citing. The 30-case table is compiled from forums, news and prior papers, not verified by the authors, and the driver experiment in §7 is a Facebook-group report. Nothing here measures what a worker knows or infers; it measures what a rule yields.
