## What it argues

MacIntyre defines a practice as "any coherent and complex form of socially established cooperative human activity through which goods internal to that form of activity are realized in the course of trying to achieve those standards of excellence which are appropriate to, and partially definitive of, that form of activity, with the result that human powers to achieve excellence, and human conceptions of the ends and goods involved, are systematically extended" (p. 30). The boundary cases are given: "Tic-tac-toe is not an example of a practice in this sense, nor is throwing a football with skill; but the game of football is, and so is chess. Bricklaying is not a practice; architecture is. Planting turnips is not a practice; farming is" (p. 30). The chess-for-candy child fixes the two kinds of good: external goods (candy, "prestige, status and money") can always be had some other way and are "always some individual's property and possession," so that "the more someone has of them, the less there is for other people"; internal goods "cannot be had in any way but by playing chess or some other game of that specific kind," and "can only be identified and recognized by the experience of participating in the practice in question. Those who lack the relevant experience are incompetent thereby as judges of internal goods" (pp. 30–31). Entering a practice means accepting "the authority of those standards and the inadequacy of my own performance as judged by them" (p. 31). A virtue is then "an acquired human quality the possession and exercise of which tends to enable us to achieve those goods which are internal to practices and the lack of which effectively prevents us from achieving any such goods" (p. 32). A practice "is never just a set of technical skills" (p. 33). The institutional contrast follows: "Chess, physics and medicine are practices; chess clubs, laboratories, universities and hospitals are institutions. Institutions are characteristically and necessarily concerned with what I have called external goods" (p. 33); no practice survives long without an institution, yet "the ideals and the creativity of the practice are always vulnerable to the acquisitiveness of the institution ... Without them, without justice, courage and truthfulness, practices could not resist the corrupting power of institutions" (p. 34). Where external goods dominate, "the concept of the virtues might suffer first attrition and then perhaps something near total effacement, although simulacra might abound" (p. 34).

## Relation to the argument

MacIntyre supplies the machinery RQ3 attributes to Vallor, and it maps onto both axes more exactly than Vallor's own text does. The practice/institution distinction is the objective axis in older dress: an intermediary that pursues engagement, revenue or throughput is an institution bearing a practice of coordination, and MacIntyre's claim that institutions "necessarily" pursue external goods predicts the cooptation reading rather than treating it as a pathology. But the internal-goods clause is what a structural account cannot supply: internal goods "can only be identified and recognized by the experience of participating" (p. 31), so the judge of whether a friction preserves the practice must be someone formed by it. Φ can detect that a third party is in the loop and the objective axis can name its goal; neither can say which goods the coordination was for, because that verdict is only available from inside. That is the precise sense in which the virtue criterion adds something rather than reducing to "whose objective." Two points cut against the construct. A practice is "cooperative human activity" (p. 30); an intermediary that is a party (Φ > 0) but cannot recognise internal goods is a non-human participant the theory has no place for, so a mediated triad is not straightforwardly a practice at all. And the chess child shows that friction which serves only external goods is not neutral but corrosive of the standards — a friction that a platform imposes for its own objective is exactly the "acquisitiveness of the institution."

## Caution

I read the 1981 journal excerpt, not the book chapter; the excerpt omits the narrative-unity and tradition stages that MacIntyre says the definition needs (p. 29), and its pages do not correspond to *After Virtue* pages (the practice definition is commonly cited at p. 187 of the 2nd/3rd editions; not verified here). The account is about communities of practitioners over historical time, with chess, portraiture and farming as cases; it says nothing about technology, mediation or design, and MacIntyre's own politics treat the modern state and market as the corrupting institutions. Vallor 2015 does not cite him; whether *Technology and the Virtues* (2016) does could not be verified in this pass.
