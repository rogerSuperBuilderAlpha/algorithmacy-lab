## What it argues

The book reports the Vai project in Liberia, where an indigenous syllabary is learned outside school, Arabic literacy is learned in Qur'anic schooling, and English literacy in state schools — a natural experiment separating literacy from schooling. What Ong verifies of the project: the Vai script is a syllabary in which "there is not a full one-to-one correspondence between the visual symbols and the units of sound. The writing provides only a kind of map to the utterance it registers, and it is very difficult to read, even for a skilled scribe (Scribner and Cole 1978, p. 456)" (Ong pp. 86–87); and in the Vai system "the writer can predict only approximately what the reader will read off" (Ong pp. 83–84). On syllogisms, Ong relays Fernandez's gloss on Cole and Scribner's Liberian work: "persons not academically educated are not acquainted with this special ground rule but tend rather ... to go beyond the statements themselves, as one does normally in real-life situations or in riddles" (Ong p. 52). The book's central result — the one the field cites and this card cannot verify from the text — is that general cognitive effects (abstraction, syllogistic reasoning, metalinguistic awareness) track *schooling*, not literacy as such, and that each literacy carries the specific skills of its practices ("literacy without schooling"), which Ong's book, published a year later, does not engage.

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. Scribner and Cole are the empirical reason the paper must confine itself to the competence/institution register: their finding, as reported everywhere, is that literacy's cognitive consequences are practice-specific. Read for the paper's purposes, that is not a loss but a support: if what literacy delivers is the competence of a particular practice, then what a member of a literate order had to be able to do is exactly the right question, and "algorithmacy" as a practice-bound competence is the right kind of construct. Ong's selective use of them (for the syllabary, not the null result) is itself a caution about how the orality–literacy literature handles disconfirming evidence.

The scaffolding essay's use. Not about print. Relevant only as the reason not to say that print "rendered inscribed knowledge pervasive" in any cognitive sense.
