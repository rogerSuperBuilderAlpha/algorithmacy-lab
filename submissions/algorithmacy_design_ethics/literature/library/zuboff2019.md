## What it argues

The book opens with an eight-clause definition, set as a page of its own before Chapter 1 ("The Definition"): surveillance capitalism is "1. A new economic order that claims human experience as free raw material for hidden commercial practices of extraction, prediction, and sales; 2. A parasitic economic logic in which the production of goods and services is subordinated to a new global architecture of behavioral modification; 3. A rogue mutation of capitalism marked by concentrations of wealth, knowledge, and power unprecedented in human history; 4. The foundational framework of a surveillance economy; 5. As significant a threat to human nature in the twenty-first century as industrial capitalism was to the natural world in the nineteenth and twentieth; 6. The origin of a new instrumentarian power that asserts dominance over society and presents startling challenges to market democracy; 7. A movement that aims to impose a new collective order based on total certainty; 8. An expropriation of critical human rights that is best understood as a coup from above: an overthrow of the people's sovereignty" (front matter; the scan and Olivier 2020 agree on the wording; Olivier's "p. 8" for it is wrong — p. 8 is the paragraph below). The working definition is the paragraph in Chapter 1 §II, "What Is Surveillance Capitalism?": "Surveillance capitalism unilaterally claims human experience as free raw material for translation into behavioral data. Although some of these data are applied to product or service improvement, the rest are declared as a proprietary *behavioral surplus*, fed into advanced manufacturing processes known as 'machine intelligence,' and fabricated into *prediction products* that anticipate what you will do now, soon, and later. Finally, these prediction products are traded in a new kind of marketplace for behavioral predictions that I call *behavioral futures markets*. Surveillance capitalists have grown immensely wealthy from these trading operations, for many companies are eager to lay bets on our future behavior" (p. 8, wording as in the scan; see Caution for a variant). The same section draws the line the draft needs: "When a firm collects behavioral data with permission and solely as a means to product or service improvement, it is committing capitalism but not surveillance capitalism" (ch. 1 §II), and states the turn from prediction to intervention: "surveillance capitalists discovered that the most-predictive behavioral data come from intervening in the state of play in order to nudge, coax, tune, and herd behavior toward profitable outcomes" (ch. 1 §II). Part III names the power. Chapter 12 opens: "As to this species of power, I name it instrumentarianism, defined as the instrumentation and instrumentalization of behavior for the purposes of modification, prediction, monetization, and control. In this formulation, 'instrumentation' refers to the puppet: the ubiquitous connected material architecture of sensate computation that renders, interprets, and actuates human experience. 'Instrumentalization' denotes the social relations that orient the puppet masters to human experience as surveillance capital wields the machines to transform us into means to others' market ends" (p. 352 per Johns). Zuboff insists the power is not coercive: "Totalitarianism was bent on the reconstruction of the human species through the dual mechanisms of genocide and the 'engineering of the soul.' Instrumentarian power, as we shall see, takes us in a sharply different direction. Surveillance capitalists have no interest in murder or the reformation of our souls" (ch. 12 §I); it "is best understood as the precise antithesis of Orwell's Big Brother" (ch. 12). The apparatus is "Big Other: it is the sensate, computational, connected puppet that renders, monitors, computes, and modifies human behavior. … Surveillance capitalism's economic logic is directed through Big Other's vast capabilities to produce instrumentarian power, replacing the engineering of souls with the engineering of behavior" (p. 376 per Huberman).

## Relation to the argument

The draft uses the phrase once, in the Vallor section: "Expecting personal mindfulness and ethical discernment to neutralize the coercive architectures of surveillance capitalism leaves the underlying monopolistic corporate incentives completely unaddressed." The phrase is Zuboff's coinage and the sentence uses it at her register — the objective encoded in the system is the firm's, and no individual discipline reaches it — so the citation is warranted: (Zuboff, 2019, p. 8) for the definition, or the book alone. One word cuts against her: *coercive*. Zuboff's whole Part III argument is that instrumentarian power is *not* coercion — it "has no interest in murder or the reformation of our souls" and works by "nudge, coax, tune, and herd" — and she builds the distinction against exactly the Orwellian reading the draft's adjective invites. Proposed wording: "Expecting personal mindfulness and ethical discernment to neutralize architectures built to tune and herd behavior toward the firm's ends — Zuboff's (2019) surveillance capitalism — leaves the underlying corporate incentives untouched." "Monopolistic" is defensible from clause 3 of her definition ("concentrations of wealth, knowledge, and power unprecedented in human history") but is not her word; "concentrated" is. FIT, with the adjective repaired. Scope limit for the paper: Zuboff models extraction from a source, not coordination between parties; the draft's triad (two humans and an intermediary whose objective neither set) is a case she does not treat, and her remedy is democratic law and collective action, not design — so she supports the Vallor-section diagnosis and nothing in the three principles. **Cross-reference:** the coordinative-sovereignty arm carries `submissions/coordinative_sovereignty/literature/library/cards/zuboff2019age.md` at `verified: abstract` from the Hachette catalogue page, with two page cites (57–58, 141–142) unconfirmed and its own audit rating the fit for that chapter's §3.1 "loose." ## Caution

A trade book, polemical by design, 127 pages of notes but no method; Johns (2020) faults it for ignoring the labour that builds the devices and cleans the data and for a "petit sovereign" individualism in its politics. Only three passages were read, through a search index of an unauthorised scan; the rest of the book is known here only through reviewers. **Wording variant:** the scan reads "applied to *product or* service improvement" and "*eager* to lay bets"; van Elteren (2019) and Elliott (2026) both print "applied to service improvement" and "*willing* to lay bets." Either two printings differ or the reviewers copied Zuboff's earlier published excerpt; the printed copy must be checked before the definition paragraph is quoted verbatim. The eight-clause definition is the safer quotation — scan and Olivier (2020) agree on it — and it carries no page number in the US edition. Do not use the Profile pagination with these pages.
