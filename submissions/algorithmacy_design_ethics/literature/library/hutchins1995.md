# Hutchins, E. (1995). *Cognition in the Wild*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.

**Identifier:** ISBN 9780262082310 (hardback); ISBN 9780262581462 (paperback, 1996) — both as verified against OpenLibrary edition records in the lab's existing card at `submissions/lima_pdw/literature/cards/hutchins1995.md` · **Read depth:** secondary (not opened in this pass; what follows rests on Christoffersen & Woods 2002, pp. 2–3, and Norman 1990, pp. 5–6 and reference list, both read in full, plus the publisher-description-level card above) · **Source-tier:** book, MIT Press; cognitive-anthropological monograph · **Evidence basis:** secondary · **Parties modeled:** many humans + artifacts over one shared task (a ship's navigation team) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

As the HF sources on this pass use it, Hutchins supplies the claim that cognition in a work setting is a property of the whole arrangement of people and artifacts, not of any one head. Christoffersen and Woods (2002, p. 3) cite the book for the observation that "what we actually see is a network of interdependent and mutually adapted activities and artifacts," so that "the cognitive demands of the work domain are not met simply by the sum of the efforts of individual agents working in isolation, but are met through the interaction and coordinated efforts of multiple people and machine agents," and (p. 2) for the conclusion that "automation and people have to coordinate as a joint system, a single team." Norman (1990), whose paper was funded on a NASA-Ames grant shared with Hutchins, credits Hutchins's then-forthcoming work on team navigation for showing "how this continual verbal interaction in a system with highly social crews serves to keep everyone attentive and informed, helps the continual training of new members of the crew, and serves as a natural monitor for error" (p. 5). The lab's existing card summarises the book's own argument: the unit is the socially distributed cognitive system, the method is tracing representational state as it propagates across media, and the ship's fix is computed by the arrangement rather than by a navigator.

## Relation to the argument

Structural axis, as the nearest HF-adjacent tradition to the irreducibility claim: if the cognitive unit is the joint system, then a coordination form in which no participant could compute the outcome alone is not exotic but the normal case Hutchins documents. That is the sense in which the pass brief calls it the closest tradition to Φ > 0. Two cautions follow from how the HF authors deploy it. First, the joint system is on one side of one task — a navigation team fixing a ship's position, a crew flying an aircraft — with a shared goal; the distributed system is irreducible with respect to the fix, not with respect to a coordination between two principals with divergent interests. Second, the HF citations use Hutchins to argue that automation should be *added* to the team as a member that keeps the loop open, which is the channel ideal again. On this pass's question: many humans, richly modelled, all co-members of one cognitive system; no counterpart party and no external objective.
