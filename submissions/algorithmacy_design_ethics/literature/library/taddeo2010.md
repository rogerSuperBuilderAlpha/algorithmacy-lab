# Taddeo, M. (2010). Modelling trust in artificial agents, a first step toward the analysis of e-trust. *Minds and Machines*, 20(2), 243–257.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/s11023-010-9201-3 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-deposited manuscript, University of Hertfordshire repository UHRA eprint 2209, file 904046.pdf, 19 pp.; the copy still carries "reference removed due to double blind review," so it is the accepted manuscript and section numbers below are its own, not journal pages) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed; the Floridi-school reference on e-trust, later cited by Mittelstadt et al. 2016 for the definition of trust · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** trustor–trustee dyad of artificial agents; extended in §5 to human–technology · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Trust is not a first-order relation between agents but "a second-order property of first-order relations." The definition (§4): given first-order relations functional to a goal, a trustor who "chooses to achieve its goal by the action performed by the trustee, and ... rationally selects the trustee on the basis of its trustworthiness" enters a relation that "has the property of minimising the trustor's effort and commitment in the achievement of that given goal. Such a property is a second-order property ... and is called e-trust." Symbolically T(P(A, B, g)): trust ranges over the purchasing relation between A and B about good g, not over B as such — otherwise "by trusting a seller on e-Bay one also trusts that seller to be a good lawyer or a good astronaut." Trustworthiness is prior and objective: a ratio of successful to total actions for a similar goal, compared to a threshold set above the mean, and it is goal-specific ("an AA that is trustworthy for ... collecting the data about the last n online purchases of a user, is not trustworthy for another goal, such as guaranteeing the privacy of the same user," §3). E-trust "does not occur a priori"; one-shot trust is possible only because trustworthiness is "a chronicle of an agent's actions" — reputation systems such as Web of Trust are the example. Trust's effect is to remove supervision: "the absence of supervision is justified there by the trustee's trustworthiness" (§4), and the level of e-trust is modelled as y = (1 − 2x)³ against resources spent on supervision, with e-distrust (y < 0) the state in which an agent "uses a high level of its resources to supervise (or ultimately replace) the dis-trustee's performances" (§4.1). Section 5 extends the definition to human trustors by raising the level of abstraction so that the criteria of trustworthiness are left unspecified, with an explicit footnote defining LoA (n. 14).

## Relation to the argument

RQ7, indirectly, and one of the cleaner CUTS AGAINST cards in the cluster. Taddeo's model is dyadic to the bone — trustor, trustee, first-order relation — and its whole point is that trust *reduces* supervision: the more one trusts, the less one monitors. Algorithmacy is the inverse competence: sustained interpretation, contestation and anticipation directed at an intermediary whose trustworthiness for the goal at hand is exactly what the human parties cannot assess, because the "chronicle" of its actions (ratings, match histories, scores) is authored by the intermediary itself. In Taddeo's own vocabulary the paper's construct is institutionalised e-distrust: high supervision resources spent on a party one is nonetheless bound to. The model also states, without noticing, the triadic problem: "The users of eBay ... trust its rating system" (§2) — the first-order relation P(A, B, g) between two humans runs through a platform whose rating system supplies the trustworthiness value both sides use, so the second-order property attaches to a relation the platform mediates. Taddeo treats this as a human-trusts-artefact case; for the paper it is the case where the trustworthiness chronicle is a determination the intermediary commits on both parties. The goal-specificity point is worth keeping: trust in an intermediary for matching is not trust in it for privacy, which supports indexing algorithmacy to the intermediary's objective rather than to the intermediary as such.

## Caution

A Kantian rational-agent idealisation for artificial agents in a distributed system, extended to humans only by sketch; the author says psychological factors are set aside because AAs have none. The y = (1 − 2x)³ curve is stipulated, not fitted, and the "power expresses friendliness of context" reading is an interpretation of an arbitrary exponent. No empirical work, no platform case, and the human extension (§5) is two paragraphs. The eBay remark is an aside, not an analysis.
