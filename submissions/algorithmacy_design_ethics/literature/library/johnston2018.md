## What it argues

"This paper examines challenges to freedom of association and the effective recognition of the right to collective bargaining for workers in the gig economy, and explores the broad range of strategies that gig-economy workers are using to build collective agency, and to promote effective regulation of gig work" (p. 1). Agency is defined "as intentional action that results in an observable outcome," expressed collectively "when individuals make decisions to act together to maximize their ability to exert influence" (p. 1, n. 1). "As has been the case throughout modern history, collective bargaining holds promise for responsive regulation balancing the needs of platforms, requestors, and those performing work through them. However, to be sustainable, improvements achieved through bargaining must be enforceable; a challenge we regularly observed" (p. 2). The structural obstacles: workers "labour independently, in isolation, over geographically expansive areas, and in direct competition with one another," with "high worker turnover rates" (p. 3). §7 reports that the project's own objective — "to identify and analyze examples of collective bargaining between workers and employers in the platform economy" — met "difficulty in locating specific examples of 'fully fledged' collective bargaining," for four reasons: novelty, the sector's small size, platform resistance, and competition law (p. 23). On resistance, Uber's Amit Singh is quoted saying "the purpose that collective bargaining was originally structured for doesn't necessarily hold," and the authors conclude: "many platform companies have been unwilling to bargain with workers directly" (p. 23). On law: "organized activity undertaken by independent contractors can be considered to be contrary to competition statutes or other anti-trust laws," and "Platform-based work often involves triangular relationships, making it difficult to identify the employer, and consequently, the bargaining counterpart" (p. 23). The FNV Kunsten (C-413/13) and Irish EQUITY cases show competition authorities treating self-employed workers as "undertakings" whose agreements are "price fixing" (pp. 24–25), with Ireland's 2017 Competition (Amendment) Act as the legislative repair (p. 25).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8. Sorted: **collective standing — the oldest instrument, and the evidence that the mediator resists it**. Three uses. First, the paper is the honest baseline for Principle I's collective half: as of 2017 the ILO could not find a completed collective agreement with a platform, platforms "have been unwilling to bargain," and the construct's "hold one's ground as it changes" has, at the collective level, almost no track record. Second, "triangular relationships, making it difficult to identify... the bargaining counterpart" is the construct's structural axis observed by labour lawyers: when the intermediary interprets both sides, neither side can locate the party it should bargain with, and the paper can cite this as an independent discipline's naming of the triad. Third, the competition-law finding — that workers who coordinate their prices against a platform are cartelists — is a legal ceiling on the collective strategies in hardt2023.md and sigg2025.md: #DeclineNow is a coordinated reservation price, and the ILO shows why such coordination is legally exposed for independent contractors. CUTS AGAINST the paper's Principle I in that everything here is about *standing to bargain*, obtained through organisations, and nothing about an agent acting inside a coordination; the paper should cite it for the reason collective standing must precede counter-delegation (a counter-agent that declines orders on a contractor's behalf faces the same "undertaking" problem), not as a source for the instrument.

## Caution

A working paper, largely secondary, with interviews unnamed except where quoted; §§2–6, which contain the actual cases (Deliveroo, Uber guilds, Turkopticon forums, cooperatives), were not read this round and should be before any case is cited from it. Evidence ends in December 2017; the EU Platform Work Directive (2024) and the Commission's 2022 guidelines on collective agreements for solo self-employed post-date it and change the competition-law picture the paper describes. The year discrepancy (2018 catalogue, 2019 imprint) is unresolved.
