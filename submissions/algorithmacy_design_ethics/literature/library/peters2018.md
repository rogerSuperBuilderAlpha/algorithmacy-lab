## What it argues

The paper proposes METUX (Motivation, Engagement and Thriving in User Experience), a self-determination-theory (SDT) model for "how technology designs support or undermine basic psychological needs" (abstract, p. 1). The needs are the three SDT basics, defined on p. 2: "Autonomy (feeling agency, acting in accordance with one's goals and values), Competence (feeling able and effective), Relatedness (feeling connected to others, a sense of belonging)," and they are needs "whose neglect or frustration is associated with ill-being and distress" (p. 2). SDT's strict definition of a basic need is given as four bullets on p. 3: satisfactions that "are inherently rewarding/motivational," "when satisfied lead to flourishing," "when frustrated lead to negative experience," and "function across diverse cultures and developmental stages."

The paper's framing question is the one this arm wants: interaction designers have shifted "from mere usability to also making products enjoyable and engaging, generally with the goal of increasing usage," but "too much engagement can crowd-out healthy activities to the detriment of overall wellbeing. Thus a larger question remains: How can technology be designed to support wellbeing that encompasses more than just immediate hedonic experience, but also its longer-term eudaimonia, or true flourishing?" (p. 2). The gap it names in the existing wellbeing-design frameworks (Positive Technologies, Experience Design, Positive Design, Positive Computing) is that "none of the frameworks provides help or guidance on how design can disentangle engaging experiences that are healthy from engaging experiences that are addictive," so "more bridge-building is necessary before the practice of wellbeing-supportive design can be robustly deployed across the industry" (p. 2).

The paper's own vocabulary for the pairing the draft needs is *support/undermine* and *need-satisfying/need-frustrating*. The key sentence: "Acknowledgment of these differing spheres of experience is essential if we are to avoid creating technologies that are need-satisfying at one level but need-frustrating at another (i.e., addictive)" (p. 4). Elsewhere: "a technology can support psychological needs at one level while undermining them at another" (p. 7); "Poor usability will cause need frustration (to autonomy and competence)" (p. 9); "Many app features and communication devices can even frustrate relatedness" (p. 5); "psychological need-support within these environments has a significant impact on domain-specific (e.g., health, work and education) outcomes" (p. 10); and "autonomy-supportive" / "autonomy-support" throughout (pp. 4–6). The phrase "need thwarting" occurs only in a cited title (Bartholomew et al., 2011, in the reference list, p. 13), never in the authors' own prose.

The six spheres (Table 1, p. 8) are adoption, interface, task, behavior, life, and society. Two carry weight for this arm. The *life* sphere "is also critical for identifying addiction": "A casual game, for example, can be so need satisfying within the first few spheres, that at the life sphere, important activities get crowded out leading to drops in relatedness as human relationships are ignored, drops in autonomy as they feel less able to make decisions aligned with their values. Therefore, any technology wishing to claim it improves wellbeing, or even that it merely doesn't harm it, will need to measure at the life level" (p. 10). The *society* sphere "is the only one to step beyond the user experience," and its example is a self-driving car whose "collateral impact ... on the livelihoods of the millions who survive off of driving taxis, buses and trucks can only be revealed at a societal level of analysis" (p. 10).

Autonomy is defined against control: it "should not be confused with merely doing things independently or being in control; rather when people act with autonomy, they act with high willingness and in accordance with their personal goals and values, connecting autonomy to meaning & purpose. An individual can very willingly relinquish control or embrace interdependence" (p. 4). The design gloss: "Devices that offer options and choices over use, and do not in turn demand actions from users without their assent, enhance feelings of autonomy" (p. 5). The paper cites Friedman (1996) on "four aspects of software systems than can support or hinder user autonomy" but faults her for staying at "the interface sphere" (p. 5). On motivation type, the model distinguishes autonomous from controlled motivation and lets compulsion in through the adoption sphere: someone "may be required by their workplace to use it, in which case, they may not perceive any use for it at all but adopt the technology anyway for fear of penalty (externally regulated extrinsic motivation)" (p. 9); the SDT reframing of "perceived use" "allows for compulsory use to be taken into account" (p. 8). The instruments (ACTA, TENS-Interface, TENS-Task, TENS-Life) are in appendices; TENS-Life items such as "I spend more time on the technology than I feel I should" are meant to identify "autonomy frustrations that may relate to addictive patterns" (pp. 11–12).

## Relation to the argument

The draft sentence this card repairs (DRAFT.md, Introduction): "Evaluating a design through the lens of human flourishing requires examining whether an artifact fosters 'meaning-affording design' or installs an extractive, 'meaning-hostile' environment that fractures daily life, hijacks attention, and replaces authentic agency with algorithmic control."

Neither quoted phrase is this paper's. "Meaning-affording" does not occur; "meaning-hostile" does not occur; and the plan's hypothesised pair "need-supportive versus need-thwarting" is only half right, since the authors write "support or undermine," "need-satisfying ... need-frustrating," and "need-support," and reserve "thwarting" for a reference title. What the paper does supply is the real vocabulary for the contrast the draft wants, and it is more specific than the draft's: a technology can be *need-satisfying at the interface and task spheres while need-frustrating at the life sphere*, which is the paper's own definition of "addictive" (p. 4), and the hedonic/eudaimonic split (p. 2) is the paper's gloss on why engagement is not flourishing. Proposed replacement wording:

> Evaluating a design through the lens of human flourishing requires asking whether an artifact supports or frustrates the basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence and relatedness—that self-determination theory ties to wellbeing (Peters, Calvo & Ryan, 2018, p. 2). Peters and colleagues' own test is the one that matters here: a technology can be "need-satisfying at one level but need-frustrating at another (i.e., addictive)" (p. 4), engaging at the interface while crowding out autonomy and relatedness in a person's life as a whole (p. 10).

The source cuts against the draft in one place. The draft's "meaning-hostile environment" is a claim about the surrounding *environment* the user cannot leave; Peters et al. model a single user and a single product, and their frame treats compulsion only as a motivation type at adoption (the boss who forces the download, p. 7; "fear of penalty," p. 9), not as a structural condition. The society sphere is where they put "collateral" effects, and their example is displaced drivers, not a coordinated second party. So the paper grounds the vocabulary of need support and need frustration; it does not ground the draft's claim that platform-scale systems are "the mandatory social terrain through which all human coordination occurs." That remains this arm's extension, and the draft should not attach it to the SDT citation.

For the Berthon section, the same paper gives the draft a better anchor than Berthon for the *diagnosis* of attention capture: the healthy/addictive engagement distinction (p. 2) and the life-sphere crowd-out (p. 10) are stated here with a measurement proposal attached, which berthon2019.md's column lacks.

## Caution

A theory paper with instrument proposals, not an empirical test; the authors say the measures "are initial iterations that will require more thorough validation" and that "the spheres themselves are approximations" (p. 12). The evidence base is SDT's own literature (Ryan and Deci, Rigby and Ryan on games, Ng et al. on health), and the third author is SDT's co-founder, so the paper is advocacy for its own framework. "Eudaimonia" appears once, in the framing question (p. 2), not as a worked construct; a draft that leans on "eudaimonic versus hedonic" should cite Ryan and Deci (2001) or Huta directly rather than this paper. Two of the authors are also the authors of *Positive Computing* (Calvo & Peters, 2014), which is cited here as one of four prior frameworks (p. 2) and whose definitions this paper subsumes for the draft's purposes; no separate card was made for the book.
