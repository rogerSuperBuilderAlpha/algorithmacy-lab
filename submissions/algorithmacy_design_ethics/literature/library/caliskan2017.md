## What it argues

The draft abstract: "Here we show for the first time that human-like semantic biases result from the application of standard machine learning to ordinary language—the same sort of language humans are exposed to every day. We replicate a spectrum of standard human biases as exposed by the Implicit Association Test and other well-known psychological studies. We replicate these using a widely used, purely statistical machine-learning model—namely, the GloVe word embedding—trained on a corpus of text from the Web" (p. 1). The method contribution is "the Word Embedding Association Test (WEAT) and the Word Embedding Factual Association Test (WEFAT)" (p. 1). WEAT (Methods, p. 9) takes two sets of target words X and Y and two sets of attribute words A and B; for a word w, s(w, A, B) is the mean cosine similarity of w to the A words minus its mean cosine similarity to the B words; the test statistic sums s over X and subtracts the sum over Y; a permutation over the partitions of X ∪ Y gives a one-sided p-value; the effect size is the difference of the mean s across X and across Y divided by the standard deviation of s across X ∪ Y. The authors add: "these p-values and effect sizes don't have the same interpretation as the IAT. The 'subjects' in our experiments are words, not people" (p. 9). The stimuli are borrowed, never invented: "In the present paper we have never invented our own basket of words, but rather have in every case used the same words as were used in the psychological study we are replicating" (p. 2).

The three named replications, with the draft's numbers: (1) flowers versus insects against pleasant/unpleasant — IAT effect size 1.35, WEAT "effect size of 1.50 and with p-value < 10⁻⁷" (p. 3); (2) European-American versus African-American names against pleasant/unpleasant — IAT 1.17, WEAT "effect size of 1.41 and p-value < 10⁻⁸" (p. 4), with some low-frequency African-American names dropped and an equal number of European-American names deleted at random; the Bertrand and Mullainathan résumé-study names give 1.50 and 1.28 on two attribute sets (p. 4); (3) female names with family words and male names with career words — IAT 0.72, WEAT "effect size of 1.81 and p-value < 10⁻³" (p. 5).

Two framing claims matter beyond the numbers. On the source of the bias: "Bias should be the expected result whenever even an unbiased algorithm is used to derive regularities from any data; bias is the regularities discovered" (p. 2). On remedies: transparency, developer diversity and domain-expert collaboration "might be helpful and even necessary, [but] they could not be sufficient" (p. 2); "prejudice is a special case of bias identifiable only by its negative consequences, and therefore impossible to eliminate purely algorithmically. Rather, prejudice requires deliberate action based on knowledge of a society and its outstanding ethical challenges" (p. 2); "It cannot be entirely eliminated from the system, but rather must be compensated for" (p. 2).

## Relation to the argument

Repairs the draft sentence in "Moral Hazards," bullet 4: "As demonstrated by the Princeton/Bath word embedding studies and Stanford's facial recognition research, deep learning architectures consistently operationalize harmful demographic proxies." The "Princeton/Bath" gesture is accurate and should become a citation. Two corrections travel with it. First, GloVe is a word embedding fitted to co-occurrence statistics, not a "deep learning architecture"; the exact phrase is "word embeddings trained on ordinary web text." Second, the paper does not show a system *operationalizing* a proxy in a decision; it shows that the geometry of the embedding reproduces the associations the IAT finds in people. The inference to hiring harm runs through the Bertrand and Mullainathan analogy, not through a deployed system. Proposed wording: "Word embeddings trained on ordinary web text reproduce the race and gender associations that the Implicit Association Test finds in people (Caliskan, Bryson & Narayanan 2017)." The same card also grounds the first clause of bullet 3, "Natural language processing architectures consistently internalize and amplify systemic historical biases" — "internalize" is supported (p. 2, "bias is the regularities discovered"); "amplify" is not tested here and should go, or be carried by hofmann2024.md, which does show amplification under scale.

A second use is available and stronger for this paper's argument. The authors' own remedy stance (p. 2) is that transparency and openness of code "could not be sufficient" and that prejudice "must be compensated for" by "deliberate action based on knowledge of a society" — that is, the fix lies outside the artifact and outside reading it. This is the field's own admission that a literacy remedy does not reach the harm, and it sits well beside mitchell2019.md and green2019.md.

CUTS AGAINST the draft if bullet 4 is read as attributing the "inevitable" claim to this study. Nothing in Caliskan et al. concerns privacy or inevitability; that clause belongs to wangkosinski2018.md, and the bullet must keep the two citations attached to their own claims.

## Caution

A corpus audit of one public embedding (GloVe, web-trained) in the draft read; the draft promises "similar results for other standard tools and corpora, which we will also discuss in future versions" (p. 3), and what the *Science* version adds on that point is unverified here. Effect sizes are properties of word sets, not of people, and the authors say so (p. 9). Some original IAT stimuli were altered for corpus frequency (p. 4). No deployed system, no decision and no user is examined. The draft's title makes a stronger claim ("necessarily contain") than the published title ("contain human-like"); quote from the published version before the phrase enters the draft.
