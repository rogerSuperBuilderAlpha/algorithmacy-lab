# Cox, A. L., Gould, S. J. J., Cecchinato, M. E., Iacovides, I., & Renfree, I. (2016). Design frictions for mindful interactions: The case for microboundaries. *CHI'16 Extended Abstracts on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, 1389–1397.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/2851581.2892410 · **Read depth:** full_text (accepted manuscript, White Rose Research Online eprint 130554; unpaginated, so quotes below carry no page numbers) · **Source-tier:** CHI extended abstract (alt.chi/late-breaking track, lightly reviewed); a position paper citing the authors' own lab studies · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Against the UX consensus that "design frictions" are "points of difficulty ... [that] should be removed from technology in order to reduce the risk of user disengagement," the authors argue that small, intentional frictions can move a user from Kahneman's System 1 to System 2. Their unit is the microboundary: "an intervention that provides a small obstacle prior to an interaction that prevents us rushing from one context to another ... a smaller version of the effect of having a credit card in a block of ice." Three benefits are claimed, each with a lab or field example from the authors' group: accuracy (Soboczenski et al. 2013 found that a less legible font raised transcription accuracy with no change in completion time, which they read as a shift to a deliberate strategy); value-led behaviour (knowledge workers in Cecchinato et al. 2015 installed separate email apps so as not to answer work mail on a Saturday night); and data quality (task lockouts that force a pause reduce clicking through modal dialogs, but "if lockouts are excessively long, people will instead use the time to work on other tasks"). Photographing food before eating is read as a microboundary that behaviour-change apps lose when they automate capture. They draw the line against dark patterns by mechanism: "Dark patterns of design nudge users into behaviours which are not necessarily desired, by making sure the users do not leave System1. Microboundaries instead are used to actively support the user into shifting from System1 to System2." They are "not advocating a digital detox," and they flag two open questions: "how habituation to microboundaries through frequent exposure could influence effectiveness" and "how the level of autonomy a user has over the introduction of the microboundary affects both its effectiveness but also engagement and retention."

## Relation to the argument

This is the design-side home of the cognitive-forcing rationale that Buçinca et al. (2021) later test, and it offers a third candidate criterion for RQ3 beside Vallor's and the cooptation reading: friction is worth its cost when it changes the processing mode, regardless of whose objective it serves. That criterion is cognitive, not moral, and it is silent on formation — a microboundary that makes me deliberate once does not make me better at deliberating. On the objective axis the paper is nearer the cooptation reading than it admits: every worked example is a friction the user chose or that serves the user's own stated value (work–life boundary, dietary goal), and the dark-pattern contrast turns on who benefits as much as on which system fires. Its two open questions are the Buçinca problem stated in advance: habituation erodes the effect, and frictions imposed rather than chosen cost engagement. For the construct, the paper supports the claim that a channel (an email app, a food diary) can carry friction serving the user without any third objective, which is the literacy end of the spectrum; it says nothing about intermediaries that interpret both parties.

## Caution

Extended abstract, roughly 3,000 words, no new data; the evidence is a handful of the authors' own studies (transcription, lockouts, email interviews) with small samples and short horizons. "Mindful" is defined as "deliberate and intentional," not in the contemplative sense. No test of user preference for the frictions, so it cannot answer whether users tolerate them; Buçinca (2021) supplies the missing preference data and it is negative. No generative-AI or coordination setting.
