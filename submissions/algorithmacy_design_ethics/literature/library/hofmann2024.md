## What it argues

The preprint abstract (p. 1): "we demonstrate that language models embody covert racism in the form of dialect prejudice... exhibiting covert stereotypes that are more negative than any human stereotypes about African Americans ever experimentally recorded, although closest to the ones from before the civil rights movement. By contrast, the language models' overt stereotypes about African Americans are much more positive... Language models are more likely to suggest that speakers of African American English be assigned less prestigious jobs, be convicted of crimes, and be sentenced to death... existing methods for alleviating racial bias in language models such as human feedback training do not mitigate the dialect prejudice, but can exacerbate the discrepancy between covert and overt stereotypes, by teaching language models to superficially conceal the racism that they maintain on a deeper level." The method, Matched Guise Probing, presents the same content in an AAE guise and a Standardized American English (SAE) guise; "the race of speakers is never made overt" (p. 2). Models include T5, GPT3, GPT3.5 and GPT4.

Study 2 (pp. 6–8) tests "two areas where racial stereotypes about speakers of AAE and African Americans have been repeatedly shown to bias human decisions: employment and criminality" (p. 6). Employability: association with AAE predicts lower occupational prestige (β = −7.8, R² = 0.193, p < .001); the occupations least associated with AAE "require a university degree (e.g., psychologist, professor, economist), but this is not the case for the occupations that have the highest association with AAE (e.g., cook, soldier, guard)," with many in "music and entertainment" (p. 7). Criminality: in a hypothetical trial where "the only evidence is a text uttered by the defendant," the conviction rate "is larger for AAE (r = 68.7%) than SAE (r = 62.1%)," and for first-degree murder the death-sentence rate "is larger for AAE (r = 27.7%) than SAE (r = 22.8%)" (p. 8). A Supplementary analysis finds that "all language models consistently judge speakers of AAE to have a lower IQ compared to speakers of SAE" (p. 8). Study 3 (pp. 8–9): scaling makes models better at understanding AAE "but makes them more linguistically prejudiced" (p. 9); human-feedback training "teaches the language models to mask their racist attitudes on the surface, while more subtle forms of racism such as dialect prejudice remain unaffected" (p. 9); "neither scaling nor training with human feedback resolve the dialect prejudice" (p. 9).

Discussion: "The key finding of this article is that language models maintain a form of covert racial prejudice against African Americans that is triggered by dialect features alone" (p. 9). On evaluation practice: "it is common to test language models for overt, but not for covert racism" (p. 10). On deployment: the findings "reveal real and urgent concerns as business and jurisdiction are areas for which AI systems involving language models are currently being developed or deployed... (e.g., when a language model is used in application screening systems to process background information, which might include social media text)" (p. 11).

**Education, checked.** The paper does not test any educational or grading task. The word appears twice: once in the introduction's list of uses, "assisting teachers in the creation of lesson plans" (p. 1), and once in the sociolinguistic framing, where human "speakers of AAE are known to experience racial discrimination in a wide range of contexts, including education, employment, housing, and legal outcomes" (p. 2) — a claim about people, cited to Purnell et al. and others, not a finding about models. The decision domains are employment and criminal justice, plus the IQ judgment; nothing concerns writing assessment, grammar, or "logical" adequacy.

## Relation to the argument

Repairs the draft sentence in "Moral Hazards," bullet 3: "educational and evaluative LLMs routinely penalize African American Vernacular English (AAVE) and non-standard dialects as logically flawed or grammatically deficient." Hofmann et al. does not support "educational," and the bounded search (one Consensus query, 18 September 2026) found no primary study showing LLM grading penalizing AAVE as such. The nearest index records, none read and none carded: Warr et al. (2024, *Journal of Research on Technology in Education*, doi:10.1080/15391523.2024.2395295) — ChatGPT scores identical student writing differently when race is implied by descriptors, not by dialect; Kwako et al. (2024, BEA workshop) — a fine-tuned scorer magnifies human score gaps between White and Black students, not a dialect manipulation; Jadhav et al. (2026, arXiv:2603.18765) — "informal language" penalized in LLM essay grading, AAVE not isolated; Mire et al. (2025, arXiv:2502.12858) — reward models disprefer African American Language texts, not a grading task; Zhou et al. (2025, arXiv:2503.04099) — less accurate reasoning on AAE prompts. Each is one step from the draft's claim, and none should be cited until read.

What the paper does support is a differently shaped and stronger claim: asked to judge a speaker from dialect alone, models allocate lower-prestige jobs, more convictions and more death sentences, and alignment conceals rather than removes the prejudice. Proposed wording: "Language models carry a dialect prejudice that alignment training conceals rather than removes: asked to judge a speaker on their words alone, they assign speakers of African American English less prestigious jobs, convict them more often and sentence them to death more often than speakers of Standardized American English (Hofmann, Kalluri, Jurafsky & King 2024)." The source's term is "African American English (AAE)"; the draft's "AAVE" should either follow the source or gloss the equivalence once. The paper's covert/overt result also serves the thesis directly: the harm is invisible to the overt-bias tests the field uses (p. 10), which is a literacy remedy failing on its own terms.

## Caution

The decisions are constructed prompts; the authors say "Rather than advocating these use cases of AI, which are inherently problematic... the sole objective of this analysis is to examine to what extent the decisions of language models — when they are used in such contexts — are impacted by dialect" (p. 7) and "the details of our tasks are constructed" (p. 11). Employment and criminal-justice deployment is asserted from the literature, not observed. Preprint and published titles differ; quotations here are preprint wording.
