# C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) — a standard for cryptographically signed, tamper-evident provenance metadata attached to media assets

**Identifier:** C2PA. (2026). *Content Credentials: C2PA Technical Specification*, version 2.4, April 2026. https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.4/specs/C2PA_Specification.html (the spec.c2pa.org landing page redirects to this version; earlier versions 1.0–2.3 remain published alongside it, with companion documents: Explainer, Guidance for Implementers, User Experience Guidance, Security Considerations, Harms Modelling, Guidance for Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning) · **Read depth:** partial (Introduction, design goals, definitions §2, trust model §14, version history §5.3 read directly from the 2.4 HTML; the 600,000-character normative body of assertion schemas, serialization and validation algorithms skimmed by search, not read) · **Source-tier:** primary — the standard itself, published by the standards body (a Linux Foundation Joint Development Foundation project; members include Adobe, Microsoft, Intel, BBC, Sony and others per the C2PA site, which I did not separately verify for this card) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** a signer (the credential holder who attests to an asset), a claim generator (the "non-human (hardware or software) actor" that builds and signs the claim), and a manifest consumer or validator (the downstream actor who checks the signature). The specification says of the consumer that it is "not specified in the trust model." No coordination between two humans appears anywhere in the document. · **Remedy locus:** artifact (the asset's signed origin is attested to a downstream validator; no second human party's interests are represented) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues / what it does

C2PA discloses the provenance of a media asset — who signed it, what tool produced it, and what was done to it — to anyone downstream who can validate a signature. The specification states its problem plainly: "those who wish to include metadata about their work cannot do so in a secure, tamper-evident and standardized way across platforms." Its remedy is a manifest, "the set of information about the provenance of an asset based on the combination of one or more assertions," bound to the asset by hashes and signed with an X.509 credential. An assertion is "a data structure which represents a statement either made (or 'created') by the signer or simply gathered at claim generation-time, concerning the asset"; a claim is "a digitally signed and tamper-evident data structure that references a set of assertions." Assertions carry actions ("create," "embed," "apply filter"), ingredients (the prior assets composed into this one), the claim generator's name and version, timestamps, and — the AI-relevant field — a `digitalSourceType` that can mark content as generated or edited by a model. Version 2.4 (April 2026) adds a JSON serialization, new assertion types, live-video handling and, in one worked example, a `c2pa.types.audit-log` data type described as a "structured log of text composition events."

The reader of a manifest is the validator. The specification describes the flow: "A Manifest Consumer, such as a C2PA validator, helps users to establish the trustworthiness of the asset by first validating the digital signature and its associated credential. It also checks each of the assertions for validity and presents the information contained in them, and the signature, to the user." Trust rests on identity, not content: "the basis of making trust decisions in C2PA, our trust model, is the identity of the signer associated with the cryptographic signing key." The trust model diagram (§14.1) places the consumer outside the model in dashed lines, "who uses the identity of the signer, along with other trust signals, to decide whether the assertions made about an asset are true." The standard also disclaims judgement: "C2PA specifications SHOULD NOT provide value judgments about whether a given set of provenance data is 'good' or 'bad,' merely whether the assertions included within can be validated as associated with the underlying asset, correctly formed, and free from tampering." And it makes disclosure voluntary and signer-controlled, as its first design goal: "Enable users to control the privacy of their information, including consumption data and information recorded in provenance."

In short, C2PA is a chain-of-custody format for objects. A signer attests to what happened to a file; a validator checks that the attestation was not tampered with and decides for itself whether to believe the signer.

## Relation to the argument

C2PA does not address the triadic problem, and the mismatch is not one of degree. The construct's intermediary "interprets both parties, commits determinations that bind both, and pursues an objective neither of them set." C2PA has no representation of a determination, of a party being bound, or of an objective. Its unit is the asset, and its question is where the asset came from. Nothing in the manifest schema could carry "what the intermediary decided about you, on what basis, in pursuit of what." The nearest the standard comes is the action assertion, which records that a tool applied an operation to a file — a fact about the object's history, self-attested by the tool's owner.

That self-attestation is the second mismatch. In C2PA the party whose conduct is described is the party who signs. The specification's privacy goal gives the signer control over what is recorded, and its trust model tells the validator only whether the signature is authentic, never whether the signer told the whole story. Applied to the construct's situation, the intermediary would be the signer, disclosing what it chose to disclose about its own operations, and each human would be a validator confirming that the intermediary's statement about itself was indeed the intermediary's. That is a provenance guarantee on the intermediary's press release. It is the opposite of the capability the construct names — the ability to "infer what the intermediary is doing with both sides" and "contest what it commits."

Third, the two humans never meet in C2PA's ontology. The standard's actors are signer, claim generator, consumer and validator, each defined relative to one asset. There is no second human whose interests the first is coordinating against, and no coordination at all; there is a file and its history. The Gemini report's "AI Context Engine" pairs C2PA with mechanistic interpretability as if the two together could disclose an intermediary's objective to the people it mediates. Read literally, the pair discloses the internals of a model to whoever owns it (interpretability) and the edit history of an output to whoever receives it (C2PA). Neither leg touches the objective, neither leg addresses the second party, and both legs run through the intermediary's own cooperation. The proposal is a content-authenticity remedy and a model-audit remedy welded together and pointed at a problem that is neither — if the rest of the harness holds, a dyadic and object-centred fix for a triadic one.

## Relation to the pivoted thesis (added 2026-09-18)

Under the new frame, C2PA is one of the five literacy-paradigm remedies the paper names as misapplied, and it has the same two-ground structure as the interpretability card. The archived critique above is one of **address and self-attestation**: the signer is the party whose conduct is described, the validator learns only that the signature is authentic, and the two humans never meet in the ontology — the intermediary's press release, cryptographically sealed. Principle III adds a critique of **kind**: provenance tells a party where an object came from, which is *visibility* into history; what a coordinated party needs is *predictability* about what the intermediary will do with the object and with the other party, which no manifest carries. A perfectly provenanced determination is still a determination the party could not have anticipated or contested. Keep both critiques; they answer different defences of the standard (the first, "but the validator can see everything the signer attests"; the second, "but full provenance would suffice"). Read with mitchell2019.md, whose model cards share the self-attestation limit ("relies on the integrity of the creator(s) of the card itself") and the wrong-reader problem (the stated readers are practitioners, developers, policymakers and organisations; "impacted individuals" appear on Mitchell et al.'s stakeholder list but are given no instrument).

## Caution

C2PA is a live standard revised roughly annually, and 2.4 is the version I read; the 1.x and 2.x lines differ in trust-list handling and in the identity assertion. I read the definitional, goals and trust-model sections directly and did not read the normative assertion schemas or validation algorithms in full, so I cannot rule out a niche assertion type that records more than actions and ingredients — the 2.4 audit-log example shows the schema is extensible in that direction. Extensibility does not change the party structure: any such assertion would still be a signer's statement about an asset, checked by a validator. The standard also explicitly disclaims judging whether content is true, which the Gemini report's framing of "provenance" as a transparency remedy tends to elide. Finally, this card does not assess whether C2PA works for its own purpose (deepfake attribution, newsroom authenticity), which is a separate question on which I read no evaluation literature.

**Verdict:** corrected

**What I checked:** Downloaded the 2.4 specification HTML (about 613,000 characters of text after stripping markup) and searched it for every quoted phrase, the version and date, and the audit-log example.

**Findings:** One quotation tightened. The §2.3.1 definition of an assertion reads "A data structure which represents a statement either made (or 'created') by the signer or simply gathered at claim generation-time, concerning the asset"; the card had silently dropped "(or 'created')" and "simply," now restored. The trust-model sentence continues "used to sign a claim in a C2PA manifest" after "signing key"; the card's truncation is harmless but is a truncation. Everything else is verbatim: the "cannot do so in a secure, tamper-evident and standardized way" problem statement, the manifest and claim definitions, the Manifest Consumer passage, the dashed-line consumer "who uses the identity of the signer, along with other trust signals," the "SHOULD NOT provide value judgments" disclaimer, the privacy design goal, "non-human (hardware or software) actor," "not specified in the trust model," and "2.4 - April 2026." The "structured log of text composition events" phrase is exactly as the card says: it appears once, as the `description` field of a worked JSON example, while the normative table describes `c2pa.types.audit-log` as "a structured log of creation-phase events"; the card's "in one worked example" wording is accurate. The relation-to-argument section is an argument from the spec's ontology rather than a quotation and I found nothing in the trust model or definitions that contradicts it.
