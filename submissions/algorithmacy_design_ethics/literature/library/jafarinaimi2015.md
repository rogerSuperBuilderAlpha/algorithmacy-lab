## What it argues

Values scholarship in HCI runs on an "identify/apply logic" — "1) the need to identify and understand values so that 2) they can be better applied to design practice" (p. 93) — and every school shares it: Friedman et al.'s list and tripartite method, Flanagan et al.'s discovery-then-translation, Fleischmann's "meta-inventory," Borning and Muller's shared online lists, and "even the scholars offering alternative approaches," since Le Dantec et al.'s local discovery still identifies first and applies second (p. 94). The logic "rests on a core assumption: Values can, in theory and scholarship, be adequately addressed separately from action" (p. 94). Two objections. "We argue that not all local values are valuable, either. After all, self-interest is a value likely present in local situations — that may lead to or be expressed as intolerance and bigotry. In addition, the commonplace understanding of values in VSD as what a person or group considers important in life is not serving the values and design community because it emphasizes the identification of values rather than their service in design situations" (p. 94). And a value's bearing is unstable: Friedman's own autonomy example shows that "autonomy is protected when users are given control over the right things at the right time. Of course, the hard work of design is to decide these whats and whens" (Friedman, quoted p. 92), so "an autonomy that is highly appropriate in one circumstance may be highly problematic in another" (p. 92). The positive account is Dewey's: ethics is "a practical matter concerned with the question of action: 'What are the conditions which require action, and what is the action which they demand?'" (p. 96), and "values are not applied to situations; rather, values serve situations as hypotheses" (p. 97). Thirty designers were given Envisioning Cards and fifteen interviewed a month later (p. 98 n. 34); the conclusion drawn is that "prompts, checklists, and step-by-step procedures fail to capture the link of values and design and certainly cannot replace the hard work of design — that is, to decide the whats and whens" (p. 100). The case is Luke, a hospital custodian who re-cleans a room he has already cleaned after a distraught father accuses him: a "privacy" hypothesis "does not show potential to functionally relate what the situation is and what is to be done," while "justice" does, and in serving the situation the idea of justice itself develops "away from being solely about individual rights" (p. 101). Design and values are "storied" (pp. 102–103), and the closing line is: "Service to the situations of human life and living is the value of values" (p. 104).

## Relation to the argument

The sharpest of the critiques, and for RQ5 it does two things at once. It gives the plainest reason VSD cannot adjudicate a cooptation case: on VSD's definition a value is whatever a party considers important, so an operator's extraction interest enters the analysis with the same standing as the mediated parties' interests — the "self-interest is a value" sentence is the closest this literature comes to saying so — and the tripartite method has no step at which standing is withdrawn. The authors' replacement is not a rule but a test: a value is a hypothesis that either makes a problematic situation "more productive" or does not. Applied to the construct, the intermediary's objective is a hypothesis that serves the operator's situation and not the coordination between the parties; the paper supplies the vocabulary for that judgment while, like the rest of the cluster, leaving open who gets to make it. Second, it relocates value work into the situation of action, which for a mediated coordination is the interface — so it supports the affordances as the place where the seam's disclosures are tested against what the situation demands. It cuts against them too: fixed "prompts, checklists, and step-by-step procedures" are exactly what an affordance risks becoming, and the paper's charge against the identify/apply logic would apply to any account of algorithmacy that reduces it to a competence checklist. The Luke case has no intermediary and no third party; nothing here reaches the structural axis.

## Caution

A philosophical essay whose empirical component is small (30 practitioners, 15 interviews, thematic analysis) and whose findings page I could not read; the Luke story is borrowed from Schwartz and Sharpe's *Practical Wisdom*, not observed by the authors, and Dewey is read through Schwartz and Sharpe as much as directly. No algorithmic system, no automated intermediary, and no design outcome appears.
