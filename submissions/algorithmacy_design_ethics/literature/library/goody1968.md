What Ong takes from it: from the Introduction, that "the residual orality of a given chirographic culture can be calculated to a degree from the mnemonic load it leaves on the mind, that is, from the amount of memorization the culture's educational procedures require" (pp. 13–14; Ong p. 41), and that "illiterates profit from rubbing the book on their foreheads, or from whirling prayer-wheels bearing texts they cannot read," with Tibetan monks "'printing pages of charms and formulas on the surface of the water with woodcut blocks'" (pp. 15–16; Ong pp. 91–92); from Goody's own chapter, that "Writing is often regarded at first as an instrument of secret and magic power" and "Scraps of writing are used as magic amulets" (pp. 236, 201–3; Ong p. 91); from Tambiah, that "Literacy can be restricted to special groups such as the clergy" (pp. 113–14; Ong p. 91); from Wilks and Goody, the craft-literacy state of affairs "in West African kingdoms, such as Mali, from the Middle Ages into the twentieth century" (Ong p. 92); from Meggitt, the cargo cults in which "illiterates or semi-literates think that the commercial papers ... are magical instruments to make ships and cargo come in" (pp. 300–9; Ong p. 92). The volume's organizing concept, "restricted literacy," names societies that have writing but confine it to a class or a function.

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. "Restricted literacy" is the institutional concept the paper needs and is the volume's contribution: a literate order in which most members are not literate and are not, for that reason, outside the order — they hire, consult, or venerate writing rather than practise it. This supports the competence/institution register and complicates the analogy, because it shows that the mere presence of a new medium does not make its competence a membership requirement; that step is a separate institutional change (Havelock's "universalisation of letters"). Schofield's chapter on measuring literacy in pre-industrial England would be the empirical anchor for that step in the English case, but it was not read.

The scaffolding essay's use. The volume is the best single reason to doubt the essay's "clerical/scribal guild" monopoly as a universal pre-print condition: Tambiah's Thai village, Wilks's Sudan and Goody's Ghana all show literacy restricted to religious specialists *without* print ending the restriction, and the volume's own frame treats restriction as the normal condition of writing rather than a European clerical conspiracy that Gutenberg broke.
