## What it argues

Who loses the party line depends on who they are. The report widens Midkiff's air-carrier survey to four operational types — General Aviation, Regional/Commuter Airlines, Major Airlines and Military — distributing 4,375 surveys and receiving 738, of which 710 were usable (General Aviation 2,000 sent/242 returned; Commuter 1,075/114; airline and military rows in Table 2.5). The instrument's cover letter states the mixed-equipage problem the essay needs in one sentence: "The use of datalink by any type of aircraft will affect everyone. Even if an aircraft is not equipped with datalink, other aircraft may communicate by datalink rather than voice and therefore not contribute any 'Party Line' Information to the shared sector frequency" (Appendix A). The conclusions (Chapter 7): PLI is important overall, with the most critical elements being "immediate events required for tactical planning such as Aircraft on Landing Runway, Windshear, and Collision Avoidance"; availability and accuracy were rated only moderate, so "no PLI elements were identified as highly 'Reliable' or 'Common-Place'"; Uncontrolled Traffic and Error of Controller drew "low availability and accuracy ratings but high importance ratings" — information pilots want that the party line does not deliver well. Importance rises toward the airport and falls in cruise for airline pilots, while General Aviation pilots rate it high in every phase and single out icing and other weather their aircraft cannot handle; commuter pilots rate uncontrolled traffic highest, major-airline pilots ride reports and turbulence. Free responses on "the big picture" name traffic and weather. The design conclusions: "Voice communications will remain the best modality for certain time critical information elements, such as Windshear and Missed Approach"; unless the loss is compensated, "initial implementation of datalink communications should not be in the high-density and high-workload Terminal Area control sectors"; and graphical traffic displays such as TCAS "may enhance or replace PLI as a source of traffic information" because they require "less dissemination by the pilot than a verbal or textual message" (pp. 73–75).

## Relation to the argument

RQ9. This is the citation the planner could not pin down, and it exists in two verified forms (ASL-94-5 and ATCQ 4(1), 1996). Its contribution to the essay is the mixed-equipage point: closing the channel for some parties degrades the coordination for all, including those who never adopted the mediator. That is a structural claim about the third party's dependence on a channel two others choose, stated by the authors as a design fact rather than a finding, and it is what the lab's "suppress the direct channel" manipulation predicts. The report also gives the essay a typology of harm: the party who loses most is the least equipped and least professionally protected (General Aviation, who "gave consistently high importance ratings in all Phases of Flight"), which is a fairness reading the essay can make without the report making it. As with all party-line work the objective axis is empty; the datalink has no interest of its own, and the report's recommendation to keep time-critical items on voice is a recommendation to keep the direct channel open where the stakes are highest — itself a design principle the essay can quote.

## Caution

A survey of perceived importance, availability and accuracy in 1993–94, with a 17% return and self-selected respondents recruited through ALPA safety representatives and military squadrons; no behaviour was observed. The "mixed environment" claim is asserted in the instrument, not tested. I read the report, not the ATCQ 1996 article; quote pages from the report and re-check against the journal before citing the journal. The datalink of 1994 is not CPDLC as deployed; treat the case as historical evidence of a structural effect, not as a description of current operations.
