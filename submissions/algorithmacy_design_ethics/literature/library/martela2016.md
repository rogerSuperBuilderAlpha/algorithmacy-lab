# Martela, F., & Steger, M. F. (2016). The three meanings of meaning in life: Distinguishing coherence, purpose, and significance. *The Journal of Positive Psychology*, 11(5), 531–545.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/17439760.2015.1137623 · **Read depth:** full_text — the typeset version of record (T&F pagination 531–545), obtained as a PDF from a Caltech-hosted course copy (`its.caltech.edu/~squartz/Martela-Steger-JOPP.pdf`); every page number below is from that typeset copy · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, mid-ranked positive-psychology journal; the field's standard citation for the tripartite model (Consensus counts 1,257 citations; Google Scholar 2,445) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** n/a (individual psychology; no technology, no second party) · **Remedy locus:** none (a construct-definition paper; it proposes no remedy for anything) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The paper is a conceptual review. It argues that "meaning in life" as psychologists measure it conflates three facets, and that research should separate them: coherence, purpose, and significance. Its own summary definition (abstract, p. 531): "Coherence means a sense of comprehensibility and one's life making sense. Purpose means a sense of core goals, aims, and direction in life. Significance is about a sense of life's inherent value and having a life worth living."

The coherence section (pp. 533–534) is the one this arm needs, so here is the definition as the text gives it, verbatim:

> "Meaning in life is often associated with people making sense of the world, rendering it comprehensible and coherent. This is often referred to as the cognitive component of meaning in life, which is about 'making sense of one's experiences in life' (Reker & Wong, 1988, p. 220). Life is coherent when one is able to discern understandable patterns in it to make the wholeness comprehensible. In other words, meaning as coherence is seen to be about 'the feeling that one's experiences or life itself makes sense' (Heintzelman & King, 2014b, p. 154)." (p. 533)

Table 1 (p. 534) fixes the construct in four rows. Definition: "Sense of comprehensibility and one's life making sense." Opposite: "Uncertainty and incomprehensibility." Normativity: "Descriptive." Domains: "Understanding." The text then states what the understanding consists of:

> "Beginning at the discrete level of moment-to-moment experiences, coherence centers on the perception that stimuli are predictable and conform to recognizable patterns (Heine et al., 2006; Heintzelman & King, 2014b). From here, it would appear that ever more elaborate models of patterns and predictability can be constructed, eventually building to overarching meaning models that help people make sense of one's self, the world, and one's fit within the world (Steger, 2012a)." (p. 534)

Three further sentences settle what kind of understanding they mean. On lineage: the authors name Antonovsky's sense of coherence, "of which the first one, feeling confident that one's environment is structured and predictable, seems especially to be emphasizing this perspective on meaning as making sense of the world" (p. 533; note 2, p. 542, describes the other two SOC dimensions as "having the necessary resources to cope with the environment, and feeling that the challenges ahead are worthy of one's investment" — Antonovsky's manageability and meaningfulness, though the note does not use those labels). On function: "being able to make sense, find patterns, and establish predictability in the world confers a survival advantage to organisms" (p. 533), and Heintzelman and King's "feeling of meaning" "provides us with information about the presence of reliable patterns in the environment" (p. 533). On what the effort aims at: "Human effort to find coherence is thus an attempt to create accurate mental models of the world to facilitate predictability and consistency" (p. 536). They add that "meaning as coherence is thus an epistemic notion, it is about what we know" (p. 536), and, in the unification section, that coherence is "about forming a coherent mental representation about that world, having a cognitive map of the world that makes sense out of our experiencing" (p. 538).

The paper's composite definition of meaning in life (p. 538) reads: meaning "emerging from the web of connections, interpretations, aspirations, and evaluations that (1) make our experiences comprehensible, (2) direct our efforts toward desired futures, and (3) provide a sense that our lives matter and are worthwhile."

The closing section proposes three connections between facets, flagged as "mostly conceptual and speculative" (p. 538). The first is that coherence is a precondition of significance: "In situations where our life doesn't seem to be making any sense, where it is chaotic and messy, it might be very hard to uphold a sense of life's worthiness" (p. 538), so "Coherence thus might be a necessary condition for significance, but it is not a sufficient condition" (p. 539). The Finnish critic Seppo Heikinheimo is their counter-case: he wrote a lucid autobiography explaining his reasons and then killed himself, so "increased coherence is actually combined with decreased felt significance" (p. 539). The third connection runs the other way: "Finding a clear purpose can provide predictable structure to one's life" (p. 539).

## Relation to the argument

RQ4 asks whether coherence, in this source, requires mechanistic or textual understanding, or only that one's circumstances make sense. The text answers the second. The operative terms across the coherence section are "patterns," "predictable," "predictability," "consistency," "structured and predictable," and "reliable patterns"; the empirical anchor they cite is Heintzelman, Trent, and King's 2013 finding that "encountering coherent patterns in the environment increases people's self-reports of meaning in life" (p. 533), a result from exposure to ordered versus random stimuli, not from explaining anything. Nothing in the paper makes coherence depend on knowing *why* a regularity holds. The closest the text comes to a stronger requirement is the phrase "accurate mental models of the world," and the same sentence tells the reader what the accuracy is for: "to facilitate predictability and consistency" (p. 536). The construct is predictability-shaped.

Two consequences follow, and both cut against the critique as the arm had drafted it.

First, the sentence "generative AI makes coherence mathematically impossible, guaranteeing paralysis" is not in this paper and is not derivable from it. Martela and Steger say nothing about technology, work, or automation. What their model licenses is a hypothesis: a working life routed through a mediator whose behaviour one cannot predict puts the comprehensibility facet under strain, and, by their first speculative connection, may drag significance down with it. "Under strain" is the ceiling; they present coherence as graded ("ever more elaborate models of patterns and predictability," p. 534), not as a threshold that unpredictability switches off.

Second, and this is the finding that matters: Principle III's redefinition of coherence as operational predictability — bounded, deterministic outputs — is closer to Martela and Steger's construct than a mechanistic-interpretability reading would be. Their coherence is a sense that patterns hold and stimuli conform to them; a bounded-output design supplies exactly that, and supplies it whether or not the user can see inside the model. On this evidence the source is an ally of Principle III, not a target. The critique of the six accounts should therefore not list Martela and Steger among those who locate the remedy in the subject's understanding of mechanism; they locate nothing anywhere, and the reading of coherence their text supports is the one Principle III adopts.

What the source does not give the arm is any purchase on the relation between two coordinated people. Coherence here is a property of one person's representation of "the world" (p. 538). That a mediator's predictability from A's side says nothing about its predictability from B's, or about whether A can infer what it is doing with B, is this arm's extension, not theirs.

## Caution

This is theory, not evidence. The authors state that "none of the frequently used scales can be used to examine the three facets of meaning separately" (p. 533), so at the time of writing no measure of coherence in their sense existed; the connections between facets are "speculative" by their own label (p. 538). The Antonovsky link is presented as something "some also cite" (p. 533), not as the paper's own foundation; the primary anchor is Heintzelman and King, whose 2014 paper this library holds only at abstract depth (see heintzelman2014.md). The word "understanding" heads the Table 1 domain row, and a reader who treats "understanding" and "mechanistic understanding" as synonyms will misread the paper; the body text glosses the domain as pattern-detection and prediction throughout. The Heikinheimo example shows that predictability can coexist with despair, so an arm that wants coherence to carry the whole weight of well-being at work will find the source refusing that role. Two later papers by the same group (Martela et al. 2022, the 3DM scale; Martela & Steger 2023) revise the significance facet; they have not been read.
