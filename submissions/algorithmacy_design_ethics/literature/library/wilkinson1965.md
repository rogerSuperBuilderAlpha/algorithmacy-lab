## What it argues

Wilkinson coined "oracy" in 1965 for competence in speaking and listening, on the model of literacy and numeracy, as an educational objective for English teaching in Britain; the monograph *Spoken English*, with Davies and Atkinson, develops the case and proposes assessment methods (the ERIC abstract: "Modifications of current assumptions both about the nature of the spoken language and about its functions in relation to personality development are suggested in this book. The discussion covers an explanation of 'oracy' (the oral skills of speaking and listening) ..."). Neither Goody nor Ong uses the word; Ong's terms are "primary orality," "oral culture," "verbomotor," and Havelock's are "oral state of mind" and "craft literacy."

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. The sibling arm's audit finding is confirmed and should be carried forward: "oracy" is Wilkinson's word, an educational competence term, not a term of the orality–literacy scholarship. The paper may keep the sequence oracy → literacy → algorithmacy as its own coinage-by-extension, crediting Wilkinson, but must not write "oracy, as Goody and Ong describe it." Wilkinson's sense is also narrower than the paper's: a school competence in speaking and listening, not the competence to participate in an oral order's authority. That narrowing is worth stating, because it makes the paper's move explicit: each of the three terms names a competence an order demands, and the paper is the one generalizing them.
