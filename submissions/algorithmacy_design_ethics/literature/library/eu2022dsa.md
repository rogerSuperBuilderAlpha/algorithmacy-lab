# European Parliament & Council (2022). Regulation (EU) 2022/2065 on a Single Market for Digital Services and amending Directive 2000/31/EC (Digital Services Act), Articles 25, 27, 33 and 38. *Official Journal of the European Union*, L 277, 27.10.2022, pp. 1–102.

## What it argues

Article 25(1), "Online interface design and organisation": "Providers of online platforms shall not design, organise or operate their online interfaces in a way that deceives or manipulates the recipients of their service or in a way that otherwise materially distorts or impairs the ability of the recipients of their service to make free and informed decisions"; 25(3) names as candidate practices "giving more prominence to certain choices," repeated pop-ups and "making the procedure for terminating a service more difficult than subscribing to it." Article 27, "Recommender system transparency": providers "shall set out in their terms and conditions, in plain and intelligible language, the main parameters used in their recommender systems, as well as any options for the recipients of the service to modify or influence those main parameters" (27(1)); the parameters "shall explain why certain information is suggested" and include "the criteria which are most significant" and "the reasons for the relative importance of those parameters" (27(2)); and "Where several options are available pursuant to paragraph 1 for recommender systems that determine the relative order of information presented to recipients of the service, providers of online platforms shall also make available a functionality that allows the recipient of the service to select and to modify at any time their preferred option. That functionality shall be directly and easily accessible from the specific section of the online platform's online interface where the information is being prioritised" (27(3)). Section 5 applies to platforms and search engines with "a number of average monthly active recipients of the service in the Union equal to or higher than 45 million" and designated by the Commission (Article 33(1)). Within it, Article 38, "Recommender systems," in full: "In addition to the requirements set out in Article 27, providers of very large online platforms and of very large online search engines that use recommender systems shall provide at least one option for each of their recommender systems which is not based on profiling as defined in Article 4, point (4), of Regulation (EU) 2016/679."

## Relation to the argument

RQ9. Article 38 is the nearest thing in force to Principle II's "sever the feedback loop without losing basic utility" as a legal obligation rather than a design wish: the recipient keeps the service and the recommender, and drops the profile that closes the loop between her behaviour and what she is shown. Article 27(3) makes the severance a live interface duty — a switch "directly and easily accessible" at the point of prioritisation, changeable "at any time" — and Article 25(1) forbids designing that switch so as to "materially distort or impair" the choice, which is the legal answer to Zong and Matias's (2024) refusal "designed to placate." The paper can therefore say that the refusal affordance Principle II asks for already exists in law for one class of mediator. The limits are the finding. Article 38 covers recommender systems only, and only for the roughly 45-million-user platforms of Article 33; a gig-work allocation or a marketplace ranking outside that tier has Article 27's transparency and whatever options the provider chooses to offer. And "not based on profiling" is narrower than "pursues no objective of its own": a non-profiled feed is still ordered by the operator's rule — recency, popularity, paid placement — so Article 38 removes the personal loop and leaves the objective axis untouched. That is precisely the gap between severing the loop and factoring the coordination. Article 27(1)–(2), by contrast, is hermeneutic: it obliges the operator to explain its parameters and their "relative importance," which is the transparency ideal Cluster F's critics (Ananny & Crawford; Burrell) have already found wanting, and it runs to "recipients" only. The counterpart party's parallel duty — the business user's right to the "main parameters" of ranking — is in Regulation 2019/1150, which the sovereignty arm's card and Nolteernsting (2025) flag; the two parties to one coordination get their rights from two instruments, which is the triad's absence from the law stated as a fact about the statute book. CUTS AGAINST: nothing in Articles 27 or 38 preserves the *other* party's position when one recipient opts out of profiling, and nothing addresses a mediator that commits binding determinations (an allocation) rather than an ordering of information.

## Caution

Four articles of a 102-page regulation, read as text, with no delegated acts, guidelines or enforcement decisions consulted; whether Article 38's option is used, and how platforms have implemented 27(3), is an empirical question this card does not answer. The card confirms the sovereignty arm's earlier article numbers (17, 20, 21, 27, 38, 40) only for 25, 27, 33 and 38; the others were not re-read. "Profiling as defined in Article 4, point (4), of Regulation (EU) 2016/679" imports the GDPR definition, which turns on evaluating "personal aspects," so a recommender that ranks by content signals alone may be "not based on profiling" and still be highly personalised in effect.
