# Burrell, J. (2016). How the machine 'thinks': Understanding opacity in machine learning algorithms. *Big Data & Society*, 3(1), 1–12.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/2053951715622512 · **Read depth:** full_text (SAGE open-access HTML version of record, read in full including footnotes; section headings cited because the HTML carries no page numbers) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, one of the most-cited papers on algorithmic opacity · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (recipient of a classification vs the classifier; the author as lightweight auditor) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Opacity comes in three kinds that the emerging "politics of algorithms" literature conflates: (1) intentional corporate or state secrecy, remediable by disclosure or trusted audit (Pasquale's "remediable incomprehensibility"); (2) technical illiteracy, remediable by education; and (3) "an opacity that stems from the mismatch between mathematical optimization in high-dimensionality characteristic of machine learning and the demands of human-scale reasoning and styles of semantic interpretation." The third is the paper's subject, and it is not remediable by opening the box. Two worked examples carry the argument. A one-hidden-layer neural network for handwritten digits, visualised weight by weight, "doesn't ... break down handwritten digit recognition into subtasks that are readily intelligible to humans"; the same training run twice gives different weights because of random initialisation (Figure 4). A linear-kernel SVM spam filter trained on the 2002 SpamAssassin corpus yields per-word weights the author reads as a "lightweight audit": "Nigeria" scores −0.001861 (neutral), while the top spam words are "our" (0.50), "click" (0.46), "remov" (0.42), "guarante" (0.38), "visit" (0.37). A real 419 email is classified as spam not for "dearest," "beloved" or "trust worthy" but for "the mention of money, the words 'please,' and 'contact'"; remove the mention of money and the word "please" and it passes. Her verdict on the exercise: "probing further into the 'why' of a particular classification decision yielded suggestive evidence that seemed sufficient as an explanation, but this imposed a process of human interpretive reasoning on a mathematical process of statistical optimization. In other words, machine thinking was resolved to human interpretation. Yet ambiguities remained ... This raises doubts about whether an explanation produced in this way to satisfy the 'why' question was necessarily a particularly correct one" ("Reconsidering 'interpretability'"). On end-user-facing explanations, with Gmail's "why is this message in Spam?" box as the example: "explanations that bring forward a human-manageable list of key criteria (i.e. the 10 most heavily weighted/spammy words present in an email or a single sentence description) provide an understanding that is at best incomplete and at worst false reassurance." Domingos is quoted: "intuition fails at high-dimensions." The conclusion turns to remedies that abandon the "why" question altogether (Dwork et al.'s fairness through awareness; external audits per Sandvig et al.), education, open source, and — "perhaps surprisingly" — not using machine learning in some critical domains.

## Relation to the argument

RQ7, the transparency-critique question, and the answer must be stated carefully. Burrell **anticipates the mechanism** of Bo, Wan and Anderson's (2025) finding but **does not predict the result**. Her spam audit is, in form, exactly the intervention Bo et al. tested — per-token weights surfaced to a human reader — and she predicts what such surfacing does: the reader imposes semantic interpretation on a statistical artefact and comes away with an understanding that is "at best incomplete and at worst false reassurance." That is a prediction of *false confidence in the explanation*, and it is the same failure mode as token-level confidence highlighting misleading users. What she does not say is that the exposure will perform worse than no explanation; her claim is about the epistemic quality of the understanding, not about downstream task performance, and she offers no comparison condition. So the correct sentence for the paper is that Bo et al. supply an ordinal result (worse than nothing) that Burrell's 2016 analysis gives a mechanism for but did not itself forecast. The paper can also use her third-kind opacity as the reason Seamful Boundary Disclosure rejects raw exposure: at high dimensionality there is no human-scale reading of the weights to disclose, so disclosure of confidence is disclosure of a number the reader cannot ground.

Two further points for the construct. Burrell's remedies that "abandon answering the 'why' question" and evaluate discrimination by outcome are the ancestors of the paper's anticipatory affordances: competence lies in probing what the system does, not in reading how it thinks. And her observation that spam filtering is "socially consequential" because "a legitimate message categorized as spam ... has, in effect, been unwittingly censored" is a two-human-plus-mediator case in all but name — the sender and the recipient could reach each other, and the filter decides whether they do — which she never models as such. The "Parties modeled" field above is human–technology because that is how she frames it; the example itself is triadic.

## Caution

The audit is a Coursera exercise on a 2002 corpus with a linear kernel and no header features; the author calls it "hugely simplified" and "lightweight." There is no user study, no measurement of what any reader believed after seeing weights, and the "false reassurance" claim rests on the author's own reading of one email. The paper predates deep-learning-era XAI and LLMs; "confidence" in Bo et al.'s sense (model-reported token probability) is not the same object as an SVM weight, so the mechanism transfer is an analogy the paper must make explicitly rather than attribute to Burrell.
