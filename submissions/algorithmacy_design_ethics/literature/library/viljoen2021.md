## What it argues

"This Feature's core argument is that the data-collection practices of the most powerful technology companies are aimed primarily at deriving (and producing) population-level insights regarding how data subjects relate to others, not individual insights specific to the data subject. These insights can then be applied to all individuals (not just the data subject) who share these population features" (p. 578). Reform proposals that seek "to reassert individual control for data subjects" or "to maximize data-subject financial gain" "share a common conceptual flaw. Put simply, they miss the point of data production in a digital economy: to put people into population-based relations with one another" (p. 573). Both status quo and reform "attempt to reduce legal interests in information to individualist claims subject to individualist remedies, which are structurally incapable of representing the interests and effects of data production's population-level aims" (p. 578). Hence "Properly representing the population-level interests that result from data production in the digital economy will require far more collective modes of ordering this productive activity" (p. 579). Two problems are named: a "sociality problem" (how can law account for data's social effects) and a "legitimacy problem" ("how can the legal regimes governing data production distinguish legitimate from illegitimate data use without relying on individual notice and choice?" p. 582). Part IV's Watercorp/Waterorg example makes the point: an objector "Cate" has "a population-level interest in all household-water data" because "Waterorg doesn't need her data to get population-level insights about water consumption habits for households like hers — they may easily derive such insights from households that share relevant features" (p. 637). Individual rights "secure for households the rights to payment, exit, or recourse," but "still practically falls back on individual choice to determine the legitimacy of data collection" (p. 637). The wrong of Watercorp is that households "have no ability to meaningfully determine the quality of the data relations they are being placed in"; "They cannot exercise equal power either back onto Watercorp or over one another" (p. 640). The shift is "from providing opportunities for exit, payment, or recourse, to securing recognition and standing to shape the purposes and conditions of data production" (p. 640). Data becomes a "democratic medium" (DDM): "personal choices over data sharing should reflect the effects these choices have on others... because under current conditions of datafication, individuals already directly relay information relating to others, which is used to predict and influence the behavior of others" (p. 639). Datafication "may be wrong not only because it manipulates people, but also because the social effects it produces or materializes violate standards of equality" (p. 653).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8. Sorted: **collective standing — grounds the why**. Viljoen is the theoretical root of the whole trusts-and-cooperatives family in this cluster: she supplies the reason standing over data cannot be individual (any one person's data is substitutable at population level, so exit and consent are empty), and she designs no instrument of her own; Part IV.D (pp. 645–648) surveys existing collective proposals — Nahles's national data trust, the UK and Canadian public-data trusts, Barcelona's civic-data trust, and Sidewalk Labs's Toronto proposal (n. 183) — as instances consistent with the DDM standard, not as a design. Two things transfer to the construct with more force than the sovereignty card noticed. First, her "horizontal data relations" are the paper's second party stated in legal terms: what an intermediary learns from one party it applies to the other, and the two are bound through inferences neither made. The Cate example — her data is not needed because others like her supply it — is the mechanism by which a mediator "interprets both parties" without either's cooperation, and it explains why a single user's obfuscation (bruntonnissenbaum2015.md) or a single user's counter-agent cannot by itself alter a determination. Second, her legitimacy problem is the paper's contestation component at institutional scale: "recourse" is on her list of the *inadequate* remedies, which the paper must reckon with, since algorithmacy's "contest what it commits" is recourse by another name. Her answer is representation in the conditions of production, not a right to challenge a determination after the fact. CUTS AGAINST Principle I as an *individual* competence or a *client-side* agent: on her account both leave the population-level relation untouched and merely improve one party's position within it. The paper should concede this and argue that the counter-agent is the micro-instrument through which a collectively won standing is exercised in a particular coordination, not a substitute for the standing.

## Caution

Parts I–III (the doctrinal survey of privacy law and of propertarian and dignitarian reforms, pp. 586–634) were not read; quotations from them should be verified before use. The setting is U.S. data-governance law and the examples are municipal utilities and platforms, not matching or labour platforms. The Feature is theoretical and offers no institutional design of its own beyond the DDM standard; Part IV.D (pp. 641–653, read only by search) cites data trusts and a cooperative as existing proposals it finds congenial, without specifying any. Her "relations" are statistical (shared population features), not the interactional relation of two people coordinating, and the transfer of "horizontal" to the construct's second party is the paper's inference.
