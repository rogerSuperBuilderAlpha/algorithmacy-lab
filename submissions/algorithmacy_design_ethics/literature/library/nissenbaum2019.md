## What it argues

Part I restates contextual integrity as five theses. Privacy is "the appropriate flow of personal information," not stoppage: "CI cares only whether the flow is appropriate, not whether it takes place at all" (p. 225). Flows are appropriate when they conform to contextual informational norms (p. 228), and norms are fully specified by five parameters — subject, sender, recipient, information type, transmission principle (pp. 228–230), replacing the 2004 two-norm formulation. Contexts are constituted, not located: "Respective roles, activities, purposes, information types do not exist in a context; rather, these factors constitute a context" (p. 227), and she adds a self-correction: "In the past, I had not placed sufficient emphasis on functions, purposes (goals, ends), and values around which contexts are oriented. I would like to make up for this prior lapse by saying that these — let's call them teleological — factors define the very essence of a respective social context" (p. 226; the book cited at 129–58 and 132–37 for contexts, nn. 6, 7, 10). Thesis 4 is the answer to the conservatism objection, restated from the book: "Always resisting practices that violate entrenched norms would make CI an exceedingly conservative theory" (p. 231); "The approach outlined in Privacy in Context remains conservative, but instead of flatly rejecting novel flows, it presumptively favors entrenched norms, simultaneously offering a process for adjudicating between the two. To begin, evaluators follow the approach laid out in CI for pinpointing changes, followed by a three-layered comparative analysis ... Based on these findings, the evaluator recommends in favor of either the status quo or the challenger on grounds of moral quality" (p. 232). The three layers are interests of affected parties, ethical and political values, and "contextual functions, purposes, and values" (pp. 231–233). The third can override the subject: "Contextual purposes and values sometimes may disfavor the data subject's interests, even in the healthcare context ... CI may support overriding individual patient interests or preferences in favor of onward sharing" (p. 233). She then reports that "innumerable, unprecedented practices and associated data flows ... defy any easy comparison with preexisting practice" (pp. 233–234).

Part II introduces the data food chain: inference moves "up" from lower-order data (GPS traces, purchases) to higher-order attributes (home, pregnancy, sexual orientation, heart disease, a broker's "rural everlasting" segment). The higher-order attribute is what the paper calls an inferred or derived attribute, and she is explicit that its point is action on the person: natural and constructed categories alike "gather individuals into meaningful classes for purposeful, targeted, differential attention, be it decision, action, reward, or punishment" (p. 242), and machine learning "can support the clustering of populations based on learned vulnerabilities to targeted, disparate treatment in the interest of data processors (or their customers) without needing the middlemen, as it were, of any concept" (p. 242). The prevailing logic she attacks holds that a company which legitimately assembles data and infers from it "has not compromised privacy" (p. 243); against it, norms do not travel up the chain with inference — "The fact that heart disease is derivable from lifestyle choices and body weight does not necessarily transform the norms of flow governing heart disease" (p. 245) — and may travel down, so that lower-order data acquires the stringency of what it reveals (pp. 244–246; SSNs and metadata as precedents, p. 245). The normative evaluation "is independent of the logical (mathematical, statistical, algorithmic) principles enabling movement up the chain" (p. 246). Then the concession that matters for this library: "novel information types and newfangled actors are emerging at a dizzying pace, sometimes even transforming the contours of contexts themselves ... it is not always straightforward and may even be impossible to locate the relevant entrenched norms against which to compare the novel flows. Although this leaves CI without a quick assessment option, it fares no worse than other accounts of privacy" (p. 247). And: "I must acknowledge the harsh challenges they pose to the original heuristics of contextual integrity. Radical changes in the nature of data and surrounding technologies outpace the capacity to proceed according to the discrete steps of the CI heuristic — first to locate divergences in entrenched norms measured in terms of the five parameters and then to evaluate them. However, despite the need to relax and revise the mechanics of the heuristic, I remain convinced that a) mapping the data flows in terms of the five parameters and b) evaluating these flows ... remains an ideal worth striving for" (pp. 248–249). Part II.B, data primitives — mouse clicks, motion-sensor pulses, bare coordinates — "appear to have no meaning. Absent a semantics, they escape CI's privacy norms entirely" (abstract, p. 221). The conclusion: "There was a time when I believed that privacy could be well enough protected atomically ... as a general approach, it is overwhelmed by the scale of the shadow data universe. But passively waiting for clear breaches to surface is untenable" (p. 255); "surprising moves up the data food chain are coming at us too fast and furious to afford a decades-long learning curve for each of them" (p. 256); "I do not have a clear set of recommendations to offer" (p. 256). Four principles close: norms do not stay constant under transformation; transparency ("no unpleasant surprises"); accountability; and "the burden of proof should fall on data processors to ensure that the outcomes of the computations (e.g., machine learning) meet not only self-serving ends but also produce no harm ... including what I have called actuarial harms to the unlucky false positives and false negatives" (p. 256).

## Relation to the argument

This article is carded because it settles the adaptive-mediator half of the plan's second critique in the author's own words. The plan hypothesised that if Nissenbaum treats derived attributes as flows subject to the same norms, the pass must say whether the heuristic can keep up. She does treat them as flows — a derived attribute is information of a higher-order type flowing to a recipient, and "the theory of contextual integrity predicts that unease, if not protest, is likely to follow" such derivations (p. 242) — and she says herself that the heuristic cannot keep up: technologies "outpace the capacity to proceed according to the discrete steps of the CI heuristic" (p. 248), the chain moves "too fast and furious to afford a decades-long learning curve" (p. 256), and new actors are "sometimes even transforming the contours of contexts themselves" (p. 247). That last clause reopens, on her side, a narrow version of the collapse argument the 2011 essay closed: not that a platform *is* a context, but that platform-scale practice can reshape the context whose norms are supposed to judge it. The paper should cite this as her concession and not overstate it — her response is to keep the ideal and "relax and revise the mechanics" (p. 248), and to shift burden of proof onto processors (p. 256), which is a remedy at the level of law, not of the relation.

For the first critique, unit of analysis, this article is where contextual integrity comes closest to a determination and shows why it still cannot name one. Derived attributes exist for "differential attention, be it decision, action, reward, or punishment" (p. 242): the mediator's output on a person is in view. But it enters the evaluation as a *consequence to the data subject* — the "actuarial harms to the unlucky false positives and false negatives" of p. 256, weighed under layer (a), interests of affected parties. The party bound on the other side of the determination — the employer who receives the ranked candidate, the customer matched to the driver — has no parameter. The Barocas & Selbst citation (p. 241, n. 37) points at hiring, and even there the concern is disparate impact on the scored person, not the relation between two people the score coordinates. **Remedy locus: norms/institution** is confirmed; the closing principles are addressed to processors and regulators.

For the promotive question, p. 226 is the clearest statement in her corpus that the teleology belongs to the context: purposes and values "define the very essence of a respective social context," and p. 233 shows the consequence — the context's purposes "may disfavor the data subject's interests." The draft's sentence should be that Nissenbaum's promotive term is the flourishing of institutions (medicine, elections, democratic governance, p. 249), which can be served by overriding the person.

Two further things the paper can use. "The evaluator" and "a dispassionate analyst" (pp. 232–233) name the heuristic's agent — a third party outside the flow — which turns the plan's argument from silence into a textual finding. And Part II.B's data primitives are the tempo problem in a second form: the mediator's inputs are pulses with "no meaning" that "escape CI's privacy norms entirely" (p. 221), so the practice to be evaluated is not merely fast but, at the input layer, not describable in the vocabulary the heuristic needs. Benthall, Gürses & Nissenbaum (2017, *Contextual Integrity through the Lens of Computer Science*, p. 47, read for this purpose from the author's site) say the same from the engineering side: "This grand theory of privacy is not actionable for computer scientists," and CI's conservatism "is to warn against the agitation caused by disruptive technologies that change the environment too quickly for social evolution to adapt."

## Caution

A symposium essay, not a revision of the theory; the author says she offers no clear recommendations (p. 256), and the "origin" construct meant to repair the heuristic is only announced (p. 248, n. 57). The concessions quoted above are hers, but she frames them as challenges to "the mechanics of the heuristic," not to the benchmark, and she claims CI "fares no worse than other accounts" (p. 247) — the paper should not quote the concession without the reply. Every case is a profiling or inference case (Facebook, Target, data brokers, mHealth); no two-sided matching, ranking or pricing system appears, so the "no counterpart" reading is this card's inference from what the five parameters can and cannot name. Part II.B (data primitives, pp. 249–254) was read only in part; pp. 234–240 (the data-chain metaphor's setup and the Sherlock Holmes and computer-matching examples) were skimmed by page map and grep, not read whole.
