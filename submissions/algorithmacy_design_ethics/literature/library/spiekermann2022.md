## What it argues

Value-based Engineering (VBE), "first sketched out in primitive form in 2016" in the author's book and then standardised over five years as IEEE 7000-2021, "A Model Process for Addressing Ethical Concerns during System Design," is offered as what VSD lacks: "VSD has been unable to put forth a stringent process model that companies would be able to follow in a consistent and structured manner" (§I). The process has a value ontology from Scheler — core values, value qualities, value dispositions — elicited by asking three questions derived from utilitarianism, virtue ethics and duty ethics of "a broad group of critical stakeholder representatives" under Habermasian discourse conditions (§III.A), with a "Value Lead" who is "not 'the person in charge of ethics'" but bridges engineering, management and ethics. Value lists are kept away from elicitation because they "prime" it: a student group that had read a principle list found privacy paramount for an in-store tracking system for seniors, while a control group that visited the store found "helpfulness" (§II). Across three case studies "each stakeholder identified an average of 16–19 values," and for a Viennese telemedicine start-up, innovation-management students "identified a total of 54 unique value violations that could arise from the platform, as well as 63 positive values promoted" (§III.A). Prioritisation is the crux: core-value clusters "will not be treated as trade-offs"; instead they "are presented to executives and stakeholder representatives to rank them," against the standard's criteria (quoted from p. 41 of IEEE 7000): stakeholders agree the system "is good for society and avoids unnecessary harm"; "the organization does not use people merely as a means to some end"; leaders "can accept responsibility for the value priorities chosen according to their own personal maxims"; and the organisation respects its own principles, its business mission, the environment and "existing ethical guidelines" (§III.B). Each value tuple becomes an Ethical Value Requirement, logged in a Value Register with threats and controls so that "organizations are able to show their compliance with prioritized values" (§IV). The standard requires ecosystem partners "ready to give access to their systems, which is often not the case in today's highly distributed service architectures" (§V). UNICEF's Yoma CTO called it an "80–85% change in terms of how you design a system."

## Relation to the argument

IEEE 7000 is VSD institutionalised, and its answer to the adjudication question is the frankest in the cluster: the ranking of values is done by "executives and stakeholder representatives," so the operator ranks, under a Kantian side-constraint — no using people "merely as a means" — that is the nearest thing here to a rule that could catch cooptation. Whether that constraint bites depends on who sits at the table, and the process gives the organisation the chair. That is a complement to the affordances, not a rival: the Value Register is the back office of the seam, the traceable record of why the objective is what it is, which is what Seamful Boundary Disclosure would need to draw on to disclose the objective rather than the mechanism. The "system-of-systems" requirement is the one place a design standard admits the platform structure — the intermediary's objective is set across partner systems, and the standard's remedy is to demand access or forgo the partner. The priming result bears on Le Dantec's critique and on the construct's interpretive component: what people can say they value depends on what they were told to look for. Nothing here cuts against the affordances; the paper says nothing about users contesting anything, since its stakeholders are consulted, not opposed.

## Caution

The author co-initiated and vice-chaired the standard and reports her own case studies (one working paper, one unpublished telemedicine study, one debriefing interview); the "10 times more value harm detection" figure is from that working paper. This is a preprint of a magazine article, not a peer-reviewed study, and I did not open IEEE 7000-2021 or the 2016 book, so the standard's text is known here only through its co-author's quotations of it. The stakeholder representatives' selection and the executives' ranking are asserted to meet discourse-ethics conditions; nothing shows that they do.
