# Midkiff, A. H., & Hansman, R. J. (1993). Identification of important "party line" information elements and implications for situational awareness in the datalink environment. *Air Traffic Control Quarterly*, 1(1), 5–30.

**Identifier:** doi:10.2514/atcq.1.1.5 (Crossref-verified; AIAA ARC hosts the journal) · same study as SAE Technical Paper 922023, doi:10.4271/922023 (Aerospace Technology Conference, Long Beach, Oct 1992), MIT ASL report ASL-92-2 (May 1992), and Midkiff's MIT S.M. thesis "Identification and Experimental Validation of the Impact of 'Party Line' Information on Situational Awareness in Air Carrier Operations" (June 1992, MIT DSpace) · **Read depth:** partial — the ATCQ article itself was not opened (AIAA ARC returned 403); I read the thesis in full (105 pp., the report the article condenses) and the SAE abstract; all quotes below are from the thesis, with its section numbers · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed journal article (ATCQ, ATCA's journal, later AIAA), resting on a NASA/FAA-funded MIT report (grant NAG 2-716); survey plus full-mission simulation · **Evidence basis:** direct_read of the underlying thesis; secondary for the journal text · **Parties modeled:** two humans + mediator — pilot, controller, and the other pilots on the frequency, with the communication channel (open voice vs discretely addressed datalink) as the manipulated variable · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Closing the shared channel costs the third party its awareness. Midkiff defines the object: "Crewmembers obtain PLI through frequency monitoring by overhearing communications between ATC and other aircraft," and the threat: "the discrete nature of datalink addressing (where an ATC message is directed exclusively to a specific aircraft) may result in a loss of indirect or 'party line' information" (thesis §1). An analysis of Chicago terminal-area communications found potential PLI in about 90% of arrival transmissions, though only a small fraction is relevant to any one aircraft (§2.1). PLI carries turbulence and ride reports, sector congestion and controller workload, the assurance that one's radio still works, and urgency read off voice inflection; controllers deliberately solicit it (braking action, airspeed loss on final). It is unreliable by construction — available only when the crew is tuned and has spare capacity — and "most pilots appear to use PLI only as a supplemental information source and rarely make important decisions predicated on PLI alone" (§2.1). The tradeoff is stated without hedging: "In the absence of any form of compensation, it would appear that the intrinsic benefits of datalink — accuracy, lack of congestion, automatic FMC programming, etc. — are only possible at the expense of a reduction in the available PLI" (§2.3). Two earlier compensations, CDTI traffic displays and "proximity addressing" that copies datalink messages to nearby aircraft, both "increased the crew workload and heads down time as flight crew members were required to screen data to determine relevancy" (§2.3). The survey of active air-carrier crews rated importance, availability and accuracy of PLI elements by phase of flight; the most important elements were scripted into a full-mission simulation at NASA Ames, where "some party line elements perceived as important were effectively utilized... However, other party line elements stimulated little or no increase in situational awareness," use depending on "workload, time availability, and the tactical/strategic nature of the situations," and importance peaking "near or on the airport" (abstract).

## Relation to the argument

RQ9: yes, this is a real, citable, triadic human-factors case, and it is the essay's structural manipulation performed on a national infrastructure. Before datalink the pilot–controller exchange is dyadic in content but broadcast in form, and every other pilot on the frequency is a party to it for free; after datalink the same exchange is point-to-point, the overhearing party is cut out, and whatever the third party needs from the coordination must now be supplied by the mediator on purpose (the "compensation" the whole program is about). That is "suppress the direct channel" as a design decision with a measured human cost. The lab's Φ story maps cleanly: the open frequency is the case where the other pilots could reach the information without the mediator; the discretely addressed channel is the case where they cannot. Two limits matter for the essay. First, the mediator here (Mode S datalink, ACARS) has no objective of its own; the objective axis is empty, and the case establishes only the structural half. Second, the harmed party is the *overhearer*, not one of the two who are talking; the essay's construct puts the third party inside the coordination, whereas PLI research puts it alongside. Midkiff's own framing — a third aircraft loses "the big picture" when two others go private — is nonetheless the closest thing in the human-factors record to an empirical instance of the triad.

## Caution

I did not read the journal article; the thesis is the fuller document and the SAE and ATCQ versions condense it, but any page-numbered quotation must be checked against ATCQ 1(1) before it enters a draft. The survey population is U.S. air-carrier crews in 1991–92 with American Airlines' support; the simulation ran seven crews (see hansman1995) and was the voice-communication control phase of a separate NASA datalink experiment, not a designed PLI-versus-datalink comparison. Nothing here measures what happens *after* datalink; the loss is inferred from how PLI is used under voice. TCAS, ADS-B and CPDLC have since changed the compensation picture, so the case is historical.
