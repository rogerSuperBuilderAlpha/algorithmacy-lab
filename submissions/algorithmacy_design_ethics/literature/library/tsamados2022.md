## What it argues

The 2016 six-concern map "remains a fruitful framework" and is repopulated. Under inscrutability (§4) the paper summarises the transparency critique it inherits: transparency is "not an 'ethical principle in itself but a pro-ethical condition'" (Turilli & Floridi 2009); "complete transparency can itself cause distinct ethical problems (Ananny and Crawford 2018): transparency can provide users with some critical information about the features and limitations of an algorithm, but it can also overwhelm users with information and thus, render the algorithm more opaque (Kizilcec 2016; Ananny and Crawford 2018)"; malleability lets providers "blur the history of [an algorithm's] evolution," and constant revision "requires those affected by the algorithm to monitor it constantly and update their understanding accordingly — an impossible task for most (Ananny and Crawford 2018)." It adds the distributive point that "the ability to game algorithms is only within reach for some groups of the population — those with higher digital literacy for example — thus, creating another form of social inequality (Martin 2019; Bambauer and Zarsky 2018)," and reports Diakopoulos and Koliska's four transparency layers (data, model, inference, interface) with "uncertainty (e.g. error margins)" among the factors. Under autonomy (§7) limits come from three sources: pervasive proactive algorithms, "users' limited understanding of algorithms," and "lack of second-order power (or appeals) over algorithmic outcomes (Rubel et al. 2019)." It reports Rahwan's "Society-in-the-Loop" framework and its "algorithmic social contract," "a pact between various human stakeholders, mediated by machines," and Burke's "multi-stakeholder and multi-sided approach to defining fairness" for recommender systems. Under traceability (§8) it introduces "agency laundering," Rubel et al.'s term for "distancing oneself from morally suspect actions ... by blaming the algorithm," with Facebook's "Jew hater" ad category as the case ("we never intended or anticipated this functionality being used this way"), "the computer said so" denial (Karppi 2018), "ethics bluewashing" and "ethics dumping" (Floridi 2019b), and closes with Floridi's (2016) faultless-responsibility mechanism as the answer to "who is responsible ... for DMAs."

## Relation to the argument

RQ7, and this is the card that ties the cluster together. It supplies, in the Floridi school's own words, the three verbs of the companion draft: *distributed* (DMAs across hybrid networks), *deflected* (agency laundering, "the computer said so"), and *denied* (Facebook's disavowal), and it names Floridi (2016) as the intended remedy — so the paper can cite the school's self-understanding rather than construct the linkage. On the transparency critique it is the source that pairs Ananny and Crawford with Kizilcec (2016) for the overload mechanism, which is the right bridge to Bo et al. (2025): the theoretical claim (more disclosure can mean more opacity) and an earlier empirical non-monotonicity (Kizilcec) both predate the token-confidence result, without either predicting that specific display. Two findings CUT AGAINST or complicate the construct. First, the field frames the tactical component of algorithmacy as "gaming," a harm to be prevented and, when unequally distributed, an inequality — so the paper must argue explicitly why one party's adaptation to an intermediary that binds both is competence rather than manipulation, and must expect the objection that algorithmacy widens the digital-literacy gap. Second, the school already contains triadic and multi-party frames — Rahwan's "pact between various human stakeholders, mediated by machines," Burke's multisided fairness — so the claim that the ethics literature assumes a dyad has to be narrowed to the *principles* frameworks (AI4People, HLEG, OECD) and to the explanation literature, not asserted of the field as a whole. The "lack of second-order power (or appeals)" limit on autonomy is the ethics-side name for the contestation component and should be cited.

## Follow-up (added 2026-09-18)

The Rahwan lead this card flagged has been followed: see rahwan2018.md. The "pact between various human stakeholders, mediated by machines" is quoted here accurately from Rahwan's abstract, and the full text confirms that society-in-the-loop is a governance layer at legislative time scale ("not feedback on frequent micro-level decisions"), so the multi-party frame the school already contains does not reach the moment of a binding determination. That narrows the concession this card asked the paper to make: the ethics literature contains the *polity*, not the *coordination*. The "lack of second-order power (or appeals)" limit on autonomy remains the ethics-side name for the contestation component, and the "gaming" framing remains the objection Principle I must answer — a client-side counter-agent is, in this vocabulary, gaming automated; the paper's reply should draw on Brunton and Nissenbaum's chapter 4 tests (bruntonnissenbaum2015.md), which are the only worked ethics of user-side counter-moves in the library.
