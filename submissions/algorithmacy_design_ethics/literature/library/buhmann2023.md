# Buhmann, A., & Fieseler, C. (2023). Deep learning meets deep democracy: Deliberative governance and responsible innovation in artificial intelligence. *Business Ethics Quarterly*, 33(1), 146–179.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1017/beq.2021.42 · **Read depth:** full_text (open access, CC BY 4.0, downloaded on the author's request 2026-10-25; quotations checked against the PDF text by printed page) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed journal (BEQ); conceptual · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** the AI industry, experts, citizens and publics, with AI mini-publics mediating between them · **Remedy locus:** institution (deliberative venues: expert discourse, the public sphere, mini-publics); the citizen takes part as a member of a public, not as a party to a mediated transaction · **Relation last checked:** 2026-10-25

## What it argues

Epistemic concerns about AI split into strategic, expert and true opacity (pp. 150–152). Strategic opacity is deliberate, with motives including "avoiding accountability and evading regulations" (p. 151); expert opacity sits at "the intersection of system complexity and 'technical literacy'" and "can also arise inadvertently through attempts at disclosure and transparency that overwhelm citizens" (p. 151). From political CSR, the authors argue that AI firms are obliged to join deliberation, because "the impossibility of attaining complete 'AI transparency' can be used as an excuse for organizations not to fulfill ethical duties" (p. 157). They model "distributed deliberation" across three venues (Figure 1, p. 162). They grant that corporate engagement may be "merely a means of deflection" (p. 167) and end by holding that "a politically disengaged AI industry may pose a much greater threat" (p. 171).

## Relation to the argument

A transparency-ideal critic in BEQ, which places it in this cluster. The diagnosis matches the paper's: disclosure can itself produce opacity, and "technical literacy" is where opacity bites. The remedy moves up a level, to publics and venues, and its model "means that citizens do not need to have the expert capacity to undertake independent examination of algorithmic systems" (p. 171). That spares the citizen the reading without giving the coordinated person any control inside the mediation. It complements the counter-delegation principle rather than competing with it.

## Caution

Its subject is AI governance at the level of society, not two people coordinated through an intermediary; the relation point carries over by analogy. Its answer to the deflection worry is optimistic and untested (p. 167).
