# Wang, Y., & Kosinski, M. (2018). Deep neural networks are more accurate than humans at detecting sexual orientation from facial images. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 114(2), 246–257.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1037/pspa0000098 · **Read depth:** full_text of the version-of-record PDF (APA typeset; running header "2018, Vol. 114, No. 2, 246–257"; section "Innovations in Social Psychology"), re-obtained today from the OSF deposit at osf.io/hv28a/download (12 pages, 246–257) rather than from the planner's copy; quotations located by printed page · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, APA flagship journal; five empirical studies · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** none — a classifier study; people appear as classifier targets and, in Study 4, as human judges used as a comparison classifier; no coordination modeled · **Affiliation (author note, p. 246):** "Yilun Wang and Michal Kosinski, Graduate School of Business, Stanford University" · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The abstract (p. 246): "We used deep neural networks to extract features from 35,326 facial images. These features were entered into a logistic regression aimed at classifying sexual orientation. Given a single facial image, a classifier could correctly distinguish between gay and heterosexual men in 81% of cases, and in 71% of cases for women. Human judges achieved much lower accuracy: 61% for men and 54% for women. The accuracy of the algorithm increased to 91% and 83%, respectively, given five facial images per person... Consistent with the prenatal hormone theory of sexual orientation, gay men and women tended to have gender-atypical facial morphology, expression, and grooming styles... given that companies and governments are increasingly using computer vision algorithms to detect people's intimate traits, our findings expose a threat to the privacy and safety of gay men and women." The images came from "public profiles posted on a U.S. dating website" (p. 248), screened "to verify that the faces were adult, Caucasian, fully visible" (p. 248), balanced 50/50 gay and heterosexual within each gender (p. 248); the human judges were Amazon Mechanical Turk workers (p. 253).

The authors write their own warnings. "We would like to warn our readers against misinterpreting or overinterpreting this study's findings" (p. 254). "This study has a number of limitations. We used nonstandardized images characterized by varying quality, head orientation, or facial expression. This provides for higher ecological validity and a larger, more representative sample, but also introduces confounders... Additionally, as the images were obtained from a dating website, they might have been especially revealing of sexual orientation" (p. 255); "despite our attempts to obtain a more diverse sample, we were limited to studying White participants from the U.S." (p. 255). On publishing: "Some people may wonder if such findings should be made public lest they inspire the very application that we are warning against. We share this concern. However, as governments and companies seem to be already deploying face-based classifiers aimed at detecting intimate traits... there is an urgent need for making policymakers, the general public, and gay communities aware of the risks that they might be facing already" (p. 255); "We did not create a privacy-invading tool, but rather showed that basic and widely used methods pose serious privacy threats. We hope that our findings will inform the public and policymakers" (p. 255). Footnote 10: "The results reported in this article were shared, in advance, with several leading international LGBTQ organizations" (p. 255).

The passage the draft needs, verified verbatim. "Policymakers and technology companies seem to believe that legislation and new technologies offering individuals more control over their digital footprints can reverse this trend. However, the digital environment is very difficult to police... Thus, while we certainly need to urgently work toward policies and technologies aimed at protecting privacy, in the long term, the further erosion of privacy" — p. 255 ends here — "seems inevitable. Consequently, the safety of gay and other minorities who may be ostracized in some cultures will hinge on the tolerance of societies and governments. The postprivacy world will be a much safer and hospitable place if inhabited by well-educated, tolerant people who are dedicated to equal rights" (pp. 255–256; the page break falls between "privacy" and "seems," so the "inevitable" sentence itself straddles the two pages). ## Relation to the argument

Repairs the draft sentences in "Moral Hazards," bullet 4: "Stanford's facial recognition research... Researchers frequently frame the resulting erosion of privacy and individual autonomy as 'inevitable,' weaponizing deterministic rhetoric to justify technologies that expose marginalized populations to automated surveillance and exclusion." "Stanford" is right (Graduate School of Business) and "inevitable" is in print at pp. 255–256; both should become a named citation. The frame is deterministic in exactly the draft's sense: the "postprivacy world" is taken as given and the remedy is displaced onto "the tolerance of societies and governments."

What the text does not support is "weaponizing... to justify technologies." The authors disclaim building a tool, say they "share this concern," frame the paper as a warning, shared results with LGBTQ organizations, and call the technology a threat. A reader who checks the page will find a forecast, not a justification, and the draft's verb will look like an overreading. The stronger and defensible critique is the one this paper's argument already owns: the authors' remedy locus is the wrong one. The affected party is given a warning to read ("inform the public and policymakers," p. 255), legislation and data control are declared unable to "reverse this trend," and standing over the classification — any instrument by which the person classified could contest or refuse it — is never proposed. Proposed wording: "A Stanford study that trained a classifier to infer sexual orientation from dating-site photographs ends not with a remedy but with a forecast: 'in the long term, the further erosion of privacy seems inevitable,' so that the safety of gay people 'will hinge on the tolerance of societies and governments' (Wang & Kosinski 2018, pp. 255–256). The affected party is handed a warning to read; standing over the classification is never proposed." Drop "weaponizing" and "to justify."

The draft's neighbouring clause, "expose marginalized populations to automated surveillance and exclusion," is Gender Shades' finding rather than this study's (see buolamwini2018.md), and the bullet should cite both. If the draft wants "research" in the plural for Stanford, the sequel is Kosinski, M. (2021), "Facial recognition technology can expose political orientation from naturalistic facial images," *Scientific Reports* 11, article 100, doi:10.1038/s41598-020-79310-1 (Crossref record verified: published 11 January 2021; not read; not carded). "Facial recognition" is loose for a classifier built on facial-image features; "facial-image classification" is exact.

## Caution

Genre: a JPSP empirical paper with five studies; the human-judge comparison uses crowd workers (p. 253); no deployed system is audited, and the privacy threat is inferred from the existence of the method plus press reports of state and corporate deployment (p. 255). Sample: one U.S. dating site, White adults only, 50/50 base rate, self-posted photographs.

Three critiques to carry. (1) Agüera y Arcas, B., Todorov, A., & Mitchell, M. (2018). "Do algorithms reveal sexual orientation or just expose our stereotypes?" *Medium*, 11 January 2018 — non-peer-reviewed; read from a Wayback Machine capture of 9 December 2018, since medium.com returns a Cloudflare block to non-browser fetches. Their argument: the composite-face differences "relate to grooming, presentation, and lifestyle — that is, differences in culture, not in facial structure"; a survey of 8,000 Mechanical Turk respondents on eyeshadow, glasses and facial hair reproduces the composites' visible differences; camera angle confounds jaw and nose measurements; "The issue... is not whether the images come from a dating website or Facebook, but whether they are self-posted or taken under standardized conditions"; and simple survey-based rules reach comparable accuracy (an eyeshadow rule gives 63% for pairs of women). (2) Gelman, A., Mattson, G., & Simpson, D. (2018). "Gaydar and the fallacy of decontextualized measurement." *Sociological Science*, 5, 270–280, doi:10.15195/v5.a12 (open access, CC BY; read): the dating-site sample "may well be telling us more about the samples than about the general population they are presumed to represent" (p. 274); the study used "participants on a dating site who both self-identified as gay and wanted other people to know this" (p. 276); the 50/50 base rate and forced binary exemplify "the fallacy of decontextualized measurement" (p. 275); they add that they "are not claiming that experiments such as those of... Wang and Kosinski (2018) are useless" (p. 277). (3) Leuner, J. (2019). "A replication study: Machine learning models are capable of predicting sexual orientation from facial images." arXiv:1902.10739 (record and abstract only, unread): on 20,910 new dating-site photographs the prediction replicates at lower accuracy (DNN 68% men, 77% women) and the abstract reports the models "invariant to intentional changes to a subject's makeup, eyewear, facial hair and head pose," which cuts partly against (1). The draft should not lean on the accuracy figures; it needs only the authors' framing of privacy, which the critiques do not touch.
