## What it argues

From the abstract, verbatim: "we are addicted to our digital devices—or, more precisely, the digital experiences they enable. This addiction is both akratic (we are aware of the negative consequences) and engineered (designed on purpose). Marketers are complicit in this engineering: through digital networks and big data they ubiquitously monitor and experiment on consumers. This knowledge is used to create ever-more addictive digital experiences enabled by devices, their platforms, and their content. In this article, the authors explore marketing's role in the rise of the phenomenon: from product design, through advertising and big data, to the dynamics of ubiquitous distribution and 'free' pricing. The authors then turn to policy solutions and discuss how informing, guiding, and restricting can help ameliorate the problem and promote the public good. The article concludes by outlining multiple areas for marketing and public policy research." Keywords: apps, attention, devices, digital addiction, internet addiction. The lead author's affiliation line gives a Bentley address (pberthon@bentley.edu).

The reference list (read in full) fixes the paper's frame. It is an addiction-science and public-health-policy apparatus applied to digital products: behavioural-addiction psychiatry (Grant et al. 2010; Holden 2001; ICD-11 gaming disorder), the neuroscience of reward (Hyman, Malenka & Nestler 2006; Robinson & Berridge 2000), rational-addiction economics (Becker & Murphy 1988), the tobacco, alcohol, gambling and food-marketing policy literatures (Brandt 2007; Andrews & Netemeyer 1996; Seiders & Petty 2004, 2007; Korn et al. 2003), warning-label research (Argo & Main 2004; Kees et al. 2010), and the popular attention-economy books (Alter 2017; Eyal 2014; Wu 2017; Carr 2011; Turkle 2017; Harris 2017). Two references matter for this arm: Moller, Ryan and Deci (2006), "Self-Determination Theory and Public Policy: Improving the Quality of Consumer Decisions Without Using Coercion," in the same journal, which is the SDT bridge peters2018.md supplies from the design side; and Bahl et al. (2013) and Kim, Milne and Bahl (2018) on mindfulness, which connect this paper to the mindfulness typology in berthon2019.md. There is also Ali et al. (2015), "The Emerging Requirement for Digital Addiction Labels," which suggests the policy remedies include labelling.

## Relation to the argument

This card exists for the Bucket 4 rider. The draft sentence (DRAFT.md, Introduction): "Yet as Pierre Berthon establishes, harm mitigation represents an ethically necessary but insufficient floor." And, in the Berthon section: Berthon "identifies how contemporary digital architectures operate as 'meaning-hostile' engines, fragmenting human attention, undermining authentic self-direction, and replacing genuine significance with manipulative vanity metrics and algorithmic addictions."

(a) *The "necessary but insufficient floor."* Nothing at abstract depth supports it. The abstract frames the paper as diagnosis (marketing's role in engineered addiction) followed by three policy levers, "informing, guiding, and restricting," and the reference list is built around harm-reduction precedents from tobacco, gambling and food. That is a paper *about* harm mitigation as the remedy, not one that ranks harm mitigation as a floor beneath a more demanding design ethics. I could not read the body, so I cannot certify the phrase is absent; I can say that the paper's stated programme runs the other way, and that no title in its 140-odd references gestures at a flourishing-versus-harm hierarchy. Verdict: this paper is not the source of the floor claim on any evidence available to me, and the claim now has no candidate source in the library. It should be dropped as an attribution. If the author wants the idea, it is the paper's own and can be stated in its own voice; peters2018.md offers a citable neighbour ("any technology wishing to claim it improves wellbeing, or even that it merely doesn't harm it, will need to measure at the life level," p. 10), which is a measurement claim, not the floor claim, and should not be stretched into it.

(b) *"Meaning-hostile."* Not in the abstract or keywords, and the paper's vocabulary is addiction, attention, akrasia and engineering, not meaning. The phrase remains the draft's own; see peters2018.md and mekler2019.md for the replacement vocabulary.

(c) *"Algorithmic addictions," "vanity metrics," attention.* The abstract does carry "engineered (designed on purpose)" addiction, monitoring and experimentation on consumers through "digital networks and big data," and "attention" is a keyword; so this paper, not berthon2019.md, is the right Berthon citation for the draft's claim that digital architectures are engineered to capture attention and produce addiction. "Vanity metrics" is not in the abstract and cannot be attributed at this depth. Proposed rewording of the Berthon steel-man bullet:

> Pierre Berthon, Leyland Pitt and Colin Campbell (2019) argue that digital addiction is "both akratic (we are aware of the negative consequences) and engineered (designed on purpose)," and that marketers "through digital networks and big data ... ubiquitously monitor and experiment on consumers" to make experiences "ever-more addictive" (p. 451). Their remedies are public-policy levers—"informing, guiding, and restricting"—and, in Berthon and Pitt's companion column (2019), the user's own trained attention.

The draft's opening attribution ("As Pierre Berthon establishes, harm mitigation represents an ethically necessary but insufficient floor") should be cut or re-voiced, e.g.: "Harm mitigation is a necessary floor, not a design ethics; the more demanding question is ..." with no citation, since the claim is the paper's own.
