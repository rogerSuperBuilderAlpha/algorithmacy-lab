## What it argues

Engineering ethics is "externalist": Challenger and the Pinto treat technology "in a merely instrumental way," while what matters as much is technological mediation, "the impacts of such technologies on our moral decisions and actions" (pp. 91–92). Mediation runs in two directions, "one pragmatic, concerning action, and the other hermeneutic, concerning interpretation" (p. 94). The pragmatic side is Latour's and Akrich's: "With Madeleine Akrich, he coined the term 'script' to indicate that artifacts can prescribe specific actions, just like the script of a film or play" (p. 94), with the speed bump ("slow down before reaching me") and the coin-locked Dutch shopping cart as cases. The hermeneutic side is Ihde's, illustrated by obstetric ultrasound, which "isolates the fetus from the female body," places it "in a context of medical norms," and constitutes parents "as decision-makers regarding the life of their unborn child" (p. 94). Artifacts therefore have intentionality in the literal sense of *intendere*, "to direct," and their mediations "cannot be entirely reduced to the intentions of their designers and users" (p. 95); the 15 km/h early car used for "thin air" therapy and the cell phone's unintended effect on youth appointments are the examples. The conclusion is that "there is no such thing as 'technological intentionality'; intentionality is always a hybrid affair ... 'composite intentions' with intentionality distributed over the human and the nonhuman elements" (p. 96). Section 2.2 meets the charge that mediated action cannot be moral, which Verbeek states as two objections to speed limiters: "that human freedom is threatened and that democracy is exchanged for technocracy," and "the charge of immorality or, at best, amorality," including "a form of moral laziness" (p. 97). His reply redefines freedom as "an agent's ability to relate to what determines him or her" (p. 97). Section 3 draws the design lesson: mediations "are no intrinsic qualities of technologies, but are brought about in complex interactions between designers, users, and the technologies" (p. 99), so three agencies are at work (user, designer, artifact; Fig. 1, p. 100), and the designer's tool is "moral imagination" applied through "a mediation analysis (cf. Verbeek, 2006)" (p. 100). Building mediations in deliberately is what "Hans Achterhuis has called ... the 'moralization of technology' (Achterhuis, 1995)" (p. 101); because such choices "always have public consequences," they "should be subject to public decision-making" and the products become *res publica* in Latour's sense (p. 102).

## Relation to the argument

RQ1: the two-direction account (action via script, perception via hermeneutics) is what the construct's "interprets both parties" and "commits determinations that bind both" would translate into, but Verbeek's hermeneutic direction always runs from technology to world and back to one human; the fetus and the parents are not two intentional parties, since only the parents read. Page 99 cuts against a Φ reading taken as a property of the artifact: mediation is "no intrinsic quality," it emerges between designer, user and thing. Φ computed on a model of the coordination form rather than on the device is compatible with that, and the paper should say so. RQ2: this is the source that resolves the plan's [check] on Achterhuis. The "speed-bump charge" is not Achterhuis's; the speed bump is Latour's example and Achterhuis's contribution is the proposal to moralize apparatus, against which the technocracy and moral-laziness objections are raised (pp. 97, 101). Verbeek's answer, that designers already materialize morality so the choice is only between reflective and unreflective mediation, licenses the seam as a designed mediation. His demand that such materializations be public (p. 102) is a normative answer to asymmetric seamfulness before the question is asked: a seam disclosed to one party and withheld from the other would fail the res publica test.

## Caution

A short chapter that condenses two longer works and cites itself for the method; the "mediation analysis" is named, not specified. Achterhuis 1995 is Dutch (*Socialisme en Democratie* 52(1): 3–12, per the reference list, p. 102) and I read it only through Verbeek's account here. The three-agency figure treats "the user" as one class; there is no second human whose interests differ from the first user's, so the designer–user–artifact triangle is not the lab's triad. The examples are short and illustrative; nothing is measured.
