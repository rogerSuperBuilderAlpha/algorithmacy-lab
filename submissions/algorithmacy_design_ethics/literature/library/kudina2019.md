# Kudina, O., & Verbeek, P.-P. (2019). Ethics from within: Google Glass, the Collingridge dilemma, and the mediated value of privacy. *Science, Technology, & Human Values*, 44(2): 291–314.

## What it argues

Collingridge's dilemma has an ethical variant: "technologies often change the value frameworks we use to evaluate them," so ethics is "either 'too early' ... or 'too late'" (Introduction). Two responses fall short. Swierstra's technomoral scenarios anticipate "soft impacts" but "can only offer speculations about the future" (Introduction); van de Poel's sociotechnical experiments regulate innovation but discard anticipation and so "may lock itself up at the other pole of the Collingridge dilemma" (Dealing with Technological Value Dynamism). Mediation theory is the third way because "rather than speculating about the future or conducting responsible social experiments with technology, it studies the dynamics of technomoral change itself" (Technological Mediation). Technologies in use are "'mediators' of the relation between users and their environment," moral agency "is a hybrid affair, involving both humans and technologies (Verbeek 2014)," and the approach works "at the phenomenological microlevel that, ultimately, forms the basis of macrolevel technomoral developments."

The case is the Explorer version of Google Glass. The authors take Mashable's 2013 "Glasshole" etiquette video (1,434,785 views), collect its 2,064 comments, narrow them to 96 substantive ones, and code them thematically with a ten-instance threshold per theme (Approaching Google Glass and Privacy). Corporate framing matters because "corporate discourse co-shapes users' perception of their technologies": Google's promise was to "put you back in control of your technology." Four privacy conceptions emerge. Privacy of communication: Glass presents "an ability to be constantly watched without knowing whether you are being recorded" and leaves "the interlocutor guessing what the Glass user is really doing"; "human norms of interaction coevolve with new technologies, suggesting that with time, Glass can not only mediate what such norms are but also what meaningful communication is." Privacy as limited access to the self (the sauna, the "Motorola Brick" recalled from 1990). Privacy of experience and memories, where "the nudging design of the media platforms to motivate continuous viewing of these videos (e.g., a default option to 'autoplay' next clip) intensify human curiosity and diffuse the criteria for decision-making." Privacy in public space as civil inattention, which Glass removes because its wearers "neither visibly nor audibly manifest their intentions." The method yields a modest anticipation positioned "'just in time'" rather than too early or too late (Technological Mediation and the Collingridge Dilemma), and the conclusion: "the values used to evaluate technologies are not independent from these technologies but rather are co-constituted by them"; the resulting privacy "body multiple" is "more about lack of control and how people develop ways to deal with that."

## Relation to the argument

RQ1, adaptivity axis. This is the paper the plan named as evidence that mediation is dynamic over time on Verbeek's own account, and it is, but the dynamism sits on the wrong side for the construct. What changes here is the value framework and the social norm; Glass itself is a camera and a display that does the same thing for every wearer. "Dynamics" means coevolution of morality with a fixed device across months and years, not a device re-fitted to the person it mediates within the interaction. So the paper confirms that Verbeek's vocabulary has a temporal axis (technomoral change, "morality in the making") while leaving the run-time axis untouched; Adaptivity is classified unaddressed rather than fixed because the device's fixity is assumed, not argued.

Structural axis. The privacy-of-communication excerpt is the closest any Verbeek text comes to the triad. A wearer, a date across the table, and a device between them whose state the second person cannot read: "guessing what the Glass user is really doing" is the second party's illiteracy about the mediator, and the authors treat it as the bystander's privacy problem, not as a coordination problem shared by both people. The date remains in the world slot. The "autoplay" remark is the one place a platform with its own objective enters, in a parenthesis about YouTube's design, and it goes undeveloped. Remedy locus is the artifact: the point of the method is to anticipate mediations so that development can be guided; nothing is proposed for the two mediated parties as a pair.

RQ2. The method is a template the paper can borrow: treat online reasoning about an early technology as data on how a competence (here, privacy talk) is being re-articulated. That is a route to an empirical algorithmacy study that does not require instrumenting the platform.

## Caution

An exploratory qualitative study of 96 YouTube comments on one satirical video; the authors say the results "do not pretend to be representative." No page numbers here because the HTML has none; the print pagination 291–314 comes from OpenAlex and quotations should be re-located in the PDF before the paper cites them by page. The "third way" argument is advanced against two named Dutch approaches (Swierstra, van de Poel) and inherits Verbeek's dyadic ontology wholesale. Glass was withdrawn in 2015 and relaunched for enterprise in 2017; the case is dated but the method is not.
