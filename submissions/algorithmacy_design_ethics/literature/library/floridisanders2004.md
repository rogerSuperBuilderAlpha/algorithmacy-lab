# Floridi, L., & Sanders, J. W. (2004). On the morality of artificial agents. *Minds and Machines*, 14(3), 349–379.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1023/B:MIND.0000035461.63578.9d · **Read depth:** full_text (author-deposited preprint, University of Hertfordshire repository UHRA eprint 2235, file 901820.pdf, digitally signed by Floridi 30 Jan 2004; read via OCR because the PDF's font map is broken, so section numbers below are the preprint's, not journal page numbers) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, the field's standard reference for the Method of Abstraction in ethics · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** n/a (single agent observed at a level of abstraction; moral agent–patient dyad) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

An entity's agenthood is a property of the level of abstraction (LoA) at which an observer models it, never of the entity "in itself." A LoA is "a finite but non-empty set of observables" (§2.2); a model is "the outcome of the analysis of a system, developed at some LoA(s)"; and an entity "may be described at a range of LoAs and so can have a range of models" (§2.1). The paper's own gloss: "An x is defined or identified as y never absolutely (i.e. LoA-independently), as a Kantian 'thing-in-itself', but always contextually, as a function of a given LoA" (§2). At an adequate LoA an agent is whatever is interactive, autonomous (changes state without external stimulus) and adaptable (its interactions change its transition rules) (§2.5); a moral agent is one capable of "morally qualifiable action," i.e., action that "can cause moral good or evil" (criterion O, §3.1). Artificial agents so characterised are "morally accountable" as sources of good and evil while not "morally responsible," which requires intentional states; the Oedipus case models accountability without responsibility (§3.2, §4).

The worked example that matters here is MENACE, Michie's matchbox tic-tac-toe learner (§2.6.2). At the single-game LoA it is interactive and autonomous but not adaptive; at the tournament LoA the statistics of results reveal an evolving rule, so it is adaptive and an agent; at the system LoA, where "all of MENACE's 'code'" is observed, the apparent rule change "is now revealed ... to be a simple deterministic update of the program state," and MENACE "fails to be an agent." The authors draw the software moral explicitly: "One reflects the 'open source' view of software: the user has access to the code. The other reflects the commercial view that ... he has no access to the code. At stake is whether or not the software forms an (artificial) agent." Section 2.3 then blocks the relativist reading: "a clear indication of the LoA at which a system is being analysed allows pluralism without endorsing relativism. It is a mistake to think that 'anything goes' as long as one makes explicit the LoA, because LoA are mutually comparable and assessable," (i) interfaces "may be fairly ranked depending on how well they satisfy modelling specifications (e.g. informativeness, coherence, elegance, explanatory power, consistency with the data etc.)" and (ii) "different analyses can be fairly compared provided that they share the same LoA." The conclusion adds a payoff for responsibility: the approach lets us "stop the regress of looking for the responsible individual when something evil happens" and makes us "less likely to assign responsibility at any cost, forced by the necessity to identify a human moral agent" (§5).

## Relation to the argument

RQ7, first half, and the answer is a qualified yes. The Method of Abstraction gives the Φ verdict's model-relativity a principled form: a verdict about whether the intermediary is a channel or a party is, in Floridi and Sanders's terms, a verdict about agenthood at a stated LoA, and MENACE shows the same entity flipping between agent and non-agent as the observer's access to its code changes. That is structurally the lab's admission that changing how one encodes the coordination form can change the dyadic-versus-triadic verdict. Two things follow for the paper. First, the LoA method converts the admission from apology to obligation: state the LoA, then rank it against rival LoAs by informativeness, coherence and consistency with the data, and compare only across a shared LoA. "Relative to a stated model" is where the method starts, not where it ends. Second, the MENACE example locates the choice of LoA in the *distribution of access*: the human parties in the triad model the intermediary at the LoA the platform leaves them (the "commercial view," code concealed), while the operator models it at the system LoA. Whether the intermediary appears as a party is therefore partly a fact about who can see what — which is the epistemic asymmetry algorithmacy names. The transition-system language reinforces this: "At a lower LoA an internal transition may become external; at a higher LoA an external transition may become internal" (§2.4), which is the channel/party distinction restated as a boundary choice.

The accountability/responsibility split cuts both ways on the "distributed, deflected, denied" theme. It supplies the vocabulary for saying the intermediary is accountable (a source of the outcome) without pretending it is responsible; but Mittelstadt et al. (2016) note, citing this paper, that assigning moral agency to artificial agents "can allow human stakeholders to shift blame to algorithms," so the same move can license the deflection the companion draft criticises. The paper's own answer — stop the regress toward a responsible human — reads, in a platform setting, uncomfortably like "the computer said so."

## Caution

The LoA method is a modelling epistemology about which observables to include; it says nothing about integration or irreducibility, so it licenses the *form* of the Φ verdict's relativity, not the Φ computation or IIT's own criterion for choosing among candidate systems (the exclusion postulate). The ranking criteria for LoAs are named but not developed here (deferred to Floridi & Sanders's "The Method of Abstraction," 2003, and Floridi 2008), so the obligation the paper imposes has to be discharged with tools this source does not supply. The paper models one agent and its patient; there is no two-human-plus-mediator structure anywhere in it, and "distributed morality" is gestured at (§1, §5) rather than analysed. The OCR text was clean in the body but garbled in the abstract; quotations above come from the body sections.

## Relation to Principle III — bounded outputs, not mechanistic interpretability (cluster F pass, 2026-09-18)

**Remedy locus:** none in the paper's own terms (a modelling epistemology); for Principle III it supplies the obligation to state and rank the level.

The Method of Abstraction turns "predictable" from a property into a choice the paper must declare. A system "may be described at a range of LoAs and so can have a range of models" (§2.1), and MENACE is the demonstration: at the tournament LoA its behaviour reveals "an evolving rule," while at the system LoA, where "all of MENACE's 'code'" is observed, the same behaviour "is now revealed ... to be a simple deterministic update of the program state" (§2.6.2). Mechanistic interpretability is predictability at the system LoA — the code-in-hand view, which Floridi and Sanders themselves tie to the "open source" versus "commercial" access question. Bounded outputs are predictability at a behavioural LoA: the observables are the determinations, their envelope and their stability, and the level is chosen because it is the one the coordinated party can occupy. Principle III must say this, and §2.3 tells it how to say it without relativism: "a clear indication of the LoA at which a system is being analysed allows pluralism without endorsing relativism," levels "may be fairly ranked depending on how well they satisfy modelling specifications (e.g. informativeness, coherence, elegance, explanatory power, consistency with the data etc.)," and analyses "can be fairly compared provided that they share the same LoA." So the paper's obligation is threefold: name the behavioural LoA at which the bound is stated; rank it against the mechanistic LoA by the criterion that matters for the party (that a guarantee at this level is one she can verify), rather than by explanatory power, where the mechanistic level wins; and refuse comparisons across levels — a model interpretable at the system LoA is not thereby predictable at the behavioural one, which is the exact form of the Rudin (2019) distinction. Leveson's (2011) "safety is a system property, not a component property" is the same point in engineering dress. The transition-system remark, "at a lower LoA an internal transition may become external; at a higher LoA an external transition may become internal" (§2.4), is also the reason drift (Chen et al. 2024) is invisible at the mechanistic level: a version swap is an external transition to the deployed model and an internal one to the service, and only the higher LoA sees it. The cautions from the original relation section hold: the method says nothing about which bounds to choose, and its accountability/responsibility split can license "the computer said so."
