# van de Poel, I. (2013). Translating values into design requirements. In D. P. Michelfelder, N. McCarthy, & D. E. Goldberg (Eds.), *Philosophy and Engineering: Reflections on Practice, Principles and Process* (pp. 253–266). Springer.

## What it argues

The abstract, verbatim: "A crucial step in Value Sensitive Design (VSD) is the translation of values into design requirements. However, few research has been done on how this translation can be made. In this contribution, I first consider an example of this translation. I then introduce the notion of values hierarchy, a hierarchy structure of values, norms and design requirements. I discuss the relation of specification, by which values can be translated into design requirements, and the for the sake of relation which connects design requirements to underlying norms and values. I discuss conditions under which a certain specification of values into design requirements is adequate or at least tenable." As Umbrello and van de Poel (2021) use it, the hierarchy has three layers — values at the top, norms in the middle, design requirements at the bottom — read downward as specification and upward as a "for the sake of" relation; translation "can move in a bottom-up direction ... as well as the top-down direction," and the norms layer is where they insert Floridi et al.'s AI4SG principles. Their illustration (their Fig. 7) specifies non-maleficence through the norms of privacy protection and situational fairness into technical requirements for a contact-tracing app. Manders-Huits (2011) cites van de Poel's companion 2009 chapter as the source for the problem of "conflicting and incommensurable values" that VSD leaves unresolved. I did not see the chapter's own worked example and do not describe it.

## Relation to the argument

The hierarchy is the mechanism that joins the two levels RQ5 distinguishes: a VSD process ends in values, an interface affordance is a design requirement, and this is the only formal account in the cluster of how one becomes the other. Read that way the process/product relation is a complement by construction — VSD's stakeholder and value work sits at the top of the hierarchy, Seamful Boundary Disclosure or Counterfactual Exploration at the bottom, and the norms layer in between is where a standard such as IEEE 7000 or the AI4SG list lives. The hierarchy also gives the objective axis a place to be stated, on my reading rather than the author's: the intermediary's objective is a norm, and it either does or does not stand in a "for the sake of" relation to the mediated parties' values; a coopted objective is one whose upward chain terminates in the operator's value alone. That is a test the hierarchy could support and VSD's stakeholder analysis cannot, because the hierarchy tracks justification while stakeholder analysis tracks affectedness. Whether the chapter's adequacy conditions for a specification would license such a use is unknown to me.

## Caution

I have not read the chapter; everything above beyond the abstract is inferred from a later paper by the same author and from a critic's citation of a different chapter. The hierarchy is a normative-analytical tool, not an empirical finding, and its application to an objective set by a third party is my extension, not van de Poel's claim.
