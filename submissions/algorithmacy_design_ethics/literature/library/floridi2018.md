**Identifier:** doi:10.1007/s11023-018-9482-5 · PMC6404626 · **Read depth:** full_text (Europe PMC full-text XML of the open-access version of record; section titles cited because the XML carries no page numbers) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, heavily cited policy synthesis (Scientific Committee of 12 chaired by Floridi; adopted in substance by the EC High-Level Expert Group and OECD per Floridi & Cowls 2019) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** designer–stakeholders (a "small fraction of humanity" designing; "everyone else" affected) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

AI is "a reservoir of 'smart agency'" whose opportunities and risks fall on four axes of human dignity: who we can become (self-realisation vs devaluing skills), what we can do (agency vs removing responsibility), what we can achieve (capabilities vs losing control), and how we can interact (cohesion vs eroding self-determination). The framework synthesises 47 principles from six documents (Asilomar, Montreal, IEEE EAD v2, EGE, House of Lords AIUK, Partnership on AI) into the four bioethics principles plus one new one: "explicability, understood as incorporating both intelligibility and accountability." The definition is precise: explicability "both in the epistemological sense of 'intelligibility' (as an answer to the question 'how does it work?') and in the ethical sense of 'accountability' (as an answer to the question: 'who is responsible for the way it works?')." It is the enabling principle: "for AI to promote and not constrain human autonomy, our 'decision about who should decide' must be informed by knowledge of how AI would act instead of us; and for AI to be just, we must ensure that the technology — or, more accurately, the people and organisations developing and deploying it — are held accountable." Autonomy becomes "meta-autonomy," a "decide-to-delegate" model in which "humans should always retain the power to decide which decisions to take" and "any delegation should remain overridable in principle." Twenty recommendations follow, including a framework for explicability of "socially significant decisions" giving individuals "a factual, direct, and clear explanation of the decision-making process" (Rec. 4), scrutiny of algorithmic decisions in court with zero-knowledge proofs where disclosure is too risky (Rec. 5), and a redress mechanism with an AI ombudsperson (Rec. 7).

The framework's own answer to who its principles protect is on record. Under justice: "are we (humans) the patient, receiving the 'treatment' of AI, the doctor prescribing it? Or both?" Under explicability: "we could be either — depending on the circumstances and on who 'we' are in our everyday life. The situation is inherently unequal: a small fraction of humanity is currently engaged in the design and development of a set of technologies that are already transforming the everyday lives of just about everyone else."

## Relation to the argument

RQ7, the tooth. AI4People's frame is two-sided, but the two sides are designer and patient, not two coordinating humans. Every principle is written from the vantage of a single affected person facing a system built by someone else: meta-autonomy assumes the human who delegates is the human who chose the objective (Asilomar's "to accomplish human-chosen objectives," quoted approvingly), and the explicability recommendation gives "individuals" an explanation of "the decision-making process" as if there were one decision and one individual it concerns. In the triad neither human delegated the coordination to the intermediary and neither set its objective; the delegation was made by the operator on both their behalfs. "Decide-to-delegate" therefore has no purchase on either party. Explicability fares better than autonomy: its accountability half already names a third role — "the people and organisations developing and deploying it" — so the "who is responsible?" question has a triadic answer built in. Its intelligibility half does not: "how does it work?" is asked once, by one party, and nothing in the framework contemplates that the answer given to A reveals something about B, or that A's and B's interests in the answer conflict. A coherent triadic explicability would need intelligibility indexed to each party and accountability directed at the objective-setter; AI4People supplies the second and is silent on the first. The nearest it comes to the triad is the "societal cohesion" opportunity — AI helping with "coordination complexity" through "self-nudging" — which it treats as society-level and self-imposed, not as mediation between two particular people.

## Caution

A committee policy document, not an argument; the principles are a synthesis by inspection of six Western documents and the authors themselves flag the missing perspectives (Floridi & Cowls 2019, §4). It contains no empirical work and no theory of explanation; "factual, direct, and clear explanation" is asserted as a goal without any account of what an explanation is or when it helps, so it cannot be cited for or against the Bo et al. (2025) finding. The "dual advantage" section is written for organisations and reads as adoption advocacy. Floridi's funding disclosure in the companion HDSR paper lists Facebook and Google.
