# Cotter, K., & Reisdorf, B. C. (2020). Algorithmic knowledge gaps: A new dimension of (digital) inequality. *International Journal of Communication*, 14, 745–765.

**Identifier:** ISSN 1932-8036; no DOI (IJoC does not assign them); URL ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/view/12450; CC BY-NC-ND · **Read depth:** full_text (publisher PDF) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, open-access communication journal; survey study · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (Internet users and search-engine ranking algorithms) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

"Algorithms serve as gatekeepers and arbiters of truth online. Understanding how algorithms influence which information individuals encounter better enables them to properly calibrate their reception of the information" (p. 745). The authors propose that algorithmic knowledge follows the knowledge-gap hypothesis (Tichenor et al. 1970) — it accrues faster to the socioeconomically advantaged — and that algorithms are "experience technologies" (Blank and Dutton) learned through use, so that use, which is itself stratified, mediates the gap. Algorithmic knowledge is defined in tiers: "in its most basic form ... mere awareness that search results do not display all information sources equally," then "understanding of the criteria by which algorithms rank content," then "insight about the principles and methods of software development that underlie algorithms and/or the social and political effects of algorithms" (p. 747). The stakes: "a lack of algorithmic knowledge renders individuals unknowingly reliant on these algorithms," creating "classes of users with the skills to question and critique algorithmic representations of reality and classes more likely to unwittingly internalize the normative discourses inscribed in algorithmic outputs" (pp. 748–749). Data: a January 2017 web survey of a stratified random probability sample of 2,018 US Internet users, weighted to the online population (Google funded the study and did not see the article before publication, n. 1). Algorithmic knowledge is a six-item scale — perceived influence on search results of site popularity, location, past search history, search-engine optimization, relevance, and advertising fees — reduced by PCA to one component (58.3% of variance). Results: education predicts knowledge (β = .11, p < .001), age negatively (β = −.12); gender, race and income do not; SES alone explains 9.2% of variance (Table 1). Education and income predict frequency and breadth of search use; age negatively (Table 2). Breadth of use is the strongest predictor of knowledge (β = .46, p < .001), "nearly 5 times larger than that of education," and lifts adjusted R² from 13.0% to 27.4%; frequency drops out once breadth is entered (Table 3). Discussion: "using online search for a variety of purposes allows users to occupy various vantage points and, thus, permits a more complete view of what is happening 'under the hood'" (p. 757); but "the opacity of algorithms may preclude users from accumulating sufficient algorithmic knowledge for critical assessments ... through use alone" (p. 759), and "algorithmic knowledge does not supplant critical thinking skills or information literacy ... a broader algorithmic literacy may, too, entail familiarity with how algorithms work as well as the ability to assess their information outputs" (p. 759).

## Relation to the argument

The paper is a dyadic literacy study, and it is useful to the construct for three reasons that have nothing to do with its dyadic framing. First, the "experience technology" finding — breadth of use, not frequency, predicts knowledge — is the only quantitative evidence this pass found for the *anticipatory* and *interpretive* parts of the construct's competence: people learn what an intermediary does by encountering it from several positions. In the triad the second human is a position the first can never occupy, so the vantage-point mechanism has a structural ceiling; the paper supplies the mechanism and the construct supplies the reason it fails. Second, the authors' own ceiling argument (p. 759) — that opacity and constant change may cap what experience yields — is the human-factors reason a competence cannot substitute for a designed affordance, which is what the paper's four affordances are for. Third, the knowledge-gap result cuts against any uniform competence claim: if algorithmacy is learned through use, it will be distributed like every other digital skill, and the paper must say so (de Certeau's "elbow-room" caution, decerteau1984.md, is the same point from theory). CUTS AGAINST the construct's supersession claim in one respect: the third tier of their definition — knowledge of "the social and political effects of algorithms" — is broad enough to include knowing whose interests a ranking serves, so "algorithmic knowledge" as they define it is not purely mechanical. The construct's distinctive content is not the objective axis alone but the *co-determination* of a second party, which no item in their six-item scale touches.

## Relation to Principle I (added 2026-09-18)

Two additions under the pivoted thesis. First, the knowledge-gap result now does double duty: it is the reason a *competence* remedy is regressive (algorithmacy would distribute like every other digital skill; Gran et al. 2021 replicate the gradient at national scale in Norway) and therefore the reason Principle I places an *agent* in the relation rather than a skill in the subject — a counter-agent is the one remedy in the family whose benefit does not track the user's education. Second, the "experience technology" mechanism — knowledge accrues through breadth of use, through occupying "various vantage points" — is what a client-side agent could do on a user's behalf across many coordinations, and what a collective tool (calacci2022.md) does across many users; the paper can use Cotter and Reisdorf's mechanism to say what the counter-agent automates. The authors' opacity ceiling (p. 759) remains the limit on both.

## Caution

One domain (web search), one country (US), one survey wave (2017), a scale the authors call "a fairly simple operationalization" and "a conservative estimate" (p. 759); the survey was not designed for this question and the items measure *perceived influence* of known factors, not accuracy of a mental model. Cross-sectional: breadth of use could follow from knowledge as easily as precede it. Google funded the data collection. The search engine is an intermediary between a user and content, not between two users; the study does not model a second human at all.
