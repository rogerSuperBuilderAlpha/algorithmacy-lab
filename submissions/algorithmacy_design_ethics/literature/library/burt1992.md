## What it argues

"A structural hole is a relationship of nonredundancy between two contacts. The hole is a buffer, like an insulator in an electric circuit" (1992b: 65 [1992: 18]); "the structural holes that generate information benefits also generate control benefits, giving certain players an advantage in negotiating their relationships" (1992b: 76 [1992: 30]). The control benefit is Simmel's: "The tertius gaudens is 'the third who benefits' ... an individual who profits from the disunion of others. There are two tertius strategies: being the third between two or more players after the same relationship, and being the third between players in two or more relations with conflicting demands" (1992b: 76 [1992: 30–31]). "There is a presumption of tension here. Control emerges from tertius brokering tension between other players. No tension, no tertius" (1992b: 78 [1992: 32]). The position holds only while the contacts do not deal directly: successful tertius strategies bring "together players who are willing to negotiate, have sufficiently comparable resources to view one another's preferences as valid, but won't negotiate with one another directly to the exclusion of the tertius" (1992b: 79 [1992: 33]). Constraint is formal: contact *j* constrains player *i* by c_ij = (p_ij + Σ_q p_iq p_qj)², summed over contacts (1992: 54–55), rising as one's contacts are tied to each other. "Players with relationships free of structural holes at their own end and rich in structural holes at the other end are structurally autonomous" (1992b: 83 [1992: 45]).

## Relation to the argument

Burt gives the structural axis a second, independent formalization. A platform between a driver and a customer is a broker with *maximal structural autonomy* in his sense: every contact pair on either side is a hole (riders and drivers are never tied to each other), and there is no hole at the broker's own end (no second intermediary spans the same pair). Burt's constraint index and Φ detect the same absent tie from different sides — his is a measure of *position in a graph of existing ties*, Φ a measure of whether the *dynamics* factor without the middle node — and the paper can use the agreement as a robustness point while noting that Burt's index, like his brokers, presupposes the ties are given and asks what the position yields, whereas the bypass question asks whether the tie could be restored (the coordinative_sovereignty chapter already draws this line). "No tension, no tertius" is the RQ8 answer in one sentence: the two humans' opposition is what the broker's control benefit consumes, not an obstacle to addressing it. CUTS AGAINST: Burt's broker is a person who *chooses between substitutable contacts* — plays one bid against another; the platform does not choose among riders, it matches all of them, at scale, by rule. Its control benefit is the matching itself and the terms it sets, which is closer to Simmel's arbitrator-plus-*gaudens* than to Burt's bargaining entrepreneur. And Burt's information benefit is arbitrage between what disconnected contacts know; the platform's is that it *interprets* both — a category Burt's framework, which treats information as flows across ties rather than as readings of parties, does not have.
