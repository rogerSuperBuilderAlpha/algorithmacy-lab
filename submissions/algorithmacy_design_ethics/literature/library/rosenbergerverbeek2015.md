## What it argues

The chapter promises "a field guide to postphenomenology" while conceding "there is no strict postphenomenological methodology that scholars could follow. Postphenomenology comes in just as many flavors as there are scholars in the field" (p. 10). It summarizes "Ihde's four basic forms of technological mediation: embodiment relations, hermeneutic relations," alterity and background (p. 13), with the usual cases: glasses through which "a user looks through the glasses upon a transformed world" (p. 14); hermeneutic relations "used through an act of perceiving and" interpreting (p. 17); alterity, where "some forms of interface are devised specifically to mimic the shape of person"-to-person exchange, "voice interactive personal assistant smartphone applications" (p. 18); and background relations as "a piece of the immediate environment" (Ihde 1990, 109, quoted p. 19). The authors then add Verbeek's newer relations. "More intimate than a relation of embodiment" is the cyborg relation (p. 20); beyond the background relation "there is a relation of 'immersion,' in which a technological background interacts actively with human beings. Moreover, augmented reality technologies, which add an extra layer to our experience of the world, open up a relation of 'augmentation'" (p. 21). Immersion is glossed: smart environments "'perceive' their users too, and 'act' upon them. Examples are 'smart toilets' that automatically analyze one's excrements and produce a medical report on the basis of this, or 'smart beds' in hospitals that can..." (p. 21); such environments open "the possibility to be immersed in how smart environments perceive and act upon human beings, as is the case for some 'persuasive technologies' that give people feedback on their lifestyle. When looking in the mirror, you actually look into a camera and see..." (p. 22). The methodological second half covers multistability, from Ihde's "early-career work on visual perception" (p. 26); "variational analysis," "the method of brainstorming stabilities of a multistable technology" (p. 27), often deployed "as part of a critique of another account seen to be somehow totalizing" (p. 28); "relational strategy," the habits by which a user "read[s] off a particular stability," as when a friend "relays an alternative hermeneutic strategy" for the Necker cube (p. 29); and a proposed "postphenomenological field theory" with "two further variables" from Rosenberger (p. 23). The volume's last chapter (Michelfelder) mentions "'Smart' cars" that use seat biosensors to judge "how 'fit' the driver is to operate the car, making adjustments as perceived to be necessary" (p. 244).

## Relation to the argument

RQ1, adaptivity axis. The field's own statement of its schema, co-written by Verbeek, describes the immersion relation as an environment that perceives and acts, and its examples (toilet, bed, mirror) are read-and-respond devices whose response rule does not change with what they read. No snippet in pp. 9–42 contains "learn," "adapt," "algorithm" or "feedback" in the sense of a system updating itself; the only "feedback" is the mirror's "feedback on their lifestyle" (p. 22), which is output to the user. So the canonical schema has an active-environment relation but no learning relation, which is the narrowed critique the plan asked me to test for. Immersion is the nearest miss and it misses.

Structural axis. The chapter has one intentional human throughout; the second person appears only as the friend who relays a hermeneutic strategy (p. 29), which is a case of one human teaching another to read a fixed artifact, not of an artifact reading two humans. "Relational strategy" is nonetheless the field's own name for the competence the construct calls literacy, and the paper can say so: the Necker-cube passage is literacy in postphenomenological dress, and algorithmacy is what is needed when the artifact chooses the stability for you and chooses differently for the person opposite. Remedy locus is none; the chapter equips analysts, not users or designers.
