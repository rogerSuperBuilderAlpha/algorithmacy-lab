# Klein, G., Woods, D. D., Bradshaw, J. M., Hoffman, R. R., & Feltovich, P. J. (2004). Ten challenges for making automation a "team player" in joint human-agent activity. *IEEE Intelligent Systems*, 19(6), 91–95.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1109/MIS.2004.74 · **Read depth:** full_text (published PDF hosted by IHMC, the authors' institute; journal pagination intact) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed magazine column (Human-Centered Computing department), position piece · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology as members of a multi-party team ("an ensemble of people"), with the agent required to adopt the team's goals · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Joint activity is "an extended set of actions that are carried out by an ensemble of people who are coordinating with each other," and it has four requirements: a Basic Compact, mutual predictability, mutual directability, and common ground (p. 91). The Compact is "an agreement (often tacit) to facilitate coordination, work toward shared goals, and prevent breakdowns in team coordination," and it "involves a commitment to some degree of goal alignment. Typically this entails one or more participants relaxing their own shorter-term goals in order to permit more global and long-term team goals to be addressed" — goals that "might be shared (for example, a relay team) or individual (such as highway drivers wanting to ensure their own safe journeys)" (p. 91). It must be "continuously reinforced or renewed," and a party that intends to drop out "must signal this to the other parties" (p. 91). The ten challenges follow: enter a Basic Compact; "adequately model the other participants' intentions and actions"; be mutually predictable; be directable, with policy as the mechanism; "make pertinent aspects of their status and intentions obvious"; interpret others' signals (the "Mirror-Mirror principle": every participant "will form a model of the other participant agents"); "engage in goal negotiation"; support collaborative planning; manage attention; and control coordination costs (pp. 92–94). Two verdicts frame the list: "No form of automation today or on the horizon can enter fully into the rich forms of Basic Compact that are used among people" (p. 92), and "the agents must conform to the operators' needs rather than require operators to adapt to them" (p. 94). The aviation example is the automation that "can compensate for trouble (for example, asymmetric lift due to wing icing), but currently does so invisibly" until "the crew might take over too late" (p. 94).

## Relation to the argument

Objective axis, and the sharpest available contrast to the construct. The team-player agenda is explicitly multi-party — an ensemble, not a dyad — and it is the one classic source that puts goals, goal negotiation and the modelling of other participants at the centre. But it makes goal alignment a *precondition*: an agent is a team player only if it "understand[s] and accept[s] the enterprise's joint goals" (p. 92). The construct's intermediary pursues an objective neither party set; on this page that agent has not entered the Compact and is, by definition, not a team player — the paper's own breakdown case, "a party abandon[ing] the team without clearly signaling" (p. 91). The highway-drivers example is instructive: drivers with individual goals coordinate under shared rules, so two parties with divergent interests are admitted, but there is no intermediary between them, and when a driver rejects the Compact (rushing to hospital) the required behaviour is to signal it with emergency blinkers. Challenge 7, goal negotiation, is the closest affordance to "contest what it commits," and Challenges 5 and 6 to "infer what the intermediary is doing"; each presumes the agent wants to be understood. On this pass's question: multiple humans are modelled, all on the same team, and the agent's obligation runs to "the operators' needs." No counterpart whose interests the agent also serves appears; the possibility is excluded by the Compact rather than overlooked.

## Caution

A five-page position column with no data, derived from a longer manuscript on common ground (Klein et al. 2005). The challenges are stated as requirements, not tested, and the authors say the full Compact is beyond any foreseeable agent. Written about robotic and software agents in military and space settings, not platforms or recommender systems. The goal-alignment premise is asserted, not argued against alternatives, so it cannot be cited as a finding that misaligned agents fail — only as the field's definition of a team player.
