## What it argues

Reconstructed from Johns's quotations, all of which give AHR page numbers. Eisenstein defends the "printing revolution" thesis of her 1979 book against a "generational shift" in the history of the book (Johns, p. 108). She holds that her argument should not be "framed by" the history of the book (p. 88, per Johns p. 108 n. 5, who pairs it with her 1979 claim that the printing revolution was "sui generis — something to which conventional models of historical change cannot be applied," *Printing Press*, p. 168). She organizes the dispute around three dichotomies — "impersonal processes" versus "personal agency," the "localism" of Johns's England versus the "cosmopolitan" perspective print demands, and the printing revolution as "discursive construct" versus "historical development" (p. 90, per Johns p. 116). She writes that "The idea that simply printing a text could make it more accurate or more credible strikes me as absurd" (p. 92, per Johns p. 120 n. 35), while also holding, in the 1979 book, that "ancient and medieval scientific traditions were transformed by the capacity of printing to transmit records of observations without any loss of precision and in full detail" (*Printing Press*, p. 470, per Johns n. 35). She argues that where "ignorant" craftsmen multiplied errors, learned masters could reduce them (p. 92, per Johns p. 120); she cites a fifteenth-century Freising missal as evidence that print created fixed, identical texts (p. 93, per Johns p. 121); and she offers Gassendi's supposed printed letter organizing observation of the 1631 transit of Mercury as "a collaborative effort in simultaneous observation that was made possible by print and had been impossible in the age of scribes," "scarcely 'local in character'" (p. 95, per Johns pp. 114–115). She charges Johns with methodological individualism (n. 18, on Streater), with believing that gentlemen were capable of "soaring above the commercial fray" and with favouring privileges (p. 101, per Johns p. 112 — the phrase is Johns's own from *The Nature of the Book*, where he reports it as the view of "supporters of the patents regime"), with neglecting religion beyond the Catholic/Protestant division that "affected" scientific publication (p. 90), and with reducing the printing revolution to a historical "blip" by placing it in long-term context (per Johns p. 108). She also says she always maintained that the press itself was "of no more consequence than any other inanimate tool" (per Johns pp. 113–114, who traces the line to *Printing Press*, p. 703, where it is immediately dismissed as "counter-factual speculation"). The reply (pp. 126–128) presumably restates the case; its content is unknown here.

## Relation to the argument

This paper's use. Eisenstein's is the strong technological-affordance reading: standardization, dissemination and fixity as properties the press confers. She is the position the working hypothesis is tested against, and her own 2002 sentence — that simply printing a text making it more credible "strikes me as absurd" — shows the two sides closer than the polemic suggests. The paper can cite her, through Johns, for the affordance side of "as much a product of social actions as of the inherent properties of the press," but must not cite her from this card for anything not quoted above, because her article was not read. Nothing in the exchange is cognitive; it is an argument about institutions and objects.

The scaffolding essay's use. Eisenstein is the natural source for the essay's "Print shattered that monopoly" register, and she is also the source Johns shows relying on secondary literature and, in the Gassendi case, on an episode that "proves on examination to be entirely fictitious" (Johns p. 115). If the essay keeps the revolutionary framing it should cite Eisenstein for it and cite Johns's rejoinder alongside; it cannot present the framing as settled.
