# Lyons, H., Velloso, E., & Miller, T. (2021). Conceptualising contestability: Perspectives on contesting algorithmic decisions. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, 5(CSCW1), Article 106, 26 pages.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3449180 · arXiv:2103.01774v1 · **Read depth:** full_text (arXiv v1, 23 Feb 2021; DOI and article number confirmed from the paper's own ACM reference line, not independently against the ACM DL) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, top venue (PACM HCI / CSCW); qualitative thematic analysis of public-consultation submissions · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (decision maker vs decision subject) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Australia's 2019 draft AI Ethics Framework was the first to make contestability a principle in its own right — Principle 7: "When an algorithm impacts a person there must be an efficient process to allow that person to challenge the use or output of the algorithm" (§3.1). The authors read all 116 published submissions, kept the 65 that mention contest, appeal, recourse, redress, remedy or compensation, and coded them thematically. Three findings. The nature of contestability is disputed — an end in itself for some, for others a dimension of accountability or fairness, or "merely ... opportunities that can be given to people so they can exercise their autonomy" (Gradient Institute, §4.1.2). It is seen as protection for individuals against a power differential that opacity widens, since "decision makers are privy to information that is not available to decision subjects" (§4.2.1), and it needs enforceability and redress. And it is imagined on the model of contesting human decisions — internal review, ombudsman, court, with an insistence on human review; "a majority of the submissions viewed the ability to contest as a post-hoc mechanism such as an appeal process, rather than a mechanism that can be designed into an AI system" (§6). The operational map (Fig. 2) runs from policy preliminaries — what can be contested, who can, who is accountable, what review — through notification, explanation, a pathway to contest, and review. The discussion is the paper's best part. Robo-debt: an automated welfare-debt system that reversed the onus of proof was contested successfully case by case in the appeals tribunal for four years while "the system was allowed to remain in place"; only a class action ended it (§6.2). Hence "contestability can act as a protection, but it should be considered to be the last line of defence," and contestation "places a burden on the decision subject" that falls unevenly, since "some people have less knowledge, resources or time than others." The authors also record Kaminski's doubt "whether a company whose interests do not always align with its users' will be capable of providing adequate process" (§1), and Waldman's point that the perceived objectivity of algorithms "shields algorithmic systems from critical interrogation" so that people "may not even contemplate that a decision is wrong, and so will not contest" (§6.4).

## Relation to the argument

This is the empirical record of what "contest" means to the organisations that will implement it, and it cuts against reading "contest what it commits" as something an interface delivers. In this corpus contestation is institutional, post hoc and dyadic: a decision maker and a decision subject, a human reviewer, a tribunal. No submission models a second mediated party; the counterpart the construct calls co-determined does not appear, which is itself a finding for RQ5 — contestability discourse inherits VSD's dyad and misses the third party. Two passages support the construct directly. The competence gradient (knowledge, resources, time) is algorithmacy's distribution problem stated by practitioners, and Waldman's "shield" is the interpretive deficit that precedes contest. Robo-debt is the paper's contribution to the objective axis: individual contest corrected outcomes and left the objective intact, so contest at the interface cannot be the whole answer to cooptation. The paper's own recommendation — Almada's contestability by design, participatory and preventive — is where it hands off to Alfrink et al. (2023) and to the seam.

## Caution

The sample is not decision subjects: 65 submissions, mostly from academics, firms, industry bodies and government, coded by the first author alone with Braun and Clarke's reflexive method; the authors flag that "there are many voices that have not been heard." It is one national consultation in 2019, framed by the discussion paper's own wording, and it contains no design, no interface and no observed contestation. The human-review preference the authors label "humanity-fetishism" is their interpretation, not a finding.

## Relation to Principle II — structural refusal, not hermeneutics (cluster F pass, 2026-09-18)

**Remedy locus:** subject (contest is something the decision subject does, after the fact, at her own cost).

This is the lead card for Principle II, and the case for refusal over hermeneutics can be made almost entirely in the paper's own words. Robo-debt was contested successfully, case by case, for four years "while the system was allowed to remain in place"; the contests corrected outcomes and left the objective — reversing the onus of proof at scale — exactly where it was, and only a class action, which is refusal of the scheme rather than of its determinations, ended it. From that the authors draw the conclusion Principle II starts from: contestability "should be considered to be the last line of defence." Two further findings do the rest. Contest "places a burden on the decision subject" that falls unevenly, since "some people have less knowledge, resources or time than others" — so a remedy that consists in reading and arguing is a remedy whose benefit is distributed by competence, which is the objection to hermeneutics stated as an equity finding. And Waldman's "shield": the perceived objectivity of the algorithm means people "may not even contemplate that a decision is wrong, and so will not contest," so the hermeneutic remedy fails first for those who most need it. What the paper reports the corpus lacking is as telling as what it has: nowhere in the authors' thematic account does a submission propose that the subject be able to withdraw from the automated process while keeping the service (the nearest is the observation that "an individual's inability to disengage" from government warrants a higher standard for government decisions, §4.2.1; the paper's own §2.2.2 notes GDPR Article 22(1)'s right not to be subject to solely automated decisions, but as law, not as a submission's proposal), and the contest they imagine is "post-hoc ... an appeal process, rather than a mechanism that can be designed into an AI system." Principle II is the design of that missing option. The connections to the rest of cluster F: Hirschman (1970) explains why voice of this kind is the weakest position; Zong and Matias (2024) name the offered appeal a refusal "designed to placate"; Alfrink et al. (2023) cite this paper for "a right to a new decision, compensation of harm inflicted, or reversal" — that is Alfrink's wording; the submissions' own terms, at §4.2.3 "Redress," are "a new decision, compensation for any harm erroneously caused, or reversal of the effects of a decision" — and go no further; and GDPR Article 22(3), which gives exactly the three things this corpus wants (intervention, a point of view, a contest), is the legal form of the last line of defence. One caution for the paper: the authors' own preferred remedy is Almada's participatory, ex-ante contestability by design, which is still hermeneutic in kind — it argues about the model earlier — and the paper should not enlist them for refusal without saying that.
