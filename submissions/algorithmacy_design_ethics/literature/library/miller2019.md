# Miller, T. (2019). Explanation in artificial intelligence: Insights from the social sciences. *Artificial Intelligence*, 267, 1–38.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/j.artint.2018.07.007 (Crossref-verified 2026-09-18: *Artificial Intelligence* 267, pp. 1–38, 2019) · arXiv:1706.07269 · **Read depth:** partial (arXiv copy, 66 pp.: §1 incl. 1.2 Major findings, §2.3 and §2.6 Explanation and XAI, §4.5 Explanation evaluation, §4.6, §5.3 Social explanation and XAI, §6 Conclusions read closely; §3 social attribution and §4.1–4.4 skimmed by heading; page numbers are the arXiv copy's) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, *Artificial Intelligence*; the standard survey bringing philosophy and cognitive psychology of explanation into XAI · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** explainer–explainee dyad (an explanatory agent and one human) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Four findings from the philosophy and psychology of explanation that XAI ignores at its cost (§1.2, p. 6): (1) "Explanations are contrastive — ... people do not ask why event P happened, but rather why event P happened instead of some event Q"; (2) "Explanation[s] are selected (in a biased manner) — people rarely, if ever, expect an explanation that consists of an actual and complete cause"; (3) "Probabilities probably don't matter — while truth and likelihood are important in explanation and probabilities really do matter, referring to probabilities or statistical relationships in explanation is not as effective as referring to causes ... using statistical generalisations to explain why events occur is unsatisfying, unless accompanied by an underlying causal explanation for the generalisation itself"; (4) "Explanations are social — they are a transfer of knowledge, presented as part of a conversation or interaction." From these: "Extracting a causal chain and displaying it to a person is causal attribution, not (necessarily) an explanation ... for most AI models, it is not reasonable to expect a lay-user to be able to interpret a causal chain, no matter how it is presented" (§2.6.1, p. 20). On probability (§4.5.2, pp. 44–45): "The use of statistical relationships to explain events is considered to be unsatisfying on its own. This is because people desire causes to explain events, not associative relationships" (the red-balls example from Josephson & Josephson; the student who scored 50%); "the most likely or 'true' cause is not necessarily the best explanation" (Hilton); McClure's studies "found that people tend not to judge the quality of explanations around their probability, but instead around their so-called pragmatic influences." Kulesza et al.'s explanatory-debugging study of a music recommender found "Complete but unsound explanations improved the participants' mental models more than soundness ... but reduced trust," and yielded three principles, "Be sound; Be complete; but Don't overwhelm," of which "principles 1 and 2 are at odds with principle 3" (p. 45). Section 5.3.5, "Dilution," warns that "the less relevant parts of the chain will dilute those parts that are crucial to the particular question," and 5.3.4 that sceptical explainees "could interpret explanations as biased." An explanatory agent needs "a model of it's own decision making — a model of self — that exists merely for the purpose of explanation" (§2.6.4, p. 22), and explanation "will have to be interactive and adhere to maxims of communication" (§5.3.1, p. 56).

## Relation to the argument

RQ7, transparency-critique question: Miller carries the **closest thing in this cluster to a theoretical prediction of Bo et al.'s (2025) finding**, and it should be cited ahead of Burrell or Ananny and Crawford for that purpose. Token-level confidence highlighting is a display of "probabilities or statistical relationships" in place of causes; finding (3) says such a display is "not as effective as referring to causes" and "unsatisfying" without a causal account of the generalisation; dilution says surfacing everything drowns the part that answers the question; Kulesza's "don't overwhelm" is the design corollary; and §5.3.4 predicts that low-trust explainees will read the display as biased. What Miller does not predict is the ordinal outcome — worse than no explanation — nor does he treat confidence displays specifically; the prediction is that they will underperform causal, contrastive, selected explanations, which is a weaker claim than Bo et al.'s result. The paper can say that the 2025 finding was foreseeable on Miller's 2019 synthesis and leave the ordinal surprise as Bo et al.'s own.

Miller also supplies the positive form of the interpretive component: the competence the human needs is to pose a *contrastive* question — why this determination rather than that — which is exactly what counterfactual-exploration affordances (Wachter et al. 2018) let a user do, and "if a person provides a foil, they are implicitly pointing towards the part of the model they do not understand" (p. 20). And the "model of self" argument gives the intermediary side of Seamful Boundary Disclosure: an explanatory model that "may be only an approximation of the original model" but exists to be explained from — which is a warrant for disclosing boundaries and dependencies rather than internals.

The paper CUTS AGAINST the construct's triad by construction: its explainer–explainee model has one explainee, and "Theory of Mind" (§5.3.3) is a model of *the* other. Nothing in it contemplates an explainer whose explanation to A is also information about B, or whose objective is not the explainee's. That is a gap the paper can name, not a finding to cite.

## Caution

A survey of other people's experiments; the psychology it draws on (Hilton, Lombrozo, McClure, Kulesza) was run on stories, medical vignettes and a song recommender, not on LLM outputs or confidence displays. The Kulesza soundness/completeness result is one study. Finding (3) is hedged in the title ("probably") and Miller notes Lombrozo's evidence that base-rate information does shift explanation choice when supplied jointly (p. 45). I did not read §3 or §4.1–4.4 closely. The DOI above resolves in Crossref to this article (vol. 267, pp. 1–38).
