# Parasuraman, R., & Manzey, D. H. (2010). Complacency and bias in human use of automation: An attentional integration. *Human Factors*, 52(3), 381–410.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/0018720810376055 · **Read depth:** full_text (author deposit at TU Berlin's DepositOnce repository, publisher layout, journal pagination intact) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed, flagship HF journal; integrative review with a proposed model · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology (one operator or one crew, one automation); teams enter only as redundant second crewmembers on the same side · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Complacency and automation bias have been studied in different paradigms — supervisory control and decision support — and the paper argues they are "different manifestations of overlapping automation-induced phenomena, with attention playing a central role" (p. 381). Complacency is operationalised as poorer detection of automation failures under automation than under manual control: in Parasuraman, Molloy and Singh (1993) detection was 82% under variable-reliability automation and 33% under constant reliability, a "149% difference," against about 97% in the single-task baseline (p. 384); the effect survives in pilots with ~440 flight hours, in general-aviation pilots on a real EICAS, and in air traffic controllers on a conflict probe (p. 387), and 60 minutes of practice does not remove it (p. 387). Automation bias follows Mosier and Skitka's (1996) definition, using an aid's output "as a heuristic replacement for vigilant information seeking and processing" (p. 391), and splits into omission and commission errors; the flagship numbers are Mosier et al.'s (1998) 55% omission and 100% commission rates in commercial pilots and the 67% "phantom memory" of cues that were not there (pp. 392–393), Skitka et al.'s (1999) 41% omission rate in students, and Bahner et al.'s (2008) 21% commission rate under a wrong first diagnosis despite full access to the raw parameters (p. 400). Three contributing factors are named: cognitive-miser heuristics, perceived authority of automation, and diffusion of responsibility, the last framed by analogy to social loafing — "to the extent that human users perceive an automated aid as another team member, they may perceive themselves as less responsible for the outcome" (p. 392). The integrated model (Figure 6, p. 404) has system properties, task context and person feeding a "complacency potential" that becomes an attentional bias, with a positive feedback loop of "learned carelessness" and a negative loop driven by experienced failures (p. 405); Moray's "eutactic" operator, who samples at the normatively optimal rate, is adopted as the standard against which complacency is inferred (pp. 399–400).

## Relation to the argument

Objective axis, indirectly, and a clean negative on this pass's question. Every study reviewed has one operator and one automation over one process, and where a second human appears it is a redundant crewmember on the operator's own side: "the presence of a second crewmember did not affect the strength of automation bias for either omission or commission errors," replicated in 48 glass-cockpit pilots (p. 397), and the summary lists "(f) can affect decision making in individuals as well as in teams" (p. 397). Teams here are co-operators sharing the automation; there is no counterpart whose interests the aid also serves. The paper's normative anchor is the same as Lee and See's — a "well calibrated" operator "whose trust is calibrated to the actual reliability of automation" (p. 399) — so complacency is defined relative to reliability at the operator's own task, and the "phantom memory" and "looking-but-not-seeing" findings (pp. 393, 402) show operators reconstructing evidence to fit the aid's verdict. That last result matters for the interpretive competence in the construct: an intermediary that also serves the other party would be issuing verdicts the operator has this documented tendency to rationalise. The social-loafing analogy is the closest the paper comes to treating the automation as a party rather than a tool, and it treats that perception as a bias to be corrected, not a structure to be designed for.

## Caution

A review of reviews: the model is proposed, not tested, and the authors call it "a heuristic framework" and concede that "strong associations between personality... and automation complacency have not been found" because "the currently available database is very small" (p. 390). Most cited studies use MATB or W/PANES microworlds with students and failure rates (12% or more) the authors themselves call "unrepresentative of any real automated system" (p. 384). Complacency inferred from detection rates without an independent measure of monitoring is exactly the inference Moray and Inagaki (2000) reject, and the authors accept that objection for most of the literature (p. 399). Nothing here concerns adversarial or multi-principal automation.
