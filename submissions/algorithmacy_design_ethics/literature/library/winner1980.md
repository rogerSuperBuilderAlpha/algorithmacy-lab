# Winner, L. (1980). Do artifacts have politics? *Daedalus*, 109(1), 121–136. Reprinted as ch. 2 of *The Whale and the Reactor: A Search for Limits in an Age of High Technology* (U Chicago Press, 1986), pp. 19–39.

## What it argues

Winner defines politics as "arrangements of power and authority in human associations as well as the activities that take place within those arrangements" (p. 123) and offers "two ways in which artifacts can contain political properties." The first: "the invention, design, or arrangement of a specific technical device or system becomes a way of settling an issue in a particular community" (p. 123) — Moses's "two hundred or so low-hanging overpasses on Long Island" built "to specifications that would discourage the presence of buses on his parkways" (pp. 123–124, on Caro's evidence), McCormick's pneumatic moulding machines that broke the union and were then abandoned (pp. 124–125), and the tomato harvester that cut growers from four thousand to six hundred (p. 126). He generalizes this first kind beyond intention: "the technological deck has been stacked long in advance to favor certain social interests" (pp. 125–126), and technological innovations "are similar to legislative acts or political foundings that establish a framework for public order that will endure over many generations" (p. 128). The second kind is "inherently political technologies, man-made systems that appear to require, or to be strongly compatible with, particular kinds of political relationships" (p. 123; section from p. 128). Its source is Engels's "On Authority": the cotton mill, the railway and the ship at sea "require the subordination of workers to an 'imperious authority'" arising "independently of all social organization" (p. 129), and Plato's ship that cannot be run democratically. Winner then sorts these claims on two axes (p. 130): "One version claims that the adoption of a given technical system actually requires the creation and maintenance of a particular set of social conditions as the operating environment of that system" — the contemporary form is "if you accept nuclear power plants, you also accept a techno-scientific-industrial-military elite. Without these people in charge, you could not have nuclear power" — while "a second, somewhat weaker, version ... holds that a given kind of technology is strongly compatible with, but does not strictly require, social and political relationships of a particular stripe" (solar energy and decentralization). "The meaning of 'required' here is that of practical (rather than logical) necessity" (p. 130). Crossing this is a second distinction between conditions "internal to the workings of a given technical system and those that are external to it" (p. 130). The atom bomb is the clean case: "As long as it exists at all, its lethal properties demand that it be controlled by a centralized, rigidly hierarchical chain of command ... The internal social system of the bomb must be authoritarian; there is no other way" (p. 131). Chandler's railroads "demanded the creation of the first administrative hierarchies in American business" (p. 132), though Winner grants the empirical question is open (Yugoslav self-management, Swedish teams, p. 132) and that "in a world in which human beings make and maintain artificial systems, nothing is 'required' in an absolute sense" (p. 134). The conclusion is "both/and": the first kind of politics is flexible and must be traced to the actors who chose the design; in the second "the initial choice about whether or not to adopt something is decisive in regard to its consequences" (p. 134), and "a ship at sea may well require ... a single captain and obedient crew. But a ship out of service, parked at the dock, needs only a caretaker" (p. 135).

## Relation to the argument

RQ6 turns on claim (b), and the text supports the pass's instinct that (b) is the better fit but shows where the generalization from object to form breaks. What fits: Winner's "required" is *practical* necessity for the system to operate at all — the same modality as "remove the intermediary and the coordination collapses." His internal/external distinction maps cleanly: a Φ>0 triad is an *internal* requirement (the authority structure is inside the coordination form), which is the strong cell of his 2×2 (Joerges reads the same cell as "inherently powerful artifacts, as in nuclear energy"). And his "ship at the dock" line already says the requirement is indexed to operation, not to the object — a parked ship is not political — which is a hair's breadth from saying politics attaches to the *running form* rather than the artifact. What breaks: (1) Winner's requirement is for a *distribution of authority among humans* (captain over crew, elite over public); in the triad the authority required is the *intermediary's own*, and the humans are symmetrically subordinate to it. That is not Engels's imperious authority of a person over persons; it is closer to his "authority of the steam" — an authority of the mechanism — which Winner quotes but does not develop. (2) Winner's examples are technologies that require a *hierarchy* (centralized command); the triad requires a *hub*, and a hub is not a hierarchy — both humans may be equals who simply cannot reach each other. Nothing in Winner distinguishes "must be run by a chain of command" from "must pass through a single point." (3) Winner offers no measure and admits (p. 132, p. 134) that whether a system "requires" anything is an unsettled empirical judgment; Φ is a candidate for exactly the thing his argument lacks, a test of whether the coordination factors without the intermediary. That is the paper's opening, not a weakness to hide. CUTS AGAINST the tightened definition in one place: Winner's own summary of (b) is that "the initial choice about whether or not to adopt something is decisive" — a yes/no on adoption — whereas the construct's competence assumes the person has already been placed inside the form and must act within it. Winner has no competence claim at all; the person's only political move is the adoption decision, which in the platform case is not theirs.

## Caution

The bridges case is contested on the facts (see joerges1999.md, woolgarcooper1999.md); this card does not rely on it and the paper should not either. Winner's claim (b) rests on Engels, Plato and Chandler, i.e. on a reading of secondary historians, and he concedes the empirical question is open. The essay has no model of two humans coordinating; "politics" is the polity or the workplace hierarchy. The 1986 reprint is what Suchman and most CSCW authors cite (p. 19 for the epigraph); pagination here is *Daedalus*. Winner (1977) *Autonomous Technology* and (1993) "Upon opening the black box" were not read for this pass; Joerges (1999) reports the 1993 paper's stance toward constructivism and Winner's own remark that "I am not interested in theories, I am interested in moral issues."
