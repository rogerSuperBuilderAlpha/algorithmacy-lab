# Manders-Huits, N. (2011). What values in design? The challenge of incorporating moral values into design. *Science and Engineering Ethics*, 17(2), 271–287.

## What it argues

VSD is "the prime candidate for implementing normative considerations into design" and it fails on five counts (Abstract): no clear method for identifying stakeholders; an obscure integration of empirical with conceptual work; a risk of the naturalistic fallacy; an undetermined concept of value; and no ethical theory for trade-offs. The sharpest passages are on the last two. On values: "it seems VSD offers no methodological account for distinguishing genuine moral values from mere preferences, wishes and whims of those involved in the design process" (What are Values?), and, if the descriptive/normative distinction is not drawn, "any value serving the particular interests of designers (including the initiators and potential customers) may become a serious threat for VSD." On trade-offs: "VSD cannot be taken as a normative account in which competing values can be balanced and traded off as per a certain principle ... who makes the final decision on how to prioritize these competing values? Is this left up to the majority of stakeholders to decide ... or is it left up to the designers?" (In Need of Ethical Theory). Her running example is an opaque online decision-support system for aneurysm patients who "are unaware of the available alternatives," and among the value questions she lists for it is bias: "is the system not secretly a system produced or sponsored by a pharmaceutical company favouring one of their products?" On stakeholders she notes that complex systems make even direct stakeholders hard to find, "let alone identifying the indirect stakeholders," and that stakeholders "may err or have mistaken beliefs about factual and normative issues concerning the technology." The proposal, "Value Conscious Design," adds an explicit ethical theory, explicit value aims, a stated choice of "whose values are to be considered (which stakeholders)," and a "values advocate" on the team who identifies "choices for including certain stakeholders and leaving out others" and explicates "value conflicts and trade-offs." Her closing line: "Taking values into account is not the same as normative reasoning."

## Relation to the argument

The clearest statement in the cluster that VSD cannot, on its own terms, adjudicate a cooptation case: it can register that the operator values extraction and the mediated parties value something else, but has no principle for saying whose value wins, and the paper names "initiators and potential customers" as the parties whose interests most threaten the method. The pharmaceutical-sponsor question is the objective axis stated as a VSD design question — the system pursues an end its users did not set — and it shows the axis is visible from inside VSD once one asks it, though VSD gives no instrument for detecting it. The stakeholder critique bears on the inversion in RQ5: if identifying indirect stakeholders is already unreliable for complex systems, a counterpart who is "indirect" from the interface's view and co-determined from the structural view will be missed unless something other than VSD's stakeholder analysis finds them, which is the work Φ is meant to do. For the process/product question the "values advocate" is a process role, and it complements rather than rivals interface affordances.

## Caution

A conceptual paper from 2011 with no cases, no design work and no empirical material; its targets are the 2003–2006 VSD texts, before Friedman and Hendry (2019) and before VSD's turn to AI. The naturalistic-fallacy charge is contested — Borning and Muller (2012) reply that qualified prescriptions ("if we want to support human flourishing, then ...") avoid it — and the demand for a single explicit ethical theory is itself a contested position in ethics of technology. The alternative, Value Conscious Design, is a sketch, not a method.
