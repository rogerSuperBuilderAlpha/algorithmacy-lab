## What it argues

The premise is opacity of change: "when and how these models are updated over time is opaque" (Abstract); "A LLM like GPT-4 can be updated over time based on data and feedback from users as well as design changes. However, it is currently opaque when and how GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 are updated, and it is unclear how each update affects the behavior of these LLMs" (§1, p. 1). The consequence is stated as an integration problem: "if LLM's response to a prompt (e.g. its accuracy or formatting) suddenly changes, this might break the downstream pipeline. It also makes it challenging, if not impossible, to reproduce results from the 'same' LLM" (§1, p. 1). The evidence is a comparison of the March 2023 and June 2023 versions of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on seven task families. Headline: "GPT-4 (March 2023) was reasonable at identifying prime vs. composite numbers (84% accuracy) but GPT-4 (June 2023) was poor on these same questions (51% accuracy). This is partly explained by a drop in GPT-4's amenity to follow chain-of-thought prompting. Interestingly, GPT-3.5 was much better in June than in March in this task" (Abstract). GPT-4's rate of answering sensitive questions fell from 21.0% to 5.0% while GPT-3.5's rose from 2.0% to 8.0% (§3.3). In code generation, "over 50% generations of GPT-4 were directly executable in March, but only 10% in June," because "the June version added ```python and ``` before and after the code snippet ... While a small change, the extra triple quotes render the code not executable. This type of shift in formatting behavior can be particularly challenging to detect when LLM's generated code is used inside a larger software pipeline" (§3.5, p. 11); after stripping the non-code text, correctness had in fact risen from 52% to 70%. The authors' proposed common factor is instruction following: "GPT-4 in March was typically good at following user's instructions (e.g. generating responses following specified formats), but in June it failed to follow most of these simple instructions" (§1, p. 1). The conclusion: "the behavior of the 'same' LLM service can change substantially in a relatively short amount of time, highlighting the need for continuous monitoring of LLMs" (Abstract).

## Relation to the argument

RQ10. This is the empirical record of the problem Principle III's "hard guarantees against drift" answers, and its details sharpen the principle in three ways. First, the drift the authors document is drift in *behaviour* — format, willingness, instruction-following — more than in competence: the code got more correct and less executable at once. That is exactly why Principle III asks for bounded outputs rather than better models; a model can improve on the benchmark and break the coordination. Second, the harm mechanism is downstream: a party who built on the March behaviour is bound by the June behaviour without notice, which is the temporal form of "commits determinations that bind both" — the mediator's determinations change under the parties' feet, and neither set the change. Third, the authors' own remedy is a subject-locus remedy — "continuous monitoring" by the user — and the paper should say so: it is Bainbridge's impossible task handed to the application developer, and it presupposes the resources (a released prompt set, an evaluation harness) that the coordinated lay party does not have. Principle III relocates the remedy from the monitoring subject to the relation: the operator commits to an envelope and to notice when it changes. Leveson's (2011) frame explains why monitoring is the weaker remedy — it is an active control, dependent on detection and diagnosis, where a declared envelope is a passive one. The contrast with Rudin (2019) is also clean here: an interpretable model would have been interpretable in March and in June, and interpretability of a version says nothing about the operator's freedom to replace it. Drift is a property of the *service*, and that is why Principle III is about outputs over time rather than internals at an instant. CUTS AGAINST: the study is dyadic — a developer and a service — and its "user" is a programmer with an evaluation pipeline, not a lay party coordinating with another human through the model. Nothing in it concerns a second party, and the models' updates were the operator's improvements, not an objective adverse to the user.

## Caution

Two snapshots of two services over three months, with n = 1,000 for the prime task and 50 for code, queried "via the user prompt only" at temperature 0.1; the authors say the aim "is not to provide a holistic assessment but to demonstrate that substantial ChatGPT performance drift exists." The mechanism (instruction following) is a hypothesis with "a first step towards testing." The arXiv v3 text is what was read; the HDSR version may have changed wording or numbers, and no HDSR page numbers exist for the quotations. "Drift" here means change between released versions, not the statistical drift of a fixed model's inputs.
