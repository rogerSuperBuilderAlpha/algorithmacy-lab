# Oeldorf-Hirsch, A., & Neubaum, G. (2025). What do we know about algorithmic literacy? The status quo and a research agenda for a growing field. *New Media & Society*, 27(2), 681–701.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/14614448231182662 (first published online 1 July 2023; issue 27(2), 2025) · **Read depth:** full_text (the Sage version of record, obtained from the University of Duisburg-Essen repository, duepublico2.uni-due.de; page numbers are the journal's) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed communication journal; a narrative review ("96 articles that were deemed potentially relevant, 50 of which are included," p. 683) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology ("the human and the algorithm," p. 692); social-media filtering algorithms are the object throughout · **Remedy locus:** subject (literacy), with an explicit agenda item on shifting part of the burden to developers' transparency · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18 (upgrades oeldorfhirsch2025know.md in the coordinative_sovereignty library from abstract to full text)

## What it argues

The field "is marked by great diversity in terms of how it theorizes and measures our understanding of algorithms, due, in part, to the opaque 'black box' nature of the algorithms themselves" (abstract). Two definitions anchor it. DeVito's: "the capacity and opportunity to be aware of both the presence and impact of algorithmically-driven systems on self- or collaboratively-identified goals, and the capacity and opportunity to crystalize this understanding into a strategic use of these systems to accomplish said goals" (DeVito 2021: 3, quoted p. 683). Dogruel et al.'s: "being aware of the use of algorithms in online applications, platforms, and services, knowing how algorithms work, being able to critically evaluate algorithmic decision-making as well as having the skills to cope with or even influence algorithmic operations" (Dogruel et al. 2021: 4, quoted p. 684). The lineage runs from media literacy (Livingstone 2004) through awareness (Hargittai et al. 2020), knowledge (Cotter and Reisdorf 2020) and skill (Klawitter and Hargittai 2018) to cognitive/affective/behavioural frameworks (Swart; Lomborg and Kapsch's "knowing, feeling, and doing algorithms," p. 684). Users reach a ceiling: "even those actively invested in understanding algorithms can only glean so much from their interactions with them"; influencers "play the visibility game" without "definitive information"; "users may be reaching the limits of what they can know without greater algorithmic transparency, and are facing the consequences" (pp. 686–687). Folk theories are "not false beliefs, but the best understanding that users can develop" (p. 687). Measurement: "Dogruel et al.'s (2021) algorithmic literacy scale is currently the most comprehensive attempt to measure literacy, capturing the dimensions of algorithmic awareness and algorithmic knowledge," though it "breaks into two sub-scales that together do not measure algorithmic literacy as one cohesive construct" (p. 691). "Currently, measures of algorithmic literacy lie mostly in the cognitive dimension..., stopping at awareness or knowledge of algorithms" (p. 695); "Knowledge about algorithms is not strongly correlated with positive attitudes..., and increasing this knowledge does little to change these attitudes" (p. 695). The agenda's first item: "the omnipresence of algorithms in social media users' daily lives requires communication scholars to consider the relationship between users and algorithm creators, particularly in terms of the responsibility that each has in promoting literacy"; "Much of the focus this far has been on the literacy needs of social media users, yet there is also pressure on app developers for algorithmic transparency" (p. 692). The behavioural facet is normatively open: "is it the user feeding the algorithm with more information so that it becomes more accurate or the user refraining from disclosing information to protect their privacy that is more literate?" (p. 695).

## Relation to the argument

RQ11: the field's own map, and the card through which the paper should distinguish "algorithmic literacy" from "algorithmacy." Three things follow from the text. First, every definition the review collects takes the algorithm as the object and the individual's goals as the measure — "strategic use of these systems to accomplish said goals," "skills to cope with or even influence algorithmic operations" — and none contains a second person whom the same operation binds; the paper can quote both definitions and show the missing term. Second, the review's own diagnosis of the ceiling — users "can only glean so much," and "may be reaching the limits of what they can know without greater algorithmic transparency" — is the literacy paradigm conceding that a competence in the subject runs out where the intermediary's opacity begins, which is the paper's reason for Principle I. Third, the agenda item on "the relationship between users and algorithm creators" is the review's one gesture toward the relation as the locus, and it stops at transparency: developers should disclose, users should learn. The construct's move — an agent in the relation — is not on the agenda, and the paper can say so with the review's own words. CUTS AGAINST the paper's novelty claim in that "influence algorithmic operations" (Dogruel) and DeVito's "strategic use" already name a behavioural, tactical component; the paper's distinct content is not tactics but the co-determined counterpart and the contestation of a *binding* determination, which no reviewed definition includes.

## Caution

A narrative review of social-media filtering research, English-language, searched through 2022; it excludes labour platforms, matching, pricing and any two-sided setting, and it says so ("our focus is primarily on social media content filtering algorithms," p. 683). Claims about specific scales and studies are the reviewers' summaries; the Dogruel definition is quoted at second hand here and the scale itself was not opened (see dogruel2022.md). The agenda is proposed, not tested.
