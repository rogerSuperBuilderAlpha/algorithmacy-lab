## What it argues

"The most profound technologies are those that disappear. They weave themselves into the fabric of everyday life until they are indistinguishable from it." Writing is the model — "literacy technology" whose "constant background presence ... does not require active attention, but the information to be conveyed is ready for use at a glance." Personal computers fail this: "the computer remains largely in a world of its own ... approachable only through complex jargon." PARC's proposal is to let "the computers themselves ... vanish into the background," and "such a disappearance is a fundamental consequence not of technology, but of human psychology. Whenever people learn something sufficiently well, they cease to be aware of it" — Simon's "compiling," Polanyi's "tacit dimension," Gadamer and Heidegger's "horizon" and "ready-to-hand," Seely Brown's "periphery." "The idea of integrating computers seamlessly into the world at large runs counter to a number of present-day trends" — laptops, multimedia, and above all virtual reality, which "focuses an enormous apparatus on simulating the world rather than on invisibly enhancing the world that already exists." Electric motors are the precedent: once one engine per factory, now dozens per car, unnoticed. The paper then describes tabs, pads and boards, PARC's prototypes, and a scenario of a knowledge worker's day in which the environment answers to her without her attending to it.

## Relation to the argument

Weiser matters for this pass only as the origin of the disappearance ideal that seamful design (Chalmers and Galani; Inman and Ribes; Ehsan et al.) reacts against, and the text is worth having because the lineage is usually cited loosely. Two facts to hold. The word "seamlessly" appears once, as an ideal; there is no discussion of seams as a design resource and the phrase "beautiful seams" is not in this paper (Inman and Ribes' title and Chalmers's "literally visible, effectively invisible" both draw on Weiser's later talks). And Weiser's model of the disappearing technology is *literacy*: writing that "does not require active attention." That is the construct's own contrast term. The Φ essay's literacy pole — a channel the parties do not need to attend to because it factors out — is Weiser's design ideal exactly; the argument of this paper is that the ideal is right for channels and wrong for parties, because a party that has "disappeared" into the periphery is a party whose determinations go uncontested. Weiser's own justification for disappearance — "whenever people learn something sufficiently well, they cease to be aware of it" — assumes the thing learned is stable. A mediator that "changes" (the last clause of the construct's definition) cannot be learned to the point of disappearance, which is the human-factors reason the seamful critics give and which Weiser's psychology of tacit skill does not anticipate. CUTS AGAINST: nothing directly; the paper simply has no two-human case and no notion of a system with interests. It is the wrong target for a critique of platforms and the right anchor for the claim that "disappearance" is a literacy-era ideal.

## Caution

Read in a pre-publication draft, not the magazine text; do not quote page numbers. The paper is a vision statement with no evaluation. Attributions of "beautiful seams" or "seamful" to Weiser should cite his 1994 UIST talk or Weiser's "open house" writings, neither of which this pass located; Inman and Ribes (2019) presumably give the source and should be checked (see inmanribes2019.md).
