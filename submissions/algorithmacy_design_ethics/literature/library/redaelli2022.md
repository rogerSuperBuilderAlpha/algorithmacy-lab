# Redaelli, R. (2022). Composite intentionality and responsibility for an ethics of artificial intelligence. *Scenari*, 17: 159–176.

**Identifier:** doi:10.7413/24208914133 · ISSN 2785-3020 · **Read depth:** full_text (open access, CC-BY 4.0, mimesisjournals.com) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed Italian philosophy journal (Mimesis), open access; modest venue · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology, extended to human–AI–(other humans, machines) in §4 · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Redaelli takes Verbeek's composite intentionality and applies it to AI systems with "operational autonomy." He reconstructs Verbeek's reading of Latour and Ihde from *What Things Do*: Latour showed that "technologies themselves coshape the interactions" (Verbeek 2005, p. 103, as quoted) but "from an outside perspective," while postphenomenology "contributes ... the situated perspective, the perspective 'from inside out'" (Verbeek 2005, p. 168, as quoted, p. 160). He quotes the definition from *Moralizing Technology*: "when this 'directedness' of technological devices is added to human intentionality, a composite intentionality comes about: a form of intentionality that results from adding technological intentionality to human intentionality" (Verbeek 2011, p. 145, as quoted, p. 163), and technological intentionality "understood as a directedness (Verbeek 2008, p. 392)" (p. 165). His claim is that in AI "composite intentionality is revealed as a result, not merely a summation, of the human-machine association" (p. 164), because the machine adapts its behaviour and "human intentionality is directed at the technological intentionality of the machine, which represents and constitutes the real ... by virtue of its own relevance" (p. 165, citing Verbeek 2011, p. 146). Section 4 proposes "composite responsibility" with three requirements: account for all actors including "individuals, organizations, natural entities and technology"; adopt Gunkel's communitarian rather than individualist frame; and include "the machine-to-machine relationship," since "machine-to-machine interactions can on their own cause unwanted indirect effects with dramatic repercussions on users" (pp. 167–168). He uses COMPAS as the case for algorithmic bias (pp. 165, 169) and Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven's meaningful human control as the rival proposal (p. 167). He closes by conceding Coeckelbergh's point that Verbeek's phrase "morality of things" invites the misreadings he then has to correct (p. 170).

## Relation to the argument

RQ1, objective axis: Redaelli is the nearest published attempt to give the mediator "its own relevance" inside postphenomenology, and it supplies page-located quotations from Verbeek 2011 and 2008 that I could not otherwise verify (see the verbeek2011 and verbeek2008 cards, both secondary through this source). His "result, not merely a summation" (p. 164) is a non-additivity claim about the human–AI association, which is what Φ > 0 formalizes, stated without a measure. The card cuts against the construct in one respect: even when the AI adapts on its own, Redaelli keeps intentionality "directed at" the machine's directedness, a one-human schema, and his third requirement (machine-to-machine) adds a second machine rather than a second human. The composite he reaches is human–AI–AI, not human–AI–human. For RQ2 the source has nothing on disclosure.

## Caution

A conceptual paper in a small venue, with no cases beyond COMPAS and self-driving collisions cited at second hand. Its value here is largely as a carrier of Verbeek quotations with page numbers; S2 should re-verify those against the books, since I could not. The English is a translation and some sentences are loose ("the man who designs the machine"). Nothing in it distinguishes an AI serving its user from one serving a third party, so the objective axis is present only as "operational autonomy," not as cooptation.
