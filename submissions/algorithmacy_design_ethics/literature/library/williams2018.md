## What it argues

The preface states the thesis: digital technologies "have largely sought to grab and keep our *at*tention" rather than "supporting our *in*tentions," and "We therefore have an obligation to rewire this system of intelligent, adversarial persuasion before it rewires us" (pp. xi–xii). Chapter 4 concedes the whole of the self-regulation problem before rejecting it as a solution: "The new challenges we face in the Age of Attention are, on both individual and collective levels, challenges of self-regulation" (p. 20); secular modernity dismantled inherited "packages of constraints," so we must "bring your own boundaries where you didn't have to before" (pp. 21–22); users live on a "treadmill of incompetence," "familiar enough with their technologies to operate them, but never so fully in control that they can prevent the technologies from operating on them in unexpected or undesirable ways" (p. 23); and "in the absence of environments that reward self-control or provide effective commitment devices, we're left to our own devices" (p. 24). The chapter's last line puts the burden back on design: "far from helping us mitigate these challenges of self-regulation, our technologies have largely been amplifying them" (p. 24).

Chapter 10 names the political frame. The attention economy is "attentional serfdom" (p. 89) and "the ground of first political struggle, the politics behind politics" (p. 89). One passage bears directly on this arm: "the more that the vision of computing as intelligent, frictionless assistance becomes reality, the more the logic and values of the system will be pushed below the surface of awareness to the automation layer and rendered obscure to users, or to any others who might want to question their design" (p. 90). The chapter closes on the remedy's location: "responding in the right way means treating the design of digital technologies as the ground of first struggle for our freedom and self-determination" and "changing the system so that these technologies are, as they already claim to be, on our side" (p. 95).

Chapter 11 lists what must *not* be done, and each item is a subject-side remedy. "We must not reply that if someone doesn't like the choices on technology's menu, their only option is to 'unplug' or 'detox'" (p. 100). "We must reject the impulse to ask users to 'just adapt' to distraction: to bear the burdens of impossible self-regulation … 'Thousands of the world's brightest psychologists, statisticians, and designers are now spending the majority of their waking lives figuring out how to tear down your willpower – so you just need to have more willpower'" (p. 101). "education is necessary – but not sufficient … Nor will 'media literacy' alone lead us out of this forest" (p. 101). "We also can't expect companies to self-regulate," and, in the sentence that meets Berthon head-on, "we must not put any stock whatsoever in the notion that advancing 'mindfulness' among employees in the technology industry is in any way relevant to or supportive of reforming the dynamics of the digital attention economy" (p. 101), a hope he calls "as dangerous as it is futile" (p. 102). Voluntary reform and counter-technologies are "bandages," "not the surgery, the sustainable systemic change, that is ultimately needed" (p. 104).

Chapter 12 gives the positive program. "There are no maps here, only compasses" (p. 106); the goal is "to bring the technologies of our attention onto our side" (p. 106), and the platforms owe users "at absolute minimum, the benefit of competence" (p. 107), his term for a protected "minimum viable mind." Four intervention types (p. 108): rethinking advertising, conceptual and linguistic reengineering, changing the upstream determinants of design, and mechanisms for accountability, transparency, and measurement. Under these: advertising should move "away from attention and towards intention" (p. 111); ad blocking should ship by default, "Aggressive computational persuasion should be opt-in, not opt-out" (p. 112); a two-axis typology of persuasive technologies by constraint and goal-alignment (p. 113); benefit-corporation structures, investor incentives, and a user's choice to pay "with their money or with their attention" (pp. 114–115); regulation requiring "transparency of persuasive design goals," measured "attentional harm," and "attention offsets" (p. 116); the user's questions "What is Facebook's persuasive goal for me? On what basis does YouTube suggest that I watch one video and not another?" with "trust, but verify" (p. 117); a draft Designer's Oath (p. 120); and measurement of "what we value, rather than valuing what we already measure" (p. 121). The section ends on the mechanism he ranks first: "none of these interventions … is enough to create deep, lasting change in the absence of new mechanisms to make users' voices heard in the design process," a "labor union" for attentional labour, and "user representation in the design process. Token inclusion is insufficient: users need to have a real say in the design, and real power to effect change" (p. 123).

Chapter 13 closes: "Today, the right sort of redesign is not yet in fashion" (p. 127), and "the degree to which we are able and willing to struggle for ownership of our attention is the degree to which we are free" (p. 128).

## Relation to the argument

RQ5 flagged uncertainty over whether Williams is a companion to Berthon (subject-side remedy) or a contrast (design and regulation). He is a contrast, and an unusually explicit one. He grants Berthon's diagnosis — chapter 4 concedes that the age of attention is a self-regulation problem — and then refuses every subject-side remedy by name: detox, willpower, adaptation, media literacy, and firm-promoted employee mindfulness (pp. 100–102). His remedies are artifact-side (design defaults, persuasive-goal transparency, measurement) and upstream (corporate form, policy), with user representation in design as the capstone. Berthon's closing paragraph asks the user to cultivate wise mindfulness in order "to see how our minds are undermined by the technologies"; Williams's p. 101 sentence says that hope is futile. The draft can set the two side by side without paraphrase.

Where Williams stops short of this arm's construct is the party structure. His user faces a persuasion infrastructure; the relation he wants to repair is user–designer ("make users' voices heard in the design process," p. 123). The two people a mediator coordinates never appear, and his remedies are silent on what one user can contest when a system commits a determination that binds another. His p. 90 remark that the system's "logic and values" get "pushed below the surface of awareness to the automation layer and rendered obscure to users, or to any others who might want to question their design" is, however, the nearest statement in this library of why inference-from-behaviour, not legibility, is the competence a frictionless mediator demands. His "trust, but verify" list (p. 117) is a set of questions a user asks of a system's goals, which is algorithmacy's first clause in prototype; and "user representation in the design process" (p. 123) is a counter-delegation proposal, though aimed at the designer rather than lodged between the coordinated parties. Both can be cited as precursors of Principles I and II so long as the draft states what Williams did not model.

## Caution

Williams describes the book as "more exploration than argument" and "an unfolding of intuitions" (p. xii); it is a prize essay, not a study, and its policy proposals are gestures ("Any one could fill a whole book," p. 107) with no implementation detail. Five diagnostic chapters (5–9) were not read, so the harm taxonomy (spotlight, starlight, daylight) is cited here only from the table of contents. He is a former Google employee and says so; the book's authority on industry practice is testimonial. His refusal of "mindfulness" is aimed at mindfulness *among technology-industry employees* as a reform lever, which overlaps with but is not identical to Berthon's consumer-facing typology; the draft should quote both and not collapse them. He does not use the words "opt out" as a thesis; the nearest is his rejection of "unplug or detox" as the only option offered (p. 100), which is a refusal of that framing, not a claim that exit is impossible.
