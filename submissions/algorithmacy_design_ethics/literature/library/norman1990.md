# Norman, D. A. (1990). The 'problem' with automation: Inappropriate feedback and interaction, not 'over-automation'. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London B*, 327(1241), 585–593.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1098/rstb.1990.0101 · PMID 1970904 · **Read depth:** full_text of the author's preprint — ICS Report 8904 (July 1989), UCSD Institute for Cognitive Science, deposited at NASA NTRS as document 19900004678 and "prepared for the Discussion Meeting 'Human Factors in High-Risk Situations,' The Royal Society, June 28–29, 1989, to be published in the Philosophical Transactions"; page numbers below are the report's (1–8), not the journal's · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed proceedings of a Royal Society discussion meeting; essay with three accident case studies · **Evidence basis:** direct_read (preprint) · **Parties modeled:** human–technology–world, with the flight crew as a multi-person unit · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Automation "is at an intermediate level of intelligence, powerful enough to take over control that which used to be done by people, but not powerful enough to handle all abnormalities," and "its level of intelligence is insufficient to provide the continual, appropriate feedback that occurs naturally among human operators" (abstract, p. 1). Three aviation cases carry the argument: the 1985 China Airlines 747 whose autopilot silently compensated for a slow loss of engine power until it "could no longer keep the plane stable" and the aircraft fell 31,500 feet (p. 2); the 1979 Cape Cod commuter crash in which the first officer never noticed the captain was "incapacitated," included precisely because "no automation was involved" (pp. 2–3); and an ASRS fuel-leak report in which the autopilot "had quietly and efficiently compensated for the resulting weight imbalance" and only the second officer's remark about a discrepant fuel gauge alerted the crew (pp. 3–4). Two thought experiments then set the autopilot beside a first officer flying by hand: "as far as the captain is concerned, the control has been automated: by an autopilot in one situation and by the first officer in the other," but the first officer would say "I seem to be correcting this thing more and more — I wonder what's happening?" — "from the captain's point of view the rest of the crew serves as a type of automaton, but one that observes and remarks upon conditions" (p. 5). Hence "the culprit is not actually automation, but rather the lack of feedback" (p. 5), and the "new irony": "the problem is not that the automation is too powerful, the problem is that it is not powerful enough" (p. 7). Why do systems lack feedback? "The automation itself doesn't need it!" — the designer's task is done when the device functions (p. 7). The close asks for "a soft, compliant technology, not a rigid, formal one" (p. 8), and names the required capacity "a monitoring of its own monitoring abilities" (p. 7).

## Relation to the argument

Structural axis, and the classic source that comes closest to the construct's idea of an intermediary as a *party* — then defines the ideal party as one that reports. Norman's model has more than one human in it: a crew whose "informal chatter... tends to keep everyone informed of the complete state of the system" (p. 5, crediting Hutchins). But the second human is the crewmate, on the captain's side, and the argument's move is to make the crewmate the standard the automation should meet: an automaton "that observes and remarks upon conditions." That is a channel ideal, in the construct's terms — the good intermediary is one that makes the coordination factor by keeping everyone in the loop — and the paper never entertains an intermediary whose silence serves someone else. On this pass's question, then: a second human is present, and is modelled, but as a co-operator and as the template for good automation, never as a counterpart whose interests the automation also manages. The paper's account of why feedback is missing — the automation does not need it, and its designer stops when the device works — is nonetheless the earliest statement in this corpus of the objective-axis mechanism: what the intermediary reports is decided by what the intermediary's own task requires.

## Caution

Read from the 1989 preprint, whose text may differ from the 1990 published version in wording and pagination; journal page numbers are not available here and no quotation should be cited to pp. 585–593 without checking. An essay built on three cases and two thought experiments, with the author's own note that a NASA-Ames simulator replication of the fuel-leak case was informal (footnote 2, p. 6). The Hutchins reference is to a chapter then "in press," not to *Cognition in the Wild*.

## Relation to Principle III — bounded outputs, not mechanistic interpretability (cluster F pass, 2026-09-18)

**Remedy locus:** artifact (feedback the automation gives about what it is doing).

Norman's cases fix what kind of feedback Principle III asks for, and it is not a view inside. The China Airlines autopilot and the fuel-leak autopilot each did the right thing by their own lights and said nothing about *what state they had drifted into* — compensating harder and harder for a condition the crew could not see. The remedy Norman draws is the first officer who says "I seem to be correcting this thing more and more — I wonder what's happening?" (p. 5): a report of the automation's own operating regime and of the fact that it is nearing the edge of it, in the automation's own terms and at a rate the captain can follow. That is regime disclosure — Sarter and Woods's mode awareness, the ODD's "fallback" — and it is the operational sense of predictability, not the mechanistic one; no crew member wanted the autopilot's control law. Two of his diagnoses transfer directly. "The culprit is not actually automation, but rather the lack of feedback" (p. 5) is the argument that the problem Principle III addresses is a design choice, not a property of complexity; and "the automation itself doesn't need it!" (p. 7) — feedback is omitted because the designer's task ends when the device works — is the mechanism by which a mediator's silence about its regime is the default, and under cooptation a convenient one. His "new irony," that the automation "is not powerful enough" (p. 7), also reads differently in the triad than in the cockpit: a more powerful mediator that reports more would, on his account, be better, whereas Principle III wants a mediator that does *less* outside its envelope and says so. The paper should take the feedback requirement and leave the "more powerful" prescription. The original relation section's caution stands: his second human is a crewmate on the same side, and the ideal he draws from her is a channel ideal.
