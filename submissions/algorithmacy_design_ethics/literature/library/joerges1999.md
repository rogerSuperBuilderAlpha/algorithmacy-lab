# Joerges, B. (1999). Do politics have artefacts? *Social Studies of Science*, 29(3), 411–431.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/030631299029003004 · **Read depth:** full_text (author's accepted manuscript, EconStor hdl.handle.net/10419/71061, CC BY-NC-SA; unpaginated relative to the journal, so citations below are by section title; SAGE version of record paywalled) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed "Comment" in the field's flagship journal · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** n/a (artifact, designer, and the discourse that tells the story) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

The abstract states the thesis: the Moses low-bridges story "turns out to be counterfactual, but even if a small myth is disenchanted, it serves a purpose: to resituate positions in the old debate about the control of social processes via buildings and other technical artifacts." Joerges reconstructs the "Chinese Whispers" by which the story mutated (in a German journal Moses became an architect, the parkways Autobahnen, the discoverer Winner), and identifies the "strong, design-version" of artifacts-have-politics — "someone wills a specific social state, and then suitably transfers this intention into an artifact" — for which "the only immediately plausible empirical case Winner adduces ... are the low bridges." He notes that Winner separately grades "inherently political technologies" along "strict requirement versus strong compatibility" and "internal versus external" and that the strict-and-internal cell "produces inherently powerful artifacts, as in nuclear energy" (n. 4). The factual case ("How It Really Was"): Caro's account rests on two interviews (engineer Sidney Shapiro; planner Lee Koppelman's yardstick story, Caro pp. 318, 951), with "no other (and, especially, earlier) sources"; Columbia's Kenneth Jackson finds many Caro episodes "doubtful or tendentious"; Long Island had a railway since 1877 and "a rather dense system of ordinary roads ... parallel and across the parkways," so no *point de passage obligatoire* existed; and "the fact remains that blacks could gain physical access to Long Island beaches via many routes. And yet Jones Beach remained a white strand." US engineers gave two reasons for low bridges: "commercial traffic was excluded from the parkways anyway" and raising them would have cost too much; nationally "trucks, buses and other commercial vehicles were prohibited on all parkways," so "Moses could hardly have let buses on his parkways, even if he had wanted differently." Moses was "what one might call a 'structural racist'" who "never pursued explicitly racist schemes." The theoretical payoff: Winner's parable belongs to the "discourse of control" (Bentham's Panopticon as its archetype) against the "discourse of contingency" (Woolgar, the Tower of Babel); Joerges proposes a middle road treating built things as "boundary objects," "media of mediation, negotiation and translation between the reciprocal expectations and requirements of many people," and concludes "the power represented in built and other technical devices is not to be found in the formal attributes of these things themselves. Only their authorization, their legitimate representation, gives shape to the definitive effects they may have." "Built spaces always represent control rights. They belong to someone and not to others."

## Relation to the argument

For RQ6 this settles two things. First, the bridges case cannot carry weight — not because Woolgar is right that everything is text, but because on Joerges's reading of the primary sources the physical fact (low bridges) did not produce the social effect (exclusion), since alternatives existed: the direct channel to the beach survived. That is, in the construct's own terms, a *channel* misread as a *party* — the bridges were bypassable, so the coordination factored. The Moses story is a cautionary tale for exactly the mistake Φ is meant to catch. Second, Joerges leaves Winner's claim (b) alone: his target is the design-version, and he explicitly notes the requirement/compatibility grid as a separate, more graded argument (n. 4) without contesting it. The construct's use of (b) is therefore not exposed to this critique. What Joerges adds beyond clearing the ground is his closing formula, which is a structural-relational account of technical power avant la lettre: things "represent control rights," and the effect comes from "authorization" — who may use the thing and who may not — rather than from form. Transposed to the triad, that says the intermediary's power lies not in its code but in the fact that it is the *authorized* route and the parties are not authorized to route around it; the pass should note that this is a *legal/institutional* reading of why the direct channel is absent, which complements rather than competes with the Φ reading (the channel is missing because it is forbidden, or because it was never built, and Φ does not distinguish these). CUTS AGAINST: Joerges's "boundary object" alternative, following Star, makes the mediating thing a site of *negotiation* among many parties' expectations, which presupposes that the parties can reach and reinterpret it. A mediator that interprets both sides privately and commits determinations is not a boundary object in that sense; the construct should say so rather than borrow the term.

## Caution

The postprint is unpaginated; quotations are verbatim but journal page numbers could not be assigned. Joerges's "fieldwork" is correspondence with engineers and historians (Timothy Davis, Kenneth Jackson) and his son's inquiries; he admits "not having undertaken any primary surveys myself" (n. 37). Woolgar and Cooper (1999) respond in the same issue that the argument itself is a "Chinese whisper" and that a student had ridden buses over the bridges — which Joerges does not report and which, if true, strengthens his conclusion while embarrassing his method. Joerges replied in "Scams cannot be busted" (SSS 29(3): 450–457), not read.
