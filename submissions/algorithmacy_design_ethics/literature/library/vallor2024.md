## What it argues

The chapters are: Introduction; 1 The AI Mirror; 2 Minds, Machines, and Gods; 3 Through the Looking Glass; 4 The Thoughts the Civilized Keep; 5 The Empathy Box; 6 AI and the Bootstrapping Problem; 7 In a Mirror, Brightly (per the search index and Risse). The governing claim is that generative models, which "produce novel outputs of the same general kind as their training data" (p. 19, per Hudson), are mirrors of the human record rather than minds: AI "merely projects complex reflections cast by our recorded thoughts, judgments, desires, needs, perceptions, expectations, and imaginings" (p. 2, per Bate and Lloyd). On automation of judgement, three chapters bear. Chapter 4 argues from Sellars that one cannot negotiate with an opaque system in "the space of reasons" (p. 106, per Hudson), and that "opaque AI decision systems are highly attractive tools for those in power [offering] a virtually bulletproof accountability shield" (p. 119, per Hudson); the Asimov "Multivac" case (p. 112) makes democratic deliberation moot. Chapters 3–4, per Bate and Lloyd, warn that as AI takes decision authority in healthcare, policing and governance "it begins to distort moral deliberation by simulating reasoning rather than genuinely engaging in it," and that "the danger ... is a growing risk of moral deskilling, where we increasingly outsource ethical judgments to systems that are incapable of moral reasoning" (reviewers' words). Chapter 6's bootstrapping problem is that a backward-looking mirror cannot supply the moral growth needed to correct it: "Pursuing more goodness, guided only by the forms of goodness that we most readily recognize ... might be like trying to get out of a hole by continuing to dig" (p. 163, per LSE). The positive programme is "the exercise of technomoral wisdom" (p. 171, per Hudson) toward ends "from social fairness and justice, to privacy and autonomy, to the transparency and accountability of sociotechnical systems, to democratic health and the sustainability of the planet" (p. 170, per Hudson), and, per Bate and Lloyd, "a fundamental shift in AI development — one that prioritises contestability, pluralism, and ethical reflection over passive optimisation." Fernandez adds that chatbots "often apologize and capitulate when ... we insist they are wrong" and, in his own words, "in the absence of this friction and resistance to our own views of the world, narcissism grows relatively unchecked."

**On the [check] item.** I could not open the text, so whether the word "friction" appears in the book is unresolved. What is verified is that Vallor uses it in her own voice about the book's target, in the *Nautilus* interview: Silicon Valley's ideal is "achieving a situation where the problem is solved and there's no more friction, no more ambiguity, nothing left unsaid or undone, you've dominated the problem and it's gone," and "efficiency is never defined with any reference to any higher value." The reviewers who use "friction" (Fernandez, the Taylor & Francis article the search surfaced) use it in their own voices. Chapters 4 and 6 are where the discussion of automated judgement sits; whether either names friction is not known.

## Relation to the argument

The book extends the 2015 criterion to generative AI in a way that sharpens the reduction question. The space-of-reasons argument (ch. 4) is structural: an intermediary that cannot give reasons cannot be contested in the way the definition's "contest what it commits" requires, so contestation has to happen at Bainbridge's meta-level. The accountability-shield line (p. 119) is the objective axis stated as a political fact — opacity serves whoever deploys the system. But the bootstrapping problem (ch. 6) is the strongest statement in this cluster that the virtue criterion does not reduce to "whose objective": even an intermediary aligned with the user's objective and pursuing nothing of its own deskills, because it reflects only the goodness already in the record and so removes the friction by which the user's judgement would have grown past it. Fernandez's capitulating chatbot is the same point at interface scale — an intermediary optimised to agree is frictionless *and* serves the user's stated preference *and* deforms the user. That is the case the cooptation reading cannot see and Φ cannot measure. The reviewers' summary that Vallor asks AI to prioritise "contestability" over "passive optimisation" is, if accurate to the text, the closest she comes to the algorithmacy programme, and it should be verified first when the book is opened.

## Caution

Every quotation here is a quotation of a reviewer quoting the book; the page numbers are the reviewers' and must be checked against a copy before use, and the paraphrases marked "reviewers' words" (moral deskilling, contestability) must not be attributed to Vallor verbatim. Hudson calls the political chapters "anti-capitalist sentiments" and Risse dissents on machine consciousness, so the reviews are not neutral summaries. The book addresses generative AI as an epistemic and political object; it does not, on any reviewer's account, treat interface design, friction as a design variable, or the mediated triad. "Friction" is unverified in the text.
