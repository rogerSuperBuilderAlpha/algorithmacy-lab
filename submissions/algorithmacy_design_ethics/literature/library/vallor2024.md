## What it argues

The chapters are: Introduction; 1 The AI Mirror; 2 Minds, Machines, and Gods; 3 Through the Looking Glass; 4 The Thoughts the Civilized Keep; 5 The Empathy Box; 6 AI and the Bootstrapping Problem; 7 In a Mirror, Brightly (per the search index and Risse). The governing claim is that generative models, which "produce novel outputs of the same general kind as their training data" (p. 19, per Hudson), are mirrors of the human record rather than minds: AI "merely projects complex reflections cast by our recorded thoughts, judgments, desires, needs, perceptions, expectations, and imaginings" (p. 2, per Bate and Lloyd). On automation of judgement, three chapters bear. Chapter 4 argues from Sellars that one cannot negotiate with an opaque system in "the space of reasons" (p. 106, per Hudson), and that "opaque AI decision systems are highly attractive tools for those in power [offering] a virtually bulletproof accountability shield" (p. 119, per Hudson); the Asimov "Multivac" case (p. 112) makes democratic deliberation moot, partly because Multivac's "speed ... exceed[s] the grasp of human thought by magnitudes" and "its system architecture and internal logic are not isomorphic to human reasoning" (snippet, p. 113), a mismatch the book states generally as "the speed of machine and human cognition, and the inherently uninterpretable and unpredictable behavior of many machine learning processes" (snippet, p. 105). Chapter 6's bootstrapping problem is that a backward-looking mirror cannot supply the moral growth needed to correct it: "Pursuing more goodness, guided only by the forms of goodness that we most readily recognize ... might be like trying to get out of a hole by continuing to dig" (p. 163, per LSE). The positive programme is "the exercise of technomoral wisdom" (p. 171, per Hudson) toward ends "from social fairness and justice, to privacy and autonomy, to the transparency and accountability of sociotechnical systems, to democratic health and the sustainability of the planet" (p. 170, per Hudson).

**Resolved on the cluster B pass: reverse adaptation.** Chapter 3 imports Winner. The passage at p. 88 reads, in the snippets: "... Winner in 1977 called 'reverse adaptation.'21 Here, instead of machines being designed to support human ends, humans are enlisted to do whatever is necessary to augment and adapt to the machines' abilities. In this way humans are rewarded for behaving more and more like robots. It is what happens whenever AI tools instruct Amazon warehouse workers to bend their bodies at the 'optimal' angle for box-picking speed, ignoring the human worker's unique embodied situation (the sore right shoulder or the tricky knee) and demanding that they move only as the ..." (snippets, p. 88). Note 21 is "Winner (1977), 226. See also Sullins (2013)" (snippet, p. 233), which is the page on which Winner's section "Reverse Adaptation" begins (see winner1977.md). She continues: "reverse adaptation to AI explains why our lives will be worse if we don't alter the current trajectory of AI development. But the examples above don't capture the full sadness of such a failure of humanity's most meaningful existential task: what Ortega called our autofabrication" (snippet, p. 90). The index gives "reverse adaptation, 88–91" (snippet, p. 255). Winner recurs at p. 199 ("technological innovation and political oligarchy have emerged as fraternal twins," quoted as Winner's) and p. 216 ("technological somnambulism," where we "willingly sleepwalk through the process of reconstituting the conditions of human existence"), both snippets.

As well-articulated by feminist philosophers and care ethicists, such as Virginia Held and Joan Tronto, the compassionate virtues of care and service are ..." (snippet, p. 189). "Slow" occurs once in the body, at p. 189: "But AI isn't wisdom, it's just another tool for it. These tools don't know what we need, or who we are, or what we can do together, or why we should. Fernandez's "friction" (the capitulating chatbot) remains the reviewer's word.

## Relation to the argument

The book extends the 2015 criterion to generative AI in a way that sharpens the reduction question. The space-of-reasons argument (ch. 4) is structural: an intermediary that cannot give reasons cannot be contested in the way the definition's "contest what it commits" requires, so contestation has to happen at Bainbridge's meta-level. The accountability-shield line (p. 119) is the objective axis stated as a political fact — opacity serves whoever deploys the system. But the bootstrapping problem (ch. 6) is the strongest statement in this cluster that the virtue criterion does not reduce to "whose objective": even an intermediary aligned with the user's objective and pursuing nothing of its own deskills, because it reflects only the goodness already in the record.

What the snippets add is the bridge the paper's tempo argument needs. Vallor herself now cites Winner (1977, p. 226) for the mechanism by which people adapt to a machine's cadence — the warehouse worker bending "at the 'optimal' angle for box-picking speed" (p. 88) is Winner's reservation clerk (winner1977.md, p. 205) fifty years on — and she calls the result reverse adaptation, not deskilling. So the paper's line from Winner's mechanism to Vallor's remedy runs through Vallor's own citation, and the objection the paper presses is one she has half-made: pp. 88–91 diagnose a tempo set by the machine, and pp. 170–171 answer with technomoral wisdom in the person. The friction pages (186–189) confirm the pattern from the other direction. "Frictionless ease" is named as the pathology, and the remedy is a revalorized set of virtues (repair, relinquishment, care), which is to say the subject again — while the resistance she wants to rehabilitate is, in her own words, currently privatized as "individual therapy, habits of mindfulness" (p. 188). Her political programme (p. 196: contestability, redress, rights enforcement) is the other locus, and it is the artifact/institution, not the relation: contestability of "AI decisions" (index) is the controller/subject dyad that Cluster D found in every contestability source. The parties on p. 88 are a worker and a tool; the customer whose order the pace serves is not on the page.

## Caution

Every quotation is either a reviewer's transcription or a forty-word Google Books snippet cut from a paragraph I have not seen; page numbers are reliable, wording inside the cut is not guaranteed, and the bracketed reconstructions ("exceed[s]") are mine. The pp. 88–91 passage is the one the paper would quote, and it must be read whole first: I do not know what "the examples above" (p. 90) are beyond the warehouse case, or whether Vallor endorses Winner's definition or adapts it. Hudson calls the political chapters "anti-capitalist sentiments" and Risse dissents on machine consciousness, so the reviews are not neutral summaries. The book addresses generative AI as an epistemic and political object; nothing found treats interface design, a mediated triad, or a third party interpreting two people.
