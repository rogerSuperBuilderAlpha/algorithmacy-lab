## What it argues

The book studies "the ways in which users — commonly assumed to be passive and guided by established rules — operate," and "the models of action characteristic of users whose status as the dominated element in society (a status that does not mean that they are either passive or docile) is concealed by the euphemistic term 'consumers.' Everyday life invents itself by poaching in countless ways on the property of others" (pp. xi–xii). Consumption is "another production," "devious ... dispersed ... it insinuates itself everywhere, silently and almost invisibly, because it does not manifest itself through its own products, but rather through its ways of using the products imposed by a dominant economic order" (pp. xii–xiii) — the Indians who "subverted [the colonizers' rituals] not by rejecting or altering them, but by using them with respect to ends and references foreign to the system they had no choice but to accept ... they escaped it without leaving it" (p. xiii). Against Foucault's *Discipline and Punish* the question is inverted: "what popular procedures (also 'miniscule' and quotidian) manipulate the mechanisms of discipline and conform to them only in order to evade them" (p. xiv), composing "the network of an antidiscipline" (p. xv). The key definitions (all p. xix): "I call a 'strategy' the calculus of force-relationships which becomes possible when a subject of will and power (a proprietor, an enterprise, a city, a scientific institution) can be isolated from an 'environment.' A strategy assumes a place that can be circumscribed as proper (propre) and thus serve as the basis for generating relations with an exterior distinct from it (competitors, adversaries, 'clienteles,' 'targets,' or 'objects' of research)." "I call a 'tactic,' on the other hand, a calculus which cannot count on a 'proper' (a spatial or institutional localization), nor thus on a borderline distinguishing the other as a visible totality. The place of a tactic belongs to the other. A tactic insinuates itself into the other's place, fragmentarily, without taking it over in its entirety, without being able to keep it at a distance. It has at its disposal no base where it can capitalize on its advantages ... The 'proper' is a victory of space over time. On the contrary, because it does not have a place, a tactic depends on time — it is always on the watch for opportunities that must be seized 'on the wing.' Whatever it wins, it does not keep." "The weak must continually turn to their own ends forces alien to them"; the synthesis "takes the form, however, not of a discourse, but of the decision itself, the act and manner in which the opportunity is 'seized'" (p. xix). On technical systems (pp. xxiii–xxiv): "the involvement of the subject diminishes in proportion to the technocratic expansion of these systems. Increasingly constrained, yet less and less concerned with these vast frameworks, the individual detaches himself from them without being able to escape them and can henceforth only try to outwit them, to pull tricks on them, to rediscover, within an electronicized and computerized megalopolis, the 'art' of the hunters and rural folk of earlier days." And a caution about uniformity of the weak (p. xvii): "Confronted by images on television, the immigrant worker does not have the same critical or creative elbow-room as the average citizen ... Similar strategic deployments, when acting on different relationships of force, do not produce identical effects."

## Relation to the argument

RQ8 asks whether algorithmacy is tactical in de Certeau's sense, and the introduction gives a two-part answer. Yes, in its situation: the co-opted party "cannot count on a proper," acts inside "the other's place," and "whatever it wins, it does not keep" — which is exactly why the construct's definition ends with "adapt as it changes," and why the platform's ability to re-issue its rules is not incidental to the competence but constitutive of its tactical character. The "electronicized and computerized megalopolis" passage is a 1980 description of the setting the paper addresses. No, in its form: de Certeau's tactic is "not ... a discourse, but ... the decision itself," opportunistic and unstockpiled, "unsigned, unreadable, and unsymbolized" (p. xvii); a *competence* that can be named, taught, and designed for is a stock, and a stock is the beginning of a proper. Turning tactics into an affordance risks converting them into strategy, which on his account is what destroys them. The construct's "interpretive, tactical and anticipatory" triad therefore mixes a de Certeau term with two that are not his — interpretation and anticipation presuppose a standing model of the adversary, which the tactician "on the wing" does not have. The paper should either own that hybrid or use "tactical" more narrowly. Two further points. De Certeau's schema is dyadic — consumer against producer, the weak against the strong; he has no place for a *second* weak party inside the same proper who is the first party's rival, so the triad's driver-against-customer structure is outside his model, and the paper should not cite him as if he covered it. And his warning that "similar strategic deployments, when acting on different relationships of force, do not produce identical effects" is the strongest available caution against a *uniform* competence: what a tactic yields depends on the user's "elbow-room," which is Cotter and Reisdorf's knowledge-gap finding stated in 1980.

## Caution

Only the General Introduction was read; chapter 3, which the brief named, was not, and any quotation attributed to it must be verified separately. Page numbers were assigned by S2 from the page markers of a full-text copy, not from a physical copy of the Rendall edition. De Certeau's examples are reading, walking, cooking, and colonial appropriation; there is no empirical study and no technical system beyond the closing aside. His "consumer" is not a coordinating party but a user of imposed products, and his "strategy" belongs to an institution, not to a mechanism.
