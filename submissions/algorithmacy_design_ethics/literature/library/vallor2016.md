## What it argues

The book has three parts (Huff): Part I argues for "a technomoral virtue ethic of global scope" (p. 64, per Huff); Part II reads the Aristotelian, Confucian and Buddhist traditions for a shared account of moral self-cultivation and then lists the virtues; Part III applies the framework to social media, surveillance, robotics and human enhancement. The seven elements of moral self-cultivation, quoted by Huff at p. 64, are "moral habituation, relational understanding, reflective self-examination, intentional self-direction of moral development, perceptual attention to moral salience, prudential judgment, and appropriate extension of moral concern." The twelve technomoral virtues, quoted by Huff at p. 120 and confirmed by Graeff and the Notre Dame notice, are honesty, self-control, humility, justice, courage, empathy, care, civility, flexibility, perspective, magnanimity, and technomoral wisdom — the last glossed by Graeff (his words, not necessarily Vallor's) as "the supreme virtue of applying our various cultivated virtues to good ends." The Notre Dame notice quotes p. 174: "Technologies are not stone tablets delivered from on high. They are malleable human..." (truncated in the source). Huff's principal criticism is that "global agreement on the content of the virtues ... is too remote a goal" and that technology has already fragmented the communities that could sustain shared exemplars.

## Relation to the argument

The book is where the 2015 deskilling argument is meant to become a positive account, and technomoral wisdom is the candidate criterion RQ3 names. What the reviews establish is only its shape: a list of virtues plus a list of practices, applied domain by domain. Whether the book supplies a *criterion* — a rule that sorts friction that forms from friction that taxes — or only a vocabulary for describing the difference after the fact, cannot be settled from reviews, and Huff's objection (no shared community to hold the standard) is the same objection MacIntyre's internal-goods clause raises for a structural account: the virtue judgement needs a formed judge. Two reviewer-supplied facts bear on the construct. "Relational understanding" and "perceptual attention to moral salience" are among the seven practices, which is the formation-side counterpart of the interpretive and anticipatory capabilities algorithmacy names; and the twelve virtues include flexibility and perspective, which read as the character-level versions of "adapt as it changes." None of this can be cited as Vallor's argument until the text is read; it is a map of where to look.
