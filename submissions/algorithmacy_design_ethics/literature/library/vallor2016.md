## What it argues

The structure the reviews reported is confirmed on the page. The seven "habits of moral self-cultivation" are laid out at p. 64 under the heading "Appropriate Extension of Moral Concern," where Vallor says "This framework is never formally articulated in Aristotelian, Confucian, or Buddhist ethics, much less all three" (snippet, p. 64), and they are worked through in ch. 4: reflective self-examination at pp. 84–90 ("what I need to examine is whether I have adequately attended to those to whom I am connected," snippet, p. 88), moral attention at pp. 99–105, appropriate extension of moral concern at pp. 110–116. Practical wisdom is then the integration: the three "perceptual and practical" habits "(moral attention, prudential judgment, and extension of moral concern), along with the foundational habits analyzed in chapters 3 and 4, enable what is called moral or practical wisdom" (snippet, p. 99); the practically wise person's "moral attention has become, as a result of habitual moral practice and considerable experience," such that she "will notice and respond to morally significant phenomena to which the rest of us will often be oblivious or indifferent" (snippet, p. 100). She concedes the traditions' gap: all three treat "moral attention as an essential capacity of the cultivated person, [but] none fully articulate how this perceptual capacity might develop and be habitually refined— that is, how it can best be cultivated" (snippet, p. 104). The temporal shape of cultivation is stated repeatedly — one must "slowly and painstakingly transform oneself into the sort of human being that one aspires to be" (snippet, p. 63); an "uncultivated person who tried to leap into a state of total mindfulness would fail instantly" (snippet, p. 89) — and its material preconditions are named: "Certain material and social deprivations or misfortunes can inhibit the attainment of these conditions" (snippet, p. 97, in a numbered list of conditions of self-cultivation).

Two things the reviews did not report. First, the book cites MacIntyre and builds on him. Chapter 2 takes "MacIntyre's account of the conceptual structure that unifies virtue traditions" and uses "his analysis to test the coherence of our own endeavor" (snippet, p. 45); it applies his internal/external goods distinction to technosocial goods ("speed, mobility, analytical power, knowledge, pleasure, friendship, and creativity ... But are these internal or external goods," snippet, p. 46) and concludes that "As MacIntyre emphasizes, a moral practice is irreducibly a social enterprise, not just the sum total of individual efforts to live well" (snippet, p. 49). Note 3 at p. 260 cites *Dependent Rational Animals* (1999); p. 261 marks her distance ("MacIntyre is primarily concerned with the Western moral tradition ... My task here is more ecumenical"). Borgmann appears at pp. 30–31 and 182 (snippets), as a critic of the Internet's "social shallows." Second, the book uses "friction" as a term of art, in ch. 6 on social media. A section headed "Communicative Friction" opens at p. 161: "My career as an emerging technology ethicist started with new social media for a simple reason: No technology that I had ever encountered had a comparable power to so rapidly and profoundly transform people's ..." (snippet, p. 161). Social media change communication's "pace, scope, and structure. Yet they also promote a dubious ideal: frictionless interactions that deftly evade the boredom, awkwardness, conflict, fear, misunderstanding, exasperation, and uncomfortable intimacies that often arise" in face-to-face life (snippet, p. 161). "Of course, such frictions still do arise in digital media connections. Yet when they do, the emerging norms of new media increasingly afford me or my interlocutor a quick and easy escape. We text gotta go, log off Facebook, or close an app, tab, or window and quickly open another, safer, more attractive, and less demanding connection" (snippets, p. 162). Her case is the 2013 Facebook Home ad campaign, "which featured persons using the app to evade undesirable interactions at home, at work, and on an airplane," which Evan Selinger called "social engineering spectacles" (snippet, p. 162). The criterion follows, a three-part test for a communicative practice that cultivates rather than erodes: one that "1) [did not offer] quick and easy escapes from social friction, challenge, or discomfort; 2) allowed considerable freedom to experiment with different communicative strategies for managing and coping with communicative friction; and 3) tempered that freedom with virtuous constraints— norms or rituals of communication designed to habituate them to social respect, competence, care, and wisdom" (snippets, pp. 162–163; the bracketed words are my reconstruction of the clause the snippet cuts, and must be checked). In the ad "the girl ... is not forced to try out any of the techniques that we considered for negotiating the boring, alienating experience of the family dinner— she can simply nullify the experience by means of her" phone (snippet, p. 163). The contagion follows: "Ignored Aunt will soon question why she's bothering to put in effort with her distant younger niece. Eventually, she'll adapt to the Facebook Home-idealized situation and stop caring. ... Selfishness is contagious, after all" (snippet, p. 164). She then refuses both determinisms: "We must resist the easy temptation to lay the blame for civic docility or incivility at the feet of new social media platforms, as if their impact is independent of our moral agency. Yet it would be equally misguided to let them off the hook as neutral" tools (snippet, p. 184).

On the institutional question. The Introduction's programme is that the virtues be "more consciously cultivated in our families, schools, and communities, supported and actively encouraged by our local and global institutions, and exercised not only individually but together, in acts of collective human wisdom. This is a tall order; but not beyond our" reach (snippets, pp. 10–11). Institutions elsewhere appear as the eroded scaffolding of desire — "institutions of cultural education, art, and ritual that traditionally steered individuals onto the path of right desire" (snippet, p. 124) — and the twelve technomoral virtues (ch. 6, taxonomy at p. 119, per snippet of the contents page) are virtues of persons. "Deskilling" occurs once in the whole text, in the reference list (snippet, p. 296, the 2015 paper); the 2015 argument is not restated under that name.

## Relation to the argument

RQ2's hypothesis was that the book's practices depend on occasions the agent can dwell in, and that a mediator setting the tempo removes the occasions rather than the capacity. The book supports the first half in its own words — cultivation is slow and painstaking (p. 63), cannot be leapt into (p. 89), and can be inhibited by "material and social deprivations" (p. 97) — and it supplies, in ch. 6, a friction criterion that the earlier cards attributed only to the essay. That changes the 2015 card's Caution: "friction" is not Vallor's word in 2015, but it *is* her word in 2016, as "communicative friction," with a dubious ideal named as "frictionless interactions" (p. 161) and a three-part criterion at pp. 162–163. The paper can therefore cite Vallor for the friction reading directly, provided it cites the book and not the paper.

Does the book ground the virtues institutionally, the failure mode the brief anticipated? Partly, and in a way the paper can use. The remedy locus is still the subject: the twelve virtues are qualities of character, the seven habits are habits of self-cultivation, and pp. 10–11 make families, schools and institutions the *supporters* of a cultivation that happens in persons. But the MacIntyre material at pp. 45–49 says that "a moral practice is irreducibly a social enterprise," and the Facebook Home passage at p. 164 shows why that matters for the construct: the aunt who keeps the old habit *loses* — she "will soon question why she's bothering" and "adapt ... and stop caring." That is the paper's disenfranchisement mechanism, written by Vallor about a mediated dyad, and it is a fact about the relation, not about either person's character. Her criterion at pp. 162–163 is a criterion on the *practice* (does it offer escape, permit experiment, constrain), which is to say on the environment the medium affords, and the medium in her case is a platform whose ad campaign sells the escape. So the book gets to the doorstep of the relation and stops: the criterion sorts practices, the aunt's loss is diagnosed, and the remedy is still to cultivate the niece (and, at p. 184, to hold the platform responsible without letting it absolve her). What the book lacks is what the brief predicted — any account of a practice whose tempo is set by a party that "interprets both, commits determinations that bind both." The friendlier reading is available and is the honest one: Vallor's practices are social and need institutions to hold their norms; those institutions are not yet designed for a mediator that sets the norms itself; the paper designs them.

Two corrections to the library. The 2015 card's Relation section reads Vallor's criterion as orthogonal to "whose objective," and the book confirms this: the Facebook Home girl serves her own objective (escape) and is deskilled anyway. But the 2016 criterion's third clause — "virtuous constraints ... norms or rituals" — is where an intermediary's own objective would enter if Vallor let it, and she does not; the constraint is cultural, never the platform's. And the macintyre1981.md card said it could not verify whether the 2016 book cites MacIntyre; it does, at pp. 45–49, 135 and notes at pp. 258–261, and the phrase "a practice in MacIntyre's sense" can be sourced to the book (not to the 2015 paper, which the S2 pass confirmed never cites him).

## Caution

This is a snippet read. Each page number is reliable (Google Books indexes the OUP edition's pagination, and the seven-habits list at p. 64 and the twelve-virtues taxonomy at p. 119/120 match Huff's page cites), but each quotation is forty words cut from a paragraph I have not seen, and one clause at pp. 162–163 is reconstructed. The three-condition criterion in particular must be read whole before it is quoted: I do not know what noun the three conditions modify (a "communicative practice"? an "environment"? a "childhood"?), and the snippet at p. 163 mentions "the techniques that we considered for negotiating ... the family dinner," which means the criterion is preceded by material I have not read. Whether the book anywhere treats a mediator that interprets both parties, or discusses dispatch, matching or ranking systems, could not be established by keyword; "speed" (pp. 3, 46–47, 123, 147, 208) and "pace" (pp. 161, 244) hits are about technosocial change and social media generally, not about an intermediary's tempo. Do not attribute to the book any claim about Φ, coordination, or a third party's objective; those are the essay's.
