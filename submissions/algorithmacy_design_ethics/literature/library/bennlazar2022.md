# Benn, C., & Lazar, S. (2022). What's wrong with automated influence. *Canadian Journal of Philosophy*, 52(1), 125–148.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1017/can.2021.23 (Cambridge Core; open access) · **Read depth:** full_text (the Cambridge Core HTML version of record, read in full; quotations are located by the article's numbered sections since the HTML does not mark the 125–148 page breaks) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed philosophy journal; a conceptual argument, no empirical study · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** human–technology–world in a structural register: "users of the internet on the one hand and the Automated Influencers on the other" (§4.d), with the population as the site of harm; no two-human coordination through the system · **Remedy locus:** relation, at the level of political institutions ("new ways to realise collective self-determination in our digital lives," §6) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18 (upgrades benn2022s.md in the coordinative_sovereignty library from abstract to full text)

## What it argues

"Automated Influence: The use of Artificial Intelligence to collect, integrate and analyse people's data, and to deliver targeted interventions based on this analysis, intended to shape their behaviour for exogenous or endogenous ends" (§2). Two critical perspectives: the "interactional approach considers the interactions between agents... Its normative critique is grounded exclusively in the self-authenticating claims of persons"; the "structural approach evaluates the emergent social structures of which those interactions are the leading edge" — "how they influence distributions of power, knowledge, and resources — as well as their aggregate effects" (§1). The paper argues that on privacy, exploitation and manipulation "a structural version of that objection adds something crucial to its interactional counterpart" (§1). On privacy: "you do not have a weighty underived claim to unilaterally control your intrinsically nonsensitive behavioural data. That data is the product of your interaction with a digital infrastructure" (§3.a); consent fails structurally because "if enough people choose A, then B is no longer a reasonable alternative," and "your privacy-preserving choices make little to no difference to the ability of online advertisers to profile you" (§3.c); the alternative, crediting Viljoen, is "a collective approach to allocating and using behavioural data" (§3.c). On exploitation: "any given individual's data is effectively worthless," so the individual charge fails (§4.a), but "when we consider users as a group and influencers as a group" (§4, opening), the "cooperative surplus... has been divided to give digital service providers a disproportionate share of the benefit and, more importantly, a disproportionate share of the power" — "unilateral control over the 'means of prediction'" (§4.b). The atomisation argument: Automated Influence "enables a kind of personalisation and an ideology of individualism that fragments us such that we become worse at engaging in considered collective action to undertake collective bargaining with the tech companies"; tech companies frame "our experience of our digital infrastructure so that we consider ourselves atomised individuals negotiating only on our own behalf" (§4.c). On manipulation: individually "not especially effective" (§5.d), but "stochastic manipulation" of groups "concentrates power in too few hands" and "corrodes democracy" (§5.e). Conclusion: "our individual consent does little to legitimate the new power structures of Automated Influence. Indeed, assessing Automated Influence from the individual perspective at all largely misses the point"; the problems "are collective action problems, the presumptive solution to which is more power, not less"; "The task of all would-be self-governing citizens of the internet... is to answer this crisis of legitimacy with new ways to realise collective self-determination in our digital lives" (§6).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8. Sorted: **grounds the why (collective standing); explicitly not an instrument**. Benn and Lazar are the philosophical argument for why the paper's remedy cannot sit in the subject: an individual's counter-move (privacy tools, a better bargaining position) makes "little to no difference" because the harm and the power are structural. Their §4.c is the sharpest challenge in the library to Principle I as a *client-side* proposal: the ideology of "atomised individuals negotiating only on our own behalf" is, on their account, what the platforms want, and a personal counter-agent could be read as that ideology with better tooling. The paper must answer this directly, and the answer available is the one Delacroix and Lawrence supply — a counter-agent that acts for a pool's terms, not merely a person's preferences. Two further uses. Their "means of prediction" is the objective axis stated as a property question: whoever controls the model sets the objective, and neither party to a coordination does. And their observation that platforms "largely police themselves" and that "we cannot gain firsthand knowledge of the functioning of the different recommendation algorithms" (§4.b) is the epistemic asymmetry the construct's inference component answers, stated by authors who conclude that inference by individuals is not the remedy. CUTS AGAINST the paper's framing of algorithmacy as a competence in the person, more directly than any other card here; the paper should cite it as the objection and locate its reply in the relation (Principle I) and in standing (the collective family), not in more literacy.

## Caution

Political philosophy with stipulative definitions (exploitation, manipulation) and no data; empirical claims about advertising effectiveness rest on cited studies the authors do not examine. The setting is behavioural advertising, recommenders and newsfeeds; the paper never models a two-sided market or a determination that binds two parties, and its "users" are consumers of content. Its conclusion is programmatic ("new ways to realise collective self-determination") and it offers no institution, so it grounds the sort of standing the collective family builds without endorsing any of its instruments.
