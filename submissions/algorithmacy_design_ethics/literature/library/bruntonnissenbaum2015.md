## What it argues

"In a sentence: Obfuscation is the deliberate addition of ambiguous, confusing, or misleading information to interfere with surveillance and data collection" (Introduction). The revolution "is especially suited for use by the small players, the humble, the stuck, those not in a position to decline or opt out or exert control over our data emanations" (Introduction). The unifying circumstance: "unable to refuse or deny observation, we create many plausible, ambiguous, and misleading signals within which the information we want to conceal can be lost" (§1). The two automated client-side tools are chapter 1's core cases. TrackMeNot, "developed in 2006 by Daniel Howe, Helen Nissenbaum, and Vincent Toubiana," does not hide queries but obfuscates "by automatically generating queries from a 'seed list' of terms," submitted "in a manner that tries to mimic real users' search behaviors," so that "it becomes much more difficult to say of any query that it was a product of human intention rather than an automatic output of TrackMeNot" (§1.4). CacheCloak fetches results for "several likely routes" so that "an adversary is left with many possible paths" (§1.3). AdNauseam, the browser extension that clicks every ad, appears in chapter 5: "if AdNauseam activity isn't known to the adversary, it works to foil profiling... If it is known, it both frustrates the work of profiling the individual and develops a protest role — a known gesture of mocking refusal. (Build a surveillance machine to get me to click a few ads? I'll click all of them!)" (§5.3). Chapter 3 (previous pass, PDF pages): "information collection takes place in asymmetrical power relationships: we rarely have a choice as to whether or not we are monitored, what is done with any information that is gathered, or what is done to us on the basis of conclusions drawn from that information" (p. 48); "the informational or epistemic asymmetry, is a deeper and more pernicious problem" (p. 49). Chapter 4 answers the objections. Dishonesty: "legitimate ends are a necessary condition for ethical obfuscation" (§4.1). Waste: "the notion of waste is thoroughly normative"; a charge of waste against TrackMeNot rests on the view that Google's "interests, desires, or preferences... trump users' interests" (§4.1). Free riding: the test is "Is your obfuscation system... freely available to others? And are people who aren't obfuscating left no worse off as a result of your use of that system? When the answers to both of those questions are Yes... we see no exploitation"; the hard case is "closed, secretive obfuscation that results in disadvantage to non-obfuscators," and even then "blame accrues primarily to the predator" (§4.1). Pollution: "Data pollution is unethical only when the integrity of the data flow or data set in question is ethically required"; "Obfuscating systems that pollute or subvert only the obfuscators' data trail pose fewer ethical challenges than those that also affect other data subjects" (§4.1). Alternatives — opting out, self-regulation, legislation — are reviewed and found wanting: "notice and consent... remain the dominant mechanisms for protecting privacy online, despite decisive evidence that they are incomprehensible to data subjects" (§4.2). The political frame is Rawls's maximin and Pettit's non-domination: freedom "is compromised... when others have the capacity to exercise this power in ways that we don't understand and that we experience as arbitrary" (§4.2). "A just society leaves this escape hatch open" (§4.2). Chapter 5 gives six goals — "buying some time, providing cover, deniability, evading observation, interfering with profiling, and expressing protest" — and four design questions: individual or collective, known or unknown, selective or general, short- or long-term (§§5.2–5.3). "Some obfuscation projects can be used by an individual or by a small group but will become more effective as more people join in," while a technique "that relies on blending in... will become far more vulnerable if widely adopted" (§5.3). The policy conditions the authors want: "encourage large, public facing systems to offer open APIs to developers of obfuscating systems; and refuse enforcement of Terms of Service that prohibit reasonable obfuscating systems" (§5.3).

## Relation to the argument

RQ8: the strongest existing anchor for a real, automated, client-side counter-agent. Sorted: **counter-delegation proper (individual, unilateral)** — TrackMeNot and AdNauseam are software that acts for one user, inside the user's channel to the intermediary, in real time, without permission. That is what Principle I names, built and deployed. Four findings for the paper. First, the previous card's guess that chapter 4 might already contain the harm-to-third-parties point is confirmed: the free-riding and pollution tests explicitly weigh whether non-obfuscators and "other data subjects" are left worse off, so the paper cannot say the authors overlook the second party — but their second party is a *bystander* (another surveilled user), not a *counterpart* whose coordination the same determination binds. In the triad, a driver's obfuscated location degrades the match the customer needs; the book's tests would flag that as pollution affecting "other data subjects," and the paper can use its own criteria to show why obfuscation is a privacy tactic that fails as a coordination tactic. Second, the epistemic-asymmetry passage (ch. 3) is the inference component stated from the user's side; obfuscation is what a user does *instead of* inferring. Third, the design questions of chapter 5 are a ready checklist for any counter-agent — individual or collective, known or unknown — and the authors' own answer for profiling tools is that they need numbers ("more effective as more people join in"), which pulls the individual tool toward the collective family (hardt2023.md). Fourth, their two policy asks — open APIs for obfuscating tools and unenforceable anti-obfuscation terms of service — are the interoperability precondition (eu2022dma.md) demanded in 2015 by the counter-agent's own designers. CUTS AGAINST the paper's Principle I as a *parity* instrument: obfuscation changes the intermediary's input blindly and does not contest its determination; by the authors' own goals list, the nearest goal is "interfering with profiling," not negotiation. It also CUTS AGAINST the literacy paradigm more sharply than the paper does — the authors' target user is "not technically sophisticated or savvy enough to utilize strong encryption," so the remedy is deliberately built *for* the user who lacks literacy. Disambiguation (added 2026-09-19): this is the second of two answers Nissenbaum gives to the same asymmetry — the "far from equal" parties of `nissenbaum2004.md` (p. 148) — and the paper critiques the first; contextual integrity (`nissenbaum2004.md`, `nissenbaum2011.md`, remedy locus norms/institution) answers with a norm a court or regulator must enforce, and obfuscation is the user-side instrument that account lacks, operated by the weak party alone.
