## What it argues

"In a sentence: Obfuscation is the deliberate addition of ambiguous, confusing, or misleading information to interfere with surveillance and data collection" (Introduction, p. 1); "a lexicon of ways to put some sand in the gears, to buy time, and to hide in the crowd of signals" (p. 1). "Obfuscation, at its most abstract, is the production of noise modeled on an existing signal in order to make a collection of data more ambiguous, confusing, harder to exploit, more difficult to act on, and therefore less valuable" (p. 45), distinguished from "methods that rely on disappearance or erasure"; "the essence of obfuscation is in getting things overlooked, and adding to the cost, trouble, and difficulty of doing the looking" (p. 46). Cases include WWII radar chaff, TrackMeNot (2006; browser extension that issues decoy search queries so that "the purpose ... is to foil the profiling of users," ch. 1 §1.4), CacheCloak (location obscured "by surrounding it with other users' paths," §1.3), and Twitter bots that flooded the 2011 Russian protest hashtag (§1.2). The justification is asymmetry: "information collection takes place in asymmetrical power relationships: we rarely have a choice as to whether or not we are monitored, what is done with any information that is gathered, or what is done to us on the basis of conclusions drawn from that information" (p. 48); beyond that power asymmetry "the informational or epistemic asymmetry, is a deeper and more pernicious problem" — "we don't know what near-future algorithms, techniques, hardware, and databases will be able to do with our data" (p. 49). Obfuscation "is a tool particularly suited to the 'weak' — the situationally disadvantaged, those at the wrong end of asymmetrical power relationships. It is a method, after all, that you have reason to adopt if you can't be invisible — if you can't refuse to be tracked or surveilled, if you can't simply opt out" (ch. 1 §1.2). §3.4 borrows James Scott's "weapons of the weak" — foot dragging, feigned ignorance, the hidden transcript — while disclaiming a "one-to-one comparison" (p. 55); "it's not just armed uprising or nothing at all, and no one is merely passive" (p. 56); "situations arise in which we are obligated to be visible, in which we need to be visible, or want to be visible ... and still we want to muddy our tracks as best we can" (p. 56).

## Relation to the argument

This is the user-side tactic none of the four affordances provides, and the brief was right to flag it as a candidate fifth affordance or a deliberate omission. Three things follow from the text. First, obfuscation targets the *interpretive* act on the structural axis — it degrades the intermediary's reading of one party — and it is the only move in this literature that a party can make *unilaterally*, without a designed seam, a counterfactual tool, or the intermediary's cooperation; it is de Certeau's tactic made operational (the authors' Scott framing is the same lineage). Second, its logic is dyadic: the adversary is a collector, and the book has no model of a second human on the far side of the intermediary whose coordination *also* depends on the intermediary reading the first party correctly. In the triad, a driver who obfuscates location degrades the match that the customer needs; the "weapon of the weak" wounds the other weak party. That is a genuine reason a design-ethics paper might *omit* obfuscation from its affordances, and the paper should name it rather than leave the omission unexplained — it is the tertius gaudens problem from the user's side, since the parties' inability to coordinate their obfuscation is the third's advantage. Third, the epistemic-asymmetry passage (p. 49) is close to the construct's "infer what the intermediary is doing with both sides": the authors say the user cannot know what the collector will do with the data; the construct's competence is precisely the partial reversal of that asymmetry. CUTS AGAINST: Brunton and Nissenbaum treat obfuscation as a *privacy* tactic against *collection*; the construct's problem is *coordination* through *determination*. Obfuscating the input to a matching function does not contest the determination; it changes it blindly.

## Caution

Read from a circulated PDF, not the version of record; page numbers are the edition's page markers and should be spot-checked. Chapters 2, 4 (ethics of obfuscation) and 5 (whether it works) not read; the authors' own treatment of the "free-riding" and "harm to third parties" objections is in chapter 4 and may already contain the point made above about harming the other party — check before claiming the omission is unaddressed. No empirical evaluation of any obfuscation tool is offered. The book's setting is surveillance capitalism and state surveillance, not platform coordination.
