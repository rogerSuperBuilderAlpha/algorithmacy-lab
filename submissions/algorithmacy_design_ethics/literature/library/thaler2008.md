## What it argues

Thaler's ten-year retrospective states the book's idea in one sentence: "By improving the environment in which people choose—what we call the 'choice architecture'—they can make wiser choices without restricting any options." His example is GPS: "You decide where you want to go, the app offers possible routes, and you are free to decline the advice if you decide to take a detour." The normative anchor is that "the goal of a conscientious choice architect is to help people make better choices 'as judged by themselves.'" Sludge is introduced as the inverse: "But what about activities that are essentially nudging for evil? This 'sludge' just mucks things up and makes wise decision-making and prosocial activity more difficult." Sunstein's 2018 draft adds that "nudges can be for good or for bad" and that "it is reasonable to see sludge as a kind of nudge; friction can nudge people in a helpful or unhelpful way" (n. 23). So in its authors' own later statement, the framework has three moving parts: an architect, a chooser whose own judgement is the standard, and friction as one instrument that can serve or defeat that judgement.

## Relation to the argument

*Nudge* is in this library for one reason: it is the source of the criterion the RQ says cannot settle the friction question. "As judged by themselves" makes the chooser's own considered preference the test of whether a design move is a nudge or sludge, and Buçinca et al. (2021) show that criterion returning the wrong answer for cognitive forcing functions — users judge against the friction that improves their decisions. On the objective axis the framework is instructive by what it assumes: the choice architect is presumed to share, or to serve, the chooser's own ends, and the GPS example is a channel in the construct's sense (the user "decide[s] where [to] go," the app advises, the user may decline). The construct's party case — an intermediary "pursu[ing] an objective neither of them set" — is what Thaler's 2018 editorial names "nudging for evil," and his remedy is to sort architects by intent, not to equip choosers with a competence. Nothing in the framework asks the chooser to infer, contest or adapt; that is the gap algorithmacy is defined into. It also cannot see formation: a nudge that produces the right choice by keeping the chooser in System 1 counts as a success, which is exactly what Cox et al. (2016) call a dark pattern and Vallor (2015) calls deskilling.

## Caution

A card built from one paragraph of a 2018 editorial and a footnote in a draft article is a card about how the authors summarise their book, not about the book. *Nudge*'s own definitions (libertarian paternalism, the nudge as any aspect of choice architecture that "alters people's behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives") are widely quoted but were not verified here and should be checked against the 2008 text or the 2021 "Final Edition" before use. The examples are public-policy defaults (retirement saving, organ donation, cafeteria layout), not interfaces, and the empirical base is the behavioural-economics literature of the 2000s, some of which has since been contested.
