## What it argues

The column has three declared goals (p. 132): explain why mindfulness surged, recover its Buddhist range from the narrowed Western definition, and draw out "opportunities and challenges that mindfulness poses to managers and, especially, marketing." The market facts it opens with: over 25% of U.S. companies offer mindfulness training, there are "over 1000 apps," and Headspace has "over 10 million" downloads (p. 132). The three reasons for the surge (p. 132) are an "ever-rising tsunami of information and stimuli," the rising "costs of mindlessness" in a technological age, and mindfulness's fit with a secular, individualist temper. The first reason carries the attention-economy diagnosis: designers "are becoming more and more adept at hooking our attention and, in the extreme, making us addicted to the stimuli they charge for (Cooper, 2017). Simply, we live in an age of digital distraction" (p. 132).

The centre of the piece is a typology of *sati* into four modes (p. 133): observation ("how it is currently employed in Western psychology"), remembering ("its original meaning"), discrimination ("the act of recognizing, evaluating and selecting mental states"), and contemplation ("the generation of insight into the working of the body-mind"). Figure 1 (p. 134) places them on two axes, "experiential to conceptual and receptive-reflective to active-judgment"; Figure 2 (p. 135) nests them, from awareness of a phenomenon through returning, evaluation, and insight.

Section 5 turns to marketing. It opens with Tristan Harris and Time Well Spent (p. 135), then states the strongest structural claim in the paper: "Increasingly, our attention is not under our own control. Choices are made for us not necessarily in our interest but in the interest of social media companies and the advertisers they serve. In short, business models are designed to make us mindless" (p. 136). The authors then trace this to the collapse of digital marginal cost and the advertising model, "If you're not paying for it, you are the product" (p. 136).

The workplace passage the prior pass reported is real, and this is its exact wording (p. 136): "So why is it that companies are at the forefront of promoting mindfulness within their own organizations? It turns out that employees are just as distracted by social media as consumers, and firms see mindfulness practice as a way of enhancing employee's productivity and well-being." That is nearly the whole of the employee discussion: two sentences, framed from the firm's side as a productivity tool, followed by one aside on Silicon Valley parents limiting their children's social media (p. 136); the only other traces are the keyword "Employee productivity" and a p. 132 citation of evidence for mindfulness "enhancing the cognitive performance of the healthy in a range of settings from schools to business."

The typology "allows three things" (p. 136): a better understanding of mindfulness; "it allows firms to develop offerings to help particular modes of mindfulness"; and it "allows marketers to rethink the 'attention capturing' and 'more consumption is better' business models and innovate so as to develop more mindful modes of business practice."

The closing paragraph of section 5, verified verbatim (p. 136): "observational mindfulness may be of little help with addictive technology platforms and their associated content—being more immersed in a moment-by-moment Snapchat streak may even be counterproductive. Here, evaluative (right) mindfulness is needed, with the ability to discern the wholesome from unwholesome capturing of attention and thereby opening us up to the possibility of choice. … Finally, wise mindfulness or mindfulness that leads to insight is essential if we are to see how our minds are undermined by the technologies and content we interact with, and equally importantly, how the mind undermines itself."

The summary's last sentence (p. 136) restates the only firm-side recommendation (item 3 above), still without a mechanism: "Opportunities to develop offerings that promote and enable different modes of mindfulness, and challenges to innovate to develop new business models that do not run on attention become readily apparent."

## Relation to the argument

RQ5 asks what the typology licenses and what is this paper's own extension. The prior pass's characterization holds on every point I checked. The phrase "technology fast" does not appear. Economic survival, the labour market, being cast out of work, and the impossibility of opting out do not appear. The one sentence that could be stretched toward compulsion — "our attention is not under our own control" (p. 136) — is about attention capture inside the platform, not about whether one can leave it. So the framing "mandatory economic survival, can't opt out via technology fast" is this arm's extension, and the draft must say so rather than cite Berthon for it.

The defensible critique is narrower and, I think, stronger. Berthon diagnoses a design-side problem ("business models are designed to make us mindless") and prescribes a subject-side remedy: four modes of the user's own attention, escalating from bare awareness to insight into "how our minds are undermined by the technologies" (p. 136). That is the literacy paradigm applied to attention itself — read the platform's grip on you well enough to see through it. The firm-side line is an exhortation with no mechanism: it names "challenges to innovate" and stops. Nothing in the paper places a claim on the platform's design, on the relation between two users the platform coordinates, or on what a user can contest. Under this arm's construct, Berthon's wise mindfulness is a competence for a moderator world: it presumes a user who can, with enough practice, see what is being done to them. It has nothing to say about a mediator that interprets two parties and commits determinations that bind both, because the paper never models two users at all.

Two things in the text can be used positively. The nested progression — awareness, return, evaluation, insight — is a plausible scaffold for the competence gradient the paper needs (from literacy toward algorithmacy), provided the arm says clearly that Berthon aims the gradient at one's own mind, not at a third party. And "opening us up to the possibility of choice" (p. 136) is the nearest the column comes to contestation; it is choice about one's own attention, not a claim on what the platform commits.

For the contrast case, see williams2018.md: Williams treats the same attention economy and explicitly refuses the subject-side remedy, including firm-promoted employee mindfulness.

## Caution

This is a seven-page department column in a practitioner journal, with no data, no method, and a reference list mixing Buddhist scholarship (Bodhi, Dreyfus, Gethin) with *60 Minutes* and *Wired*. It is a good source for the typology and a weak one for any empirical claim about platforms; the platform claims are second-hand from Harris, Eyal, and Cooper. The Buddhist exegesis is itself a secondary reading of Bodhi, Dreyfus, Gethin, and Olendzki in *Contemporary Buddhism* 12(1). The local PDF is the in-press copy; a citation-checker should confirm the 2019 page breaks before the paper goes out. The lead author (McCallum School of Business, Bentley University, per the affiliation line on p. 131) shares an institutional home with this arm's author; the draft should say so in a note rather than leave a reader to find it, and should make plain that the source was chosen as a specimen of the subject-side remedy, not as a colleague's work to be endorsed or spared.
