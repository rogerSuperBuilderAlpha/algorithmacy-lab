# Rudin, C. (2019). Stop explaining black box machine learning models for high stakes decisions and use interpretable models instead. *Nature Machine Intelligence*, 1(5), 206–215.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1038/s42256-019-0048-x (Crossref-verified 2026-09-18: *Nature Machine Intelligence* 1(5), pp. 206–215, 13 May 2019) · arXiv:1811.10154 (v3, 22 September 2019, described by the author as the "pre-publication version" of the NMI article) · **Read depth:** full_text of the arXiv v3 author version, all 20 pages including Appendices A–E; the NMI pagination was not mapped, so page numbers below are the preprint's · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed perspective in a Nature journal; an argument paper with worked examples (COMPAS, CORELS, RiskSLIM, prototype networks), no new experiments · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Parties modeled:** decision maker–model–decision subject (a judge or clinician using a model about a person; the subject is the object of prediction, not a coordinating party) · **Remedy locus:** artifact (constrain the model class) with a policy rider (mandate interpretable models where they match black-box accuracy) · **Relation last checked:** 2026-09-18

## What it argues

Rudin's thesis: "trying to explain black box models, rather than creating models that are interpretable in the first place, is likely to perpetuate bad practices and can potentially cause catastrophic harm to society. There is a way forward – it is to design models that are inherently interpretable" (Abstract). Her definition is structural. Interpretability "is a domain-specific notion ... so there cannot be an all-purpose definition. Usually, however, an interpretable machine learning model is constrained in model form so that it is either useful to someone, or obeys structural knowledge of the domain, such as monotonicity, causality, structural (generative) constraints, additivity, or physical constraints that come from domain knowledge" (p. 1); sparsity is the usual measure for structured data, and case-based reasoning for complex domains. A black box "could be either (i) a function that is too complicated for any human to comprehend, or (ii) a function that is proprietary" (p. 2). Against post-hoc explanation she argues five points: the accuracy–interpretability trade-off is "a myth" for structured data, illustrated by the DARPA XAI figure that "was not generated by any data" (pp. 2–3); "Explanations must be wrong. They cannot have perfect fidelity with respect to the original model. If the explanation was completely faithful to what the original model computes, the explanation would equal the original model" (p. 3); explanations "often do not make sense," as with saliency maps, since "Knowing where the network is looking within the image does not tell the user what it is doing with that part of the image" (p. 4); black boxes cannot be combined with information outside the database, so a judge cannot see that COMPAS ignores the seriousness of the current crime (p. 5); and "we must troubleshoot two models rather than one" (p. 5). She proposes to "stop calling approximations to black box model predictions explanations" and call them "summaries of predictions" (p. 4). The COMPAS case is her centrepiece: a three-rule CORELS list on age and priors is "equally accurate" (p. 6, Figure 3); COMPAS "is not a machine learning model" and is "a black box of the second type – proprietary – but not the first type – complicated" (p. 7); and the business model is the obstacle, since "the companies that profit from these models are not necessarily responsible for the quality of individual predictions" (p. 7). Against the gaming argument for secrecy: "the reason a system may be gamed is because it most likely was not designed properly in the first place, leading to a form of Goodhart's law if it were revealed" (p. 7). Against counterfactual explanations: they are "sufficient (but not necessary)" changes whose cost to the individual cannot be known in advance, so "it is unclear that counterfactual explanations would suffice for high stakes decisions" (Appendix C, p. 19). On regulation: GDPR Article 22 governs a "right to an explanation," "where only an explanation is required, not an interpretable model," and "Less-than-satisfactory explanations can easily undermine these new policies" (p. 9); she proposes a mandate that "no black box should be deployed when there exists an interpretable model with the same level of performance" (p. 9). Interpretable models exist because data admit large "Rashomon sets" of near-equally accurate models, so that "a larger Rashomon set probably contains interpretable models" (p. 14); her prototype network gives explanations that "are the actual computations of the model, and these are not posthoc explanations" (p. 13).

## Relation to the argument

RQ10, as a CONTRAST CASE, and the paper must draw the line precisely because a referee will ask whether Principle III is Rudin with the serial numbers filed off. The two positions agree on the negative: post-hoc explanation of a black box is not a remedy, because the explanation is a second model that "must be wrong." They part on what replaces it. Rudin's alternative is *interpretable internal structure*: constrain the model's *form* — sparse rule lists, integer scoring systems, prototype layers — so that a human can read the function that computes the output. Principle III's alternative is *bounded outputs / operational predictability*: constrain what the system may *emit and do* — the envelope of determinations, their stability over time, their announced regime — whether or not the function inside is readable. Three consequences follow, and each is a place where the paper can show the distinction has teeth. (1) *Object of the constraint.* Rudin's constraints are on the model class ("constrained in model form"); Principle III's are on the behaviour of the deployed service, in Leveson's sense of safety constraints enforced at the system level. A three-rule list is interpretable and may still be replaced next month; an unreadable model can be held to an output envelope. Chen, Zaharia and Zou's (2024) drift is the case in point: interpretability of a version does not bind the operator across versions. (2) *Who must understand.* Rudin's reader is the expert user of the model — the judge, the clinician, the analyst — and her worked examples are dyadic decision-maker-and-subject cases; the subject is predicted about, not coordinating. Principle III addresses the coordinated lay party, for whom Bainbridge's argument applies: she cannot follow the criteria in real time even when they are legible. Sarter and Woods make the same cut — the operator needs the regime, not the computation. (3) *What secrecy is for.* Rudin's second kind of black box, the proprietary one, is the platform's kind, and her Goodhart argument assumes that a well-designed objective would survive disclosure. In the triad the objective is the operator's, so disclosing the model to the parties does not align it; that is Principle II's territory, and Rudin's optimism about transparency ("improving one's credit score should actually correspond to an improvement in creditworthiness") does not transfer. Where the two positions overlap, the paper should say so rather than pretend otherwise: monotonicity and additivity constraints are constraints on *behaviour* as much as on form (a monotone model guarantees an output relation), so Rudin's toolbox already contains bounded-output devices; and her Appendix C on counterfactual explanations is a direct warning against the counterfactual-exploration affordance carded elsewhere in this library (Wachter et al. 2018), on the ground that the "minimal" change is minimal only by a cost the system does not know. CUTS AGAINST Principle III: Rudin would reply that a bounded black box still has issues (iv) and (v) — the decision maker cannot combine it with information outside the database, and its inputs are error-prone — and that an envelope without a readable function gives the party no way to argue *why* a determination is wrong. The paper's answer has to be that Principle III is not offered as the whole remedy: refusal (II) and parity (I) carry the argumentative burden, and III carries only stability. It should also concede her strongest point against the field: "even strong regulations such as 'right to explanation' can be undermined with less-than-satisfactory explanations" (p. 15), which is Lyons et al.'s point about contest, applied to explanation.

## Caution

A perspective piece by the leading advocate of one position, with examples drawn from her own group's methods (CORELS, RiskSLIM, "This Looks Like That"); the accuracy claims about COMPAS versus CORELS rest on Broward County data and her cited work, not on evidence in this paper. Her "interpretability" is explicitly domain-specific and she declines to define it generally, so any contrast with Principle III inherits that looseness. The paper concerns high-stakes prediction about individuals (recidivism, medical risk, air quality), not coordination between two parties through a model, and the word "predictability" does not appear in her sense of the term. Page numbers are the arXiv v3 preprint's; the NMI version is shorter and differently paginated.
