## What it argues

The essay restates the theory from *Privacy in Context* — privacy as "expected flows of personal information, modeled with the construct of context-relative informational norms," with "key parameters" of actors, attributes and transmission principles (p. 33) — and turns it on the online case. The first half dismantles notice-and-consent. Its two supports are the "right to control information about oneself" and the market paradigm in which "personal information may be conceived as part of the price of online exchange" (p. 34); "there is considerable agreement that transparency-and-choice has failed" (p. 35). Critical adherents want better notice and opt-in; Nissenbaum thinks the model is "plagued by deeper problems" (p. 36). Behavioral advertising is her exhibit: "the realm is in constant flux, with new firms entering the picture, new analytics, and new back-end contracts forged: in other words, we are dealing with a recursive capacity that is indefinitely extensible" (p. 36). Hence the transparency paradox: "transparency of textual meaning and transparency of practice conflict in all but rare instances" (p. 36). Consent works in medicine only inside "a framework of supporting assurances" — "It is not the consent form itself that draws our signature and consigns us to the operating table, but rather our faith in the system" — whereas "In the online environment, by contrast, individual consent agreements must carry the entire weight of expectation" (p. 36).

The second half is the positive account. Against the framing of "online privacy" as a distinct venue: "Questions about protecting privacy online, particularly when framed as questions about online privacy, suggest that 'online' is a distinctive venue, sphere, place, or space defined by the technological infrastructures and protocols of the Net, for which a single set of privacy rules can, or ought to, be crafted. I resist this notion. ... The Net does not constitute (drawing on the terminology of contextual integrity) a discrete context. It is not a single social realm, but the totality of experience conducted via the Net, from specific websites to search engines to platforms and on up into 'the cloud,' crisscrossing multiple realms" (p. 38). Life online "is radically heterogeneous, comprising multiple social contexts, not just one," and disruptions caused by the Net "do not warrant sui generis, uniform, cross-cutting rules determined by the medium. Instead, the contexts in which activities are grounded shape expectations" (p. 38). The compressed method follows on the same page: "The decision heuristic derived from the theory of contextual integrity suggests that we locate contexts, explicate entrenched informational norms, identify disruptive flows, and evaluate these flows against norms based on general ethical and political principles as well as context-specific purposes and values" (p. 38). For genuinely novel offerings "the theory of contextual integrity directs us beyond existing norms to underlying standards, derived from general moral and political considerations as well as the ends, purposes, and values of respective contexts" (p. 40). A search engine is to be judged by function — "akin to conducting research, seeking information and association, searching a library catalog" — so its governing norm would be "strict confidentiality with regard to Web search histories," and Google's voluntary barrier between search logs and profiles is welcomed with the caveat that "the commitment can be revoked at any moment, as was Google's commitment to forgo behavioral advertising" (p. 43). Then: "This view of online privacy also implies that contexts, not political economy, should determine constraints on the flow of information" (p. 43); databases "shared in one context, under constraints of the relevant informational norms, should not be treated as just another asset, akin to buildings, furniture, and supplies," while "Online conglomerates ... strive to achieve vertical integration by controlling the raw materials of their industry, namely, information" (p. 44). Companies name their offerings in contextual terms, and "By calling its online offering a university, a shoe store, a church, a medical center, a friendship network, or a bank, a company gives users a way to understand the services or activities that take place there, and it invites evaluation against respective norms, whether these are embodied in law or simply arise from reasonable expectations" (p. 44), which "requires companies to commit to partitioning information holdings along contextual contours rather than along lines of corporate ownership" (p. 44). The social function of privacy: "privacy norms do not merely protect individuals; they play a crucial role in sustaining social institutions," so restraints on search engines and social networks "are as much about sustaining important social values of creativity, intellectual growth, and lively social and political engagement as about protecting individuals against harm" (p. 44). The second recommendation, for cases "without straightforward social precedents": "I suggest starting with ends, purposes, and values and working from there back to norms" (p. 44); on a politician's talk-back site, "The issue, however, is not how particular practices affect individuals, but the implications for particular purposes and values" (p. 45). The close asks that we "make explicit much that has operated implicitly, and in the process reject entrenched norms that no longer promote the achievement of moral and political values as well as context-specific ends," and that context-based rules be embedded "in law and other specialized codes" to "yield the safety nets that buttress consent" (p. 45).

## Relation to the argument

This is the text that kills the naive collapse argument, and the plan's reading of it is confirmed at p. 38: contexts are social realms, the Net is "the totality of experience conducted via the Net ... crisscrossing multiple realms," and a platform that names itself "a university, a shoe store, a church" thereby "invites evaluation against respective norms" (p. 44). "The mediator defines the context" is therefore a move her theory refuses in advance, and the draft must not make it. What the draft can say is stronger and narrower, and this essay supplies each piece.

*Unit of analysis.* The heuristic at p. 38 has four steps and all four are about flows; its parameters at p. 33 are subject, sender, recipient, attribute, transmission principle. There is no fifth step for what the recipient does with the flow, and no parameter for a third person whom the recipient's determination binds. **Remedy locus: norms/institution** is read directly off the abstract's "backdrop of context-specific substantive norms" (p. 32) and the close's "law and other specialized codes" (p. 45).

*The heuristic has no agent, and the practice does not hold still.* The verbs at p. 38 — locate, explicate, identify, evaluate — have "we" as their subject, and the "we" of the essay is policy-makers and the FTC and Commerce Department (pp. 32, 41–42), never the person inside the flow. The individual is named once as a remedy, to be dismissed: "burdening individuals with the full weight of protecting their privacy online through notice-and-choice is unlikely to yield success" (p. 43). And the tempo problem is her own finding about behavioral advertising — "a recursive capacity that is indefinitely extensible" in "constant flux" (p. 36) — offered as the reason notice fails; the paper's point is that the same fact defeats a heuristic whose first step is to locate a stable disruptive flow. Her caveat about Google's barrier, revocable "at any moment" (p. 43), is the operator-declared envelope with nothing binding the operator to it — the Principle III bridge the plan asks F1 to test. It holds in this form: contextual integrity has the concept (a commitment to partition holdings along contextual contours, p. 44) and assigns its enforcement to law and "reasonable expectations," not to anything the mediated person holds.

*The Walzer ally, in 2011 form.* "Contexts, not political economy, should determine constraints on the flow of information" (p. 43), the refusal to treat databases as "just another asset" (p. 44), and the description of conglomerates controlling "the raw materials of their industry, namely, information" (p. 44) are the dominant-good worry of `nissenbaum2004.md` (p. 145) applied to platforms: the good is information, the possessor is the conglomerate, and the intrusion is vertical integration across contexts. Her remedy is partition — a blocked exchange — which is protective by construction.

*The promotive term.* This essay is where the plan's honest sentence comes from. Nissenbaum does have a promotive register: norms are to "promote the achievement of moral and political values as well as context-specific ends" (p. 45), privacy sustains "creativity, intellectual growth, and lively social and political engagement" (p. 44), and Franklin protected the mail "to promote a meaningful social role for the service" (p. 44). But its object is the institution — the mail, the search engine, "frank political discussion" (p. 45) — and she says so: "The issue, however, is not how particular practices affect individuals, but the implications for particular purposes and values" (p. 45). The draft should quote that sentence rather than claim she has no promotive term.

*What she would say about the dispatch algorithm.* Employment is an existing context with entrenched norms, so her first recommendation (p. 43) applies: locate the correspondence, bring the norms into view, evaluate the platform's flows against them. The draft should concede this reading in full and then observe that it yields a verdict on what the platform may know about the driver and to whom it may tell it — and no verdict on the assignment the platform makes between driver and customer, which is not a flow of information about either.

## Caution

An invited essay for a general-audience quarterly, without the apparatus of the 2004 article or the 2010 book; it is the compressed public statement of the theory, not its full defence, and the heuristic at p. 38 is offered in one sentence. Its cases are search, social networks, e-commerce, banking, e-filing and behavioral advertising; no matching, ranking, pricing or dispatch system appears, so every transfer to a two-sided mediator is this card's inference. The essay's "we" is read here as policy-makers on the strength of its addressees (FTC, Commerce Department) and its closing recommendations; the text does not state who runs the heuristic. The amacad.org HTML is unpaginated; page numbers are from the author's PDF of the MIT Press typesetting, which carries the journal's running feet but which I did not compare against MIT Press Direct's own file.
