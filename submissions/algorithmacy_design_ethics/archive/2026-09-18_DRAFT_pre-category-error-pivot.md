# Two Tests for Friction: Interface Design for Algorithmic Intermediaries with a Third Objective

## Abstract

A report circulating in my research group last month proposed an "AI Context Engine," pairing mechanistic interpretability with content provenance, to restore transparency to opaque algorithmic systems. I show that neither mechanism can do the job the report asks of it, because the problem it names is not the one either mechanism addresses. Interpretability discloses a model's internal computation to whoever holds its weights; provenance discloses a media asset's origin to a downstream validator. Both are dyadic: one human, one artifact. The problem worth naming is triadic: a person coordinates with another person through an intermediary that interprets both of them, binds both, and pursues an objective neither one set. I develop this construct against three real cases: a suppressed radio channel in commercial aviation, a 1980s workplace coordination tool, and a gig-work platform. I use it to resolve a live design dilemma. Cognitive forcing functions and seamless transparency are not rival intuitions to be settled by user preference; sorted against two independent tests, one structural and one drawn from virtue ethics, the friction dilemma yields a design rule.

**Keywords:** algorithmic mediation, design ethics, cognitive forcing functions, technological mediation, integrated information, triadic coordination

---

## 1. The wrong remedy, and the problem it revealed

A colleague forwarded me a report last month arguing that opaque artificial intelligence recapitulates the harms of the printing press, and proposing a remedy: an "AI Context Engine" combining mechanistic interpretability, which reverse-engineers what a trained network computes, with content provenance, which cryptographically attests to where a piece of media came from. Both technologies are real, active research programs, and both are worth having. Neither solves the problem the report names.

Take interpretability first. The method, at its most developed, decomposes a network's internal activations into features and traces the circuits that connect them, treating the network, in its founding paper's words, as "an object of empirical investigation," studied the way a biologist studies a cell (Olah et al., 2020). What it discloses, and to whom, is stated nowhere more plainly than in its own literature: a researcher with access to the weights learns what the model computes. No user, no deployer, no second human party enters the picture as an object of analysis (Elhage et al., 2021; Bereska & Gavves, 2024). Provenance fares no better on the question that matters here. The C2PA specification, the industry standard for content authenticity, defines three roles: a signer who attests to an asset, a claim generator that builds the signed record, and a downstream validator who checks it. It says of that validator only that it is "not specified in the trust model." Nowhere does the standard model a second human whose interests the signed asset might bind (C2PA, 2026). Both mechanisms, in other words, solve a dyadic problem: one human, reading one artifact, wants to know more about it. Neither has anything to say about a *second* human on the other side of that artifact, whose interests the artifact might also be serving, or serving against.

That gap is not incidental. I searched the literatures a report like this would draw on, postphenomenology, value-sensitive design, human-factors automation research, science and technology studies, for any account of a technology that interprets two human parties at once and pursues an objective neither of them set. Almost none exists. Of ninety-nine sources surveyed for this paper, only twelve so much as gesture at a mediator standing between two humans, and on inspection most of those twelve turn out to model the mediator as a passive channel rather than an interested party.¹ The absence recurs across literatures that have no reason to agree with each other. Automation-trust research in human factors, from its foundational statement onward, defines trust and reliance relative to a single operator's goals; where the field admits a second human at all, it is a redundant crewmember on the *same* side of the automation, never a co-served party on the other (Lee & See, 2004; Parasuraman & Riley, 1997). Value-sensitive design distinguishes stakeholders as direct or indirect by who receives a system's output, a distinction that tracks nothing about who is jointly determined by it (Borning & Muller, 2012). Even the one CSCW system built explicitly to mediate between coworkers, three decades before any of this, turns out to interpret neither of them (Suchman, 1994), a case I return to in §3.

The absence is worth naming because the construct it clears space for is not exotic. Two people coordinate through an intermediary constantly: a customer and a driver through a dispatch algorithm, a borrower and a lender through a credit model, a prescriber and a pharmacist through a medication-alert system. What is exotic is the assumption, built into interpretability and provenance alike, that making the intermediary legible to *one* party, the one who already holds the keys, solves the problem for both. I call the competence such a situation demands *algorithmacy*: not literacy in reading an interface, but the interpretive, tactical, and anticipatory capacity to infer what a third party is doing with two humans at once, contest what it commits, and adapt as it changes.² Section 2 states that construct precisely and against the philosophical literature that ought to have it already. Section 3 tests it against three real cases spanning the boundary from ordinary mediation to full triadic cooptation. Section 4 puts the construct to work on a live design dilemma, the argument between transparency and friction, and resolves it with two independent tests rather than one. Section 5 places the resulting design rule against value-sensitive design's own account of whom a designer answers to. Section 6 returns to the politics of the arrangement, and to why a report reaching for interpretability and provenance reached for the wrong century's remedy.

## 2. The construct, and what postphenomenology does not yet have

State the construct plainly before defending it. A person coordinates with another person through an automated intermediary. It interprets both parties, commits determinations that bind both, and pursues an objective neither of them set. When this third party is only a channel, one the two humans could reach past to each other, the coordination factors cleanly into two people plus a passive wire, and the competence the situation demands is ordinary literacy: read the interface, understand the message. When it is instead a *party*, no direct tie between the two humans survives, and the coordination is irreducible to any decomposition that leaves the mediator out. Literacy is not enough. That situation demands algorithmacy: the capacity to infer what the system is doing with both sides, to contest what it commits, and to adapt as its behavior changes.

The claim that some coordination forms are irreducible in this sense is not metaphorical. It borrows, deliberately, from integrated information theory's measure of causal irreducibility, Φ: a system factors, meaning Φ equals zero, when some partition of it loses nothing, and it does not factor when no partition survives.³ Applied to a small model of two humans and an intermediary, Φ answers exactly the question a channel-or-party judgment needs answered: is there any way to cut the intermediary out that leaves the coordination between the two humans intact? When the answer is yes, the intermediary is a wire. When it is no, the intermediary is load-bearing, and the competence the situation demands has changed in kind, not degree.

That structural test is one axis of the construct. It is not the only one. A human interpreter standing between two people who share no language satisfies the structural test: the coordination is irreducible, she interprets both sides, no direct channel survives without her. But nobody would call her a co-optive third party, because she pursues no objective of her own. What distinguishes an interpreter from a platform is a second, independent fact: whether the intermediary is pursuing an objective *neither party set*. I call this the objective axis, and it does no work the structural axis already does. A translator satisfies the first and not the second. A dispatch algorithm satisfies both. The competence a situation demands is set by the structural axis; its adversarial character comes from the objective axis, and a design framework that conflates the two will misdiagnose which affordance a given case actually needs.⁴

Where does this construct sit relative to the one philosophical tradition built to study exactly how technologies mediate human experience? Verbeek (2015, p. 31) states postphenomenology's own position plainly: technologies, on his account, "always mediate human practices and experiences," so a designer's task is not to preserve some prior human autonomy but to design mediations responsibly, since none will ever be absent. That is, on its face, the extensionist position the construct above needs a threshold to reject. Verbeek names it as exactly the view his own theory replaces (2015, p. 28). His working relations, drawn from Ihde, number four: embodiment, hermeneutic, alterity, background (2015, pp. 28–29). Each models one human, one technology, and a world. None is built for two.

The closest analogue in the wider literature agrees with the construct's objective axis almost to the word. Latour (1994, p. 32) describes a driver reaching for a gun: "a third agent emerges from a fusion of the other two," and what results is often "the creation of a new goal that corresponds to neither agent's program of action." But this third possibility describes one human recruiting one tool, not two humans coordinated by a party interpreting them both. And Latour refuses the arithmetic a fixed threshold needs. Pressed on how many actants a broken projector really contains, he answers that "the philosophy of technology has little use for arithmetic." The count shifts with the step of the analysis (1994, p. 36). One source in the corpus I searched comes closer than either: a study of communication technologies used by grandparents and grandchildren explicitly models two humans and a mediating device (Corti et al., 2023). Even there, the device has no objective of its own. It is a channel with unusually rich features, not a party.

I take the honest conclusion to be that postphenomenology does not yet have the relation type this construct needs, not that Φ merely fills a threshold the field was already gesturing toward. What the construct proposes, stated in postphenomenological terms, is a bilateral hermeneutic relation: a technology that reads two humans to each other simultaneously, each occupying, for the other, the position Ihde's schema reserves for "world." Φ supplies exactly the threshold question the field's own extensionism refuses to ask, not *whether* the intermediary mediates, since on Verbeek's account everything does, but whether the mediation is one a direct human tie could survive without.

## 3. Three cases, and where the axes come apart

Abstract constructs earn their keep in real cases, and three real cases, none of them designed to illustrate this argument, happen to span its whole range.

**A channel with no objective.** Before commercial aircraft carried point-to-point datalink, pilots and controllers shared an open radio frequency: every pilot on the channel could overhear every other pilot's clearances, a resource researchers call "party line information," or PLI. Surveyed pilots rated it critical to situation awareness in roughly four cases of ten, rising past four in ten near the terminal area (Midkiff & Hansman, 1993). Datalink replaces the shared channel with a point-to-point one: each pilot sees only her own clearance, and the broadcast that let everyone check everyone else's understanding of the airspace closes. In a simulation study of the transition, crews who overheard evidence that a controller had misdirected another aircraft mostly noticed and did nothing. In one scripted event, all seven crews saw that the landing runway ahead had not been cleared, and none acted (Hansman et al., 1995). The researchers' own explanation is social, not cognitive: "the professional relationship dynamics between flight crewmembers and air traffic controllers may be a reason pilots were observed to be reticent to insinuate that a controller error had taken place based upon PLI alone" (Hansman et al., 1995, §3). This is a clean instance of the structural axis without the objective axis. Suppressing the shared channel makes the coordination between pilots irreducible to a decomposition that ignores the radio, and no independent objective sits behind the change. It is, already, an instance of the contestation problem the construct names: pilots inferred a third party's error and still would not challenge it, recorded before any learning algorithm entered a cockpit.

**A mediator that binds but does not interpret.** In 1984, Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores built a workplace tool called the Coordinator, designed to structure requests, promises, and commitments between coworkers around speech-act theory. Users tagged their own messages with an illocutionary category (request, promise, counter-offer), and the system tracked the resulting conversation as a state machine, warning of breakdowns and keeping, in Lucy Suchman's phrase, "a record that can subsequently be invoked by organization members in calling each others' actions to account" (Suchman, 1994, p. 181). Suchman's critique, published under a title borrowed deliberately from Langdon Winner, is that the Coordinator is not a neutral bookkeeping device but "a tool for the reproduction of an established social order," since Winograd and Flores's own field reports found it most successful precisely where users already trusted their position in the hierarchy (Suchman, 1994, p. 186). Read against this paper's construct, the Coordinator is a genuine near-miss, and a useful one. It satisfies the objective axis in Suchman's account: the categories were the designers' own, serving a managerial notion of "effectiveness" that neither coworker chose, and her 1995 reply sharpens the point into the construct's own vocabulary: "the definition of objectives is itself a political process" (Suchman, 1995, p. 93). But it fails the structural axis, and fails it on the point that matters most. The system did not interpret either party. Users "performed a speech act using the Coordinator by selecting the illocutionary force" themselves (Winograd & Flores, 1986, p. 159, quoted in Suchman, 1994, p. 180). The interpretation was delegated to the humans, not inferred by the machine, and coworkers could and, by Suchman's own account of the field reports, did talk around the system when it did not suit them (Suchman, 1994, p. 186). A mediator that binds two parties but leaves them a working direct channel is, by this construct's own logic, closer to a wire with a memory than to a party: Φ near zero, whatever its designers intended.

**Both axes present.** The third case needs less introduction, because it is the one this construct was built to explain. A gig-work platform interprets both a driver and a customer, commits a routing and pricing determination that binds both, and pursues an objective of its own (platform liquidity, engagement, retention) that neither set. Unlike the Coordinator, the direct channel is gone by design: platforms withhold phone numbers, discourage off-platform arrangements, and route every exchange through the interface. A field study of ride-hail drivers found exactly the asymmetry the structural axis predicts: drivers who understood the dispatch algorithm built workarounds and earned more; drivers who did not, lost earnings and ratings (Lee et al., 2015). A later study of the same population found workers attempting the same interpretive labor blind, without any interface support, describing the result as "experimental reactivity": probing a system whose rating criteria shift under them before any inference can be confirmed (Rahman, 2021). Both axes, no surviving channel, and a documented cost when the competence the situation demands goes unsupported.

Set beside each other, the three cases do more than illustrate a definition. They show the axes coming apart in the world, not only on paper. That is exactly what a designer needs before deciding what kind of intervention a given interface calls for, the question §4 takes up directly.

## 4. The friction dilemma, resolved by two tests instead of one

Interface designers face a genuine dilemma over transparency, and the empirical record on it is more settled, and more surprising, than either side of the argument usually admits.

The intuitive fix for an opaque system is to expose it: show the model's confidence, show the numbers behind a decision. The evidence against this intuition is unusually direct. A 2025 study tested token-level confidence highlighting on GPT-4o's advice across reasoning and estimation tasks; shading low-probability tokens was the *worst*-performing intervention tried, worsening participants' calibration and inducing outright aversion to the system, while a plain one-sentence disclaimer outperformed it (Bo et al., 2025). A companion finding, on a different system, is just as sharp. Brief text explanations of an algorithmic feed raised users' stated awareness that curation was happening, but *lowered* their felt sense of control and fairness. Rader and colleagues describe the resulting feeling as "disconnected from power" (Rader et al., 2018). Raw exposure does not merely fail to help; in both cases it actively makes things worse. The remedy that has held up empirically is not exposure but what its proponents call seamful disclosure: revealing not a model's internals but the boundary conditions under which its assumptions hold, where a model is extrapolating beyond its training, where a dispatch rule prioritizes platform liquidity over driver proximity (Ehsan et al., 2024). Counterfactual interfaces work the same way from a different angle. An early platform study placed a curated feed beside an unfiltered one; users who saw what had been hidden re-sorted 43 percent of their contacts into new categories, and most were still doing so six months later (Eslami et al., 2015).

The harder cases are the ones where transparency, done right, still is not enough. The honest fix there is *friction*, not disclosure. Cognitive forcing functions require a person to commit to a judgment before an AI's recommendation is revealed, and the empirical result is unambiguous: forcing a decision first measurably reduces overreliance on incorrect advice. It is also unambiguously disliked. Users rate the forced-delay interfaces as more effortful and prefer the seamless ones that let them defer to the machine (Buçinca et al., 2021). Bainbridge diagnosed the same trap in industrial automation four decades earlier, in terms that do not mention algorithms at all. When a control system makes finer-grained, faster decisions than a human can check, "there is therefore no way in which the human operator can check in real-time that the computer is following its rules correctly," and the operator is left with "an impossible task": a monitoring role with none of the practice that would let anyone actually perform it (Bainbridge, 1983, p. 776). Her remedy anticipates the cognitive-forcing-function literature by four decades: give the operator control "at a rate which the operator can follow, even when this may not be the most efficient method technically" (Bainbridge, 1983, p. 777). That is friction, chosen deliberately over throughput.

This is where the dilemma sharpens into an argument, not just a trade-off. One tradition treats interface friction as harmful by default. Behavioral economics' account of "sludge" names exactly this: friction that a person, on reflection, would not have chosen for herself (Sunstein, 2021; Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). Another tradition, the one behind cognitive forcing functions, treats a great deal of friction as protective. Neither can be adjudicated by asking users what they prefer, because preference and benefit run in opposite directions in exactly the cases that matter: Buçinca's own participants disliked the intervention that helped them. Nor can it be settled by designer intent, since intent is precisely what a user cannot see and a platform has every commercial reason to misstate.

Shannon Vallor's account of moral deskilling supplies a criterion that does not depend on either. Her argument, worked out across cases in medicine, media use, and lethal autonomous weapons, is that a tool deskills when it "obviates the need for us to employ our own skillful moral judgment, rather than aiding us in its successful exercise" (Vallor, 2015, p. 113). The decisive case for this paper's purposes is her example of a robotic patient lift: a nurse operating the machine "is hardly engaged in skillful caring if [her] attention throughout must be devoted to monitoring the machine controls and not the patient—even if the end result is a safe and efficient lift" (Vallor, 2015, p. 120). Notice what this case does *not* involve. The lift's objective is not a third party's; it is the nurse's own goal and the patient's own good, pursued with no independent objective anywhere in the loop. And the design still deskills, because it captures the attention that caring practice requires. Her media-use case cuts the same way from the other side: lockout apps that block a phone during work hours serve the user's own stated objective, and she still judges them "an imperfect solution," asking "why must we choose between tools that debilitate us and digital lockboxes to keep us away from these tools?" (Vallor, 2015, p. 118). Two cases, two verdicts, and *whose objective is being served* predicts neither one. What predicts both is whether the arrangement keeps the person exercising the judgment in question. Vallor's criterion, in other words, is a formation test, not an objective test, and it is not reducible to the structural or objective axes this paper's construct already supplies. It is a third, independent question: does this friction keep the person practiced in the judgment she needs, or does it merely tax her?

The two tests do not compete for the same job. The structural and objective axes answer a designer's first question: is a third, unset goal in this coordination loop at all, such that a pause here would be doing adversarial work rather than merely slowing someone down? Vallor's formation criterion answers the second question. Given that some interruption is called for, does this particular one preserve the practice it disrupts, or only tax it? Crossing the two yields four cells, not one continuum. An interruption that serves no outside goal and preserves practice is what a translator's pause looks like: costly, sometimes, but not adversarial, and worth teaching rather than removing (the patient lift, absent a competing objective). One that serves no outside goal and does not preserve practice is Vallor's lockout app: well-intentioned, and still the wrong tool. One that serves a third party's goal and preserves practice is a cognitive forcing function done right, Buçinca's decide-first interfaces, disliked and effective at once, because the dislike is the cost of practicing a judgment a platform would rather the user never exercised. And an interruption that serves a third party's goal without preserving anything is friction as theater: a confirmation dialog that interrupts without teaching, present only so a platform can say a warning was shown. A designer holding only the first test cannot tell the third cell from the fourth. One holding only Vallor's cannot tell the first from the third, because both look, from the user's chair, like the same unwelcome pause. Neither test alone recommends the modular chunking already documented as the strongest single empirical result in this design space: a controlled study found that decomposing a complex AI task into scoped, sequential steps cut aimless re-prompting from 51 percent of sessions to 36 percent while nearly doubling active editing of the output, entirely by restructuring the interface rather than by adding any interruption at all (Wu et al., 2022). Some designs need less friction and more legibility. The two-by-two rule is what tells a designer which.

## 5. What value-sensitive design cannot decide on its own

A natural objection is that value-sensitive design already gives designers a process for exactly this kind of judgment, and does not need a formal instrument imported from a theory of consciousness to do it. The objection understates what VSD's own literature says about its limits.

Value-sensitive design distinguishes stakeholders as direct, those who interact with a system, and indirect, those merely affected by it, and asks designers to run conceptual, empirical, and technical investigations across both (Friedman & Kahn, 2006). This is a genuine methodology, and a genuine complement to the design rule in §4. VSD decides *whom* a seam or a friction serves; the two tests decide *what* the friction should do once that is settled. But VSD's own critics, writing inside the tradition rather than against it, identify a gap the construct in this paper closes rather than merely restates. Manders-Huits (2011) observes that VSD "offers no methodological account for distinguishing genuine moral values from mere preferences," and names "initiators and potential customers" as exactly the stakeholders whose preferences risk masquerading as values. Borning and Muller (2012), one of them a VSD co-founder, concede that the explicitly supported values in any VSD project are set by whoever commissions it, and that power itself sits "orthogonal" to the direct/indirect distinction. JafariNaimi and colleagues (2015) push furthest: on VSD's own definition, "self-interest is a value" too, so a platform operator's extraction interest enters a VSD process with exactly the same standing as a driver's autonomy.

The historical case that shows what this gap costs in practice predates platforms by three decades. Batya Friedman and Helen Nissenbaum's 1996 analysis of bias in computer systems studied the National Resident Matching Program, an algorithm that assigns medical residents to hospital programs and, by design, prevents residents and programs from negotiating directly once the algorithm has run. The system interprets both parties' ranked preferences, commits a determination that binds both, and the operator defended the arrangement as neutral "passive facilitation," a description Friedman and Nissenbaum's analysis does not accept (Friedman & Nissenbaum, 1996). The construct in this paper gives that case a name the field did not yet have. A direct/indirect stakeholder distinction cannot capture it, because the NRMP's own operator and the residents it matches are both, on the field's own terms, direct stakeholders, and the distinction was never built to register who *co-determines* an outcome rather than merely who receives one. That is precisely the distinction Φ was built to draw.

VSD's own contestability strand comes closest to closing this gap from the inside, and is worth naming rather than folding silently into the affordances already discussed. Designing for contestability treats a decision subject's ability to challenge a determination as a design requirement in its own right, not an afterthought (Hirsch et al., 2017; Alfrink et al., 2023). But every contestability framework surveyed for this paper inherits a controller-and-subject dyad from the field it emerged from. One party decides, one party contests, and the co-determined *counterpart* on the other side of the intermediary (the customer whose interests also ran through the same determination as the driver's) appears in none of them. A design framework built for the construct in this paper has to widen contestability's own frame before it can use it, not merely cite it.

## 6. The politics of the arrangement

Return to why a proposal built from interpretability and provenance reaches for the wrong remedy: not merely a technically insufficient one, but one aimed at the wrong location of the problem.

Langdon Winner distinguished two ways an artifact can be political: the contested case, where a specific design choice settles an argument some group would rather have had, and a second, less remarked case, where a technology "appear[s] to require, or to be strongly compatible with, particular kinds of political relationships" simply to operate at all (Winner, 1980, p. 123). His clean example is the atom bomb: "as long as it exists at all, its lethal properties demand that it be controlled by a centralized, rigidly hierarchical chain of command... there is no other way" (Winner, 1980, p. 131). This second claim generalizes further than Winner's own examples suggest, and further than most of his critics have tested. A coordination form with Φ greater than zero requires the intermediary's authority to function at all; remove the intermediary and the coordination it was carrying collapses. That is Winner's own modality, practical rather than logical necessity, applied to a structural relation rather than an object.

But the generalization also breaks at a specific point worth naming rather than papering over. Winner's authority is a hierarchy *among humans*: a captain over a crew, a technoscientific elite over a public, one person's command binding another's obedience. The triadic construct in this paper requires something else: not a chain of command but a hub, a single point both parties must pass through, with neither commanding the other. Winner never distinguishes "must be run by a chain of command" from "must pass through a single point," and the two are not the same demand. His theory of inherently political artifacts, built to explain nuclear plants and railroads, has no native vocabulary for a mediator whose authority runs *sideways*, over two nominal equals, rather than down a hierarchy neither occupies.

Georg Simmel supplies the vocabulary Winner lacks, from a source a century older than either Winner or the platforms this paper is about. Simmel's sociology of the group distinguishes three roles a third party can occupy relative to two others: the *mediator*, who "seeks to eliminate himself, and only to bring to pass that the two disunited... parties may unite directly"; the *arbitrator*, in whom "this ultimate decision" has been placed, becoming, in Simmel's phrase, "a person"; and the *tertius gaudens*, the third who profits from the other two's division, whose leverage depends entirely on the fact that "the energies of two antagonistic elements paralyze each other" (Simmel, 1902, pp. 158, 177). A platform interposed between a driver and a customer is not, in Simmel's terms, a mediator working himself out of a job. It is a *tertius gaudens*, or, where it actively engineers the parties' inability to deal directly, the more deliberate form Simmel calls *divide et impera*: "the third instigates the difference intentionally, in order to gain a controlling situation" (Simmel, 1902, p. 187). This reframes a worry that §4's design rule leaves open. Adversarial design meant to help two co-determined parties assumes they face a common antagonist, but the two humans in this construct may be adversaries of *each other* (a driver against a customer, a prescriber's judgment against a pharmacist's caution) with the intermediary profiting from exactly that division. Simmel's own logic answers the worry rather than deepening it: a *tertius gaudens*'s advantage lasts only so long as the two parties fail to combine. Adversarial design's target, in a triadic coordination form, is not confronting the mediator directly. It is restoring the two parties' ability to recognize their situation and deal with each other, a different design brief than exposing the algorithm ever was.

## 7. Conclusion

A remedy built from mechanistic interpretability and content provenance answers the wrong question. Both technologies improve what one party can know about one artifact. Neither changes who profits from two other parties' inability to reach each other, because neither was built to notice that anyone besides the model's operator and the asset's validator is in the room. The construct this paper develops names what they miss: a person coordinates with another person through an intermediary that interprets both, binds both, and pursues an objective neither set, and almost nothing in the design-ethics or human-factors literatures has a place for it. Three real cases, none built to illustrate the point, show the two axes of that construct coming apart in the world: a suppressed radio channel with no objective of its own, a 1980s coordination tool that binds two coworkers without interpreting either, and a gig platform with both. Sorted against a structural test and a formation test rather than one, the long argument between transparency and friction resolves into a design rule, not a preference. And the politics of the arrangement is not a hierarchy any interpretability tool could expose. It is a hub two people cannot see past each other.

The print-era analogy the report that prompted this paper wanted to draw is not wrong so much as pointed the wrong direction. Print-era censorship controlled what one reader could access from one text. The problem this paper describes is not access to a text. It is standing between two readers who cannot yet see that they are being kept apart, and calling that arrangement a service.
