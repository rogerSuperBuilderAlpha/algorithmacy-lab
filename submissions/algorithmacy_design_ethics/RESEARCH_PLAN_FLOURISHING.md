# Flourishing pass — research plan for the restructured "Prevailing Frameworks" and the protect/promote argument

Written 2026-09-19, after the author's decision to (1) restrict "The Prevailing Frameworks" to five
canonical design-ethics accounts — Winner, Vallor, Verbeek, Value Sensitive Design, and Nissenbaum's
Contextual Integrity — (2) move Martela & Steger and Berthon out of the critiqued list and into
supporting roles later in the paper, and (3) add a second, properly researched diagnosis: that design
ethics across all five accounts is built to *protect* people and underweights *promoting* their
flourishing. `RESEARCH_DOSSIER.md` (§§1–10) and `DRAFT.md` are the context this plan assumes;
`RESEARCH_PLAN.md` and `RESEARCH_PLAN_TERMINOLOGY.md` stand and are not superseded.

The pass is small: three parallel Fable research agents, roughly eight new cards and a dozen
targeted re-reads of already-verified cards, and a single re-check step rather than an S2 batch. The
planning read settled more than I expected. Two of the four hypotheses below already moved — one
toward the author's claim (the guideline skew is real and quantified), one away from it (the
Nissenbaum "collapse" argument, in the form the brief states it, is answered in her own 2011 text and
must be reformulated). The agents start from what §1 records, not from the brief's guesses.

Facts marked **(planner-verified)** were checked today against Crossref, Open Library, the arXiv
preprint text, or an open version of the text; **[check]** marks what remains open. One warning

## 1. The four items — question, hypothesis, sources

### 1.1 Nissenbaum's Contextual Integrity

**Question.** What is Nissenbaum's own clearest statement of the contextual-integrity remedy, and does
the paper's collapse argument — that a proprietary, adaptive mediator defines and redefines the
"context" and its norms, non-negotiably — hold against her text, or does it need reformulating?

**Hypothesis, corrected on the planning read.** The naive form of the collapse argument fails, and
it fails on a sentence Nissenbaum wrote in 2011 specifically to block it. The *Daedalus* essay, which
is open access at amacad.org and which I read today, says: "The Net does not constitute (drawing on
the terminology of contextual integrity) a discrete context. It is not a single social realm, but the
totality of experience conducted via the Net," and it resists "the notion" that "online" is a
distinctive venue **(planner-verified, wording; page [check])**. Contexts on her account are social
spheres in Walzer's sense — health care, education, friendship, commerce — not platforms, and a
platform that calls itself "a university, a shoe store, a church" thereby "invite[s] evaluation
against respective norms" **(planner-verified, wording)**. So "the mediator defines the context" is
not a move her theory permits; she would say the dispatch algorithm operates *within* the context of
employment and answers to employment's entrenched informational norms.

The defensible critique is narrower, and I think stronger, and it has three parts the pass must test
against the 2004 article and the 2010 book rather than assume.

*First, the unit of analysis.* Contextual integrity is a benchmark for **flows of information about a
person**: "I posit two types of informational norms: norms of appropriateness, and norms of flow or
distribution. Contextual integrity is maintained when both types of norms are upheld, and it is
violated when either of the norms is violated" (2004, pre-publication draft; VoR page **[check]**).
Its parties are a data subject, a sender, and a recipient. It has no term for a mediator whose
*determination* — a match, a rank, a price, a schedule — binds a second human party. The NRMP case in
`friedman1996.md`, which Nissenbaum co-authored, is the structure the construct names, and contextual
integrity would classify it as a set of flows (student's rank list to the program) with nothing to say
about the binding determination that follows. That is a remedy-locus finding of exactly the kind the
RQ12 census records, and the pass must record it: **Remedy locus: norms / institution** (courts,
policy, the "entrenched normative framework"), never the relation.

*Second, the presumption and who overrides it.* Nissenbaum herself names the conservatism worry —
"contextual integrity is conservative in possibly detrimental ways" — and answers it with "a
presumption in favor of the status quo" that "does not, however, rule out the possibility of a
successful challenge where adequate reasons exist": entrenched norms are "compared with novel
practices that breach or threaten them, and judged worth preserving, or not, in terms of how well they
promote not only values and goods internal to a given context, but also fundamental social, political,
and moral values" (2004 draft; **[check]** VoR pages). The 2011 essay compresses this into a decision
heuristic — "locate contexts, explicate entrenched informational norms, identify disruptive flows, and
evaluate these flows against norms based on general ethical and political principles as well as
context-specific purposes and values" — and adds, for novel cases, "starting with ends, purposes, and
values and working from there back to norms" **(planner-verified, wording)**. The critique the paper
can run is that this heuristic has no agent. It is addressed to a court, a regulator, or a philosopher
evaluating a practice; the person inside the flow has no instrument for invoking it, and an adaptive
mediator does not present a *novel practice* to be evaluated once — it presents a practice that
changes faster than the heuristic can be run. Schot & Rip's unsecured feedback step (`schotrip1997.md`)
is the precedent: the method assumes a moment at which the practice holds still.

*Third, and this is the ally reading the pass should not miss:* the Walzer passage is the paper's own
mechanism stated by Nissenbaum. "Walzer considers it a form of tyranny when goods of one sphere
intrude into, or become dominant in, not only one sphere but many; local norms that embody the settled
rationale of the tyrannized sphere are overturned as those who possess vast amounts of dominant goods
are able to exert tyrannical power over those who do not" (2004 draft; **[check]** VoR page). A
platform that mediates employment, credit, housing and speech at once is that dominant good. Her
remedy is to "initially resist breaches" — protective, by construction — which is the protect/promote
finding of §1.2 arriving from inside the account.

For the protect/promote question specifically: contextual integrity is a privacy theory and is
therefore protective at the level of the person by definition. Its one promotive element is at the
level of the *context* — norms are justified by "ends, purposes, and values" of the sphere (health,
education) — a teleology of institutions, not of the person's flourishing. The pass should say so
rather than let the paper overclaim that Nissenbaum has no promotive term at all.

**One disambiguation the draft needs, like the Simmel one.** The library already holds a Nissenbaum
whose remedy locus is *relation* — `bruntonnissenbaum2015.md`, TrackMeNot and AdNauseam, cited for
Principle I. The paper critiques the Contextual-Integrity Nissenbaum and cites the Obfuscation
Nissenbaum, and must say in one sentence that these are two answers by one author to the same
asymmetry, the second of which she wrote because the first has no user-side instrument. The pass's
`nissenbaum2004.md` Relation section carries that sentence.

### 1.2 The meta-analytic anchor for "AI/design ethics skews toward harm-avoidance over beneficence"

**Question.** Does a real systematic source show that AI-ethics guidance foregrounds harm-avoidance
over the promotion of flourishing, or is that the author's impression?

**Hypothesis, confirmed in a bounded form.** Jobin, Ienca & Vayena (2019) report exactly this, and
quantify it. Table 2 of the preprint gives, for 84 documents, the number mentioning each of eleven
principles: transparency 73, justice & fairness 68, non-maleficence 60, responsibility 60, privacy 47,
**beneficence 41**, freedom & autonomy 34, trust 28, sustainability 14, dignity 13, solidarity 6
**(planner-verified from the extracted preprint text; VoR table [check])**. Their own sentence:
"References to non-maleficence outweigh those to beneficence by a factor of 1.5," and, under
Beneficence, "While promoting good (beneficence in ethical terms) is often mentioned, it is rarely
defined, though notable exceptions mention … the promotion of human well-being and flourishing"
**(planner-verified, preprint wording)**. Non-maleficence sits in the five principles found "in more
than half of all the sources"; beneficence does not. A second finding in the same section is worth
the draft's attention: the authors split autonomy into "a positive freedom, specifically the freedom
to flourish" and "negative freedom, for example freedom from technological experimentation,
manipulation or surveillance," which is the protect/promote distinction drawn inside the guidelines'
own vocabulary.

Two limits the draft must state. The unit is *mentions across documents*, not weight within a
document, so "1.5" is a count of guidelines that name the principle, not a measure of emphasis. And
the corpus is guidelines — corporate, governmental, professional — not the five scholarly accounts the
paper critiques. Jobin et al. anchor the draft's opening claim that "in mainstream engineering
discourse, design ethics is routinely reduced to a negative mandate"; they cannot be cited as evidence
about Winner, Vallor, Verbeek, VSD or Nissenbaum, whose orientation the fairness matrix in pass F3 has
to establish account by account.

**Mittelstadt (2019) is relevant, not a tangent, on one point.** His abstract states that AI
development lacks, relative to medicine, "(1) common aims and fiduciary duties, (2) professional
history and norms, (3) proven methods to translate principles into practice, and (4) robust legal and
professional accountability mechanisms" **(planner-verified, arXiv abstract)**. The first of the four
is the explanation for Jobin's "rarely defined": beneficence in bioethics is owed by a fiduciary to a
patient, and the developer of a mediator owes the mediated party nothing of the kind — which is also
what `delacroix2019.md` (undivided loyalty; an interested intermediary cannot be the user's fiduciary)
already says from the data-trust side. Card it for that sentence and the "ethics washing" point; do
not let it grow into a section.

### 1.3 The bioethics origin of the non-maleficence/beneficence distinction

**Question.** Does the paper need Beauchamp & Childress at all, given that Jobin et al. and
Mittelstadt both already say the AI principles "closely resemble the four classic principles of
medical ethics"?

**Hypothesis.** Not for the distinction itself — Mittelstadt's abstract makes the bioethics lineage
explicit and Floridi & Cowls (2019) is the AI-side text that borrowed it. Card Beauchamp & Childress
only if the draft makes the stronger, inherited-skew claim: that bioethics itself ranks
non-maleficence as more stringent than beneficence and treats much of beneficence as ideal rather than
obligatory, so that AI ethics inherited a tilt it did not choose. I believe the book says something
of this shape in its non-maleficence and beneficence chapters, but I have not read those chapters for
this question and the pass must not attribute it without reading them **[check]**. If the draft does
not make the inheritance claim, the book is a reference-list entry reached through Mittelstadt and
needs no card.

**Sources.** Beauchamp, T. L., & Childress, J. F. (2019). *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (8th ed.).
Oxford University Press **(planner-verified via Open Library: editions in 1979, 1983, 1989, 1994,
2001, 2009, 2013, 2019; the 2019 printing is the 8th [check ISBN — 9780190640873 is listed and is
probably the 8th])**. The 7th (2013) is also widely cited; use the 8th if cited at all. Read only the
openings of the non-maleficence and beneficence chapters (chapter numbers **[check]**) for the
stringency and obligatory/ideal distinctions.

### 1.4 The fairness check on Vallor

**Question.** Does Vallor's operationalization of the technomoral virtues deliver a flourishing
orientation in design terms, or does it cash out as harm-avoidance and resistance in flourishing
language? This decides whether the new section can list her as an instance or must treat her as the
account that comes closest to seeing the problem.

**Hypothesis, sharpened from the verified cards.** Vallor's *telos* is flourishing and her *design
demands* are protective, and the gap between the two is the paper's existing remedy-locus critique
seen from the other side. The evidence already in the library points that way. The 2016 book is a
eudaimonist virtue ethic whose twelve virtues (honesty, self-control, humility, justice, courage,
empathy, care, civility, flexibility, perspective, magnanimity, technomoral wisdom; taxonomy at p. 119
per `vallor2016.md`) are qualities of a life well lived, not defences; nothing in that list is a
non-maleficence principle. But when she turns to what design or policy should *do*, the demands are
protective: the 2015 paper says "values by design" and "participatory design" "will not take us all
the way" (p. 122, verified) and asks for a cultural shift; *The AI Mirror* names the programme as
"contestability, and redress, clear accountability for AI-driven harms, funding of global AI risk
observatories, and above all, the low-hanging fruit of enforcing people's existing legal protections
and human rights" (2024, p. 196, snippet) and lists its ends as "social fairness and justice … privacy
and autonomy … transparency and accountability" (p. 170, per Hudson). The promotive work — the
"future worth wanting" — is assigned to the cultivated person. So the honest placement is: Vallor is
the account that *has* the flourishing vocabulary and delivers it through the subject; the design
instruments she endorses are the protective ones. That is a partial exception, and the section is
stronger for saying so than for pretending she is a clean instance.

Two passages must be read whole before the section can say this: the book's definition of
flourishing and the "future worth wanting" (Introduction and ch. 1–2, pages **[check]**), and the 2024
pp. 170–171 and 196 passages, which every prior pass has only in snippets or reviewers' transcriptions.
Both books have defeated three access routes (Oxford Academic 403; Google Books search-inside
CAPTCHA-blocked; Books API quota). The fourth route is the author's own institution — Bentley's
Oxford Scholarship Online / Oxford Academic subscription, or a physical copy — and the pass should
say plainly if that also fails and leave the section's Vallor paragraph hedged at snippet depth.

**Sources.** `vallor2016.md`, `vallor2015.md`, `vallor2024.md` (re-reads; upgrade if full text is
reached). No new cards.

### 1.5 The two re-reads the brief already scopes: VSD and the Floridi school

**VSD.** The charge sticks to the founding text and slips on the AI-era text, and the pass must
report both. `friedman1996.md` is titled "Bias in computer systems," its remedies run through
designers and the professional community, and its whole apparatus is the avoidance of "systematic
and unfair discrimination" — protective. `friedman2006.md`'s thirteen-value heuristic includes human
welfare (the list **[check]** in the card's Table 1), and Borning & Muller's reply to the
naturalistic-fallacy charge is phrased, per `manders2011.md`'s Caution, as "if we want to support
human flourishing, then …" — a conditional the pass should locate in `borning2012.md` or in Borning &
Muller's text **[check]**. The complication is `umbrello2021.md`: Umbrello & van de Poel distinguish
"values promoted by a design" (drawn from the UN SDGs) from "values respected by it" (the EU HLEG's
autonomy, non-harm, fairness, explicability), with respected values as "constraints or boundary
conditions" that set "the deontological constraints that any design must meet to be ethically
(minimally) acceptable" (verified). That is the protect/promote distinction drawn *inside* VSD, with
the protective values as the floor and the promotive ones as goals. It supports the diagnosis in one
way — the promoted values are imported from outside the method (the SDGs), and the method's own
instruments are the constraints — and complicates it in another: a reviewer who knows this paper will
not accept "VSD has no promotive term." The section should cite it as VSD's own admission that
promotion is borrowed. Add to the three Friedman cards the `Remedy locus:` field they currently lack
(see §3), because the census must now cover VSD.

**Floridi.** Scoped in §1.2 above.

**IEEE.** `ieee2019.md` is *Ethically Aligned Design: A Vision for Prioritizing Human Well-being*, and
its second General Principle makes well-being "a primary success criterion" (verified). It is not one
of the five accounts, but it is the one design-ethics document in the library whose title is
promotive, and the pass F3 memo should say in two sentences whether its well-being principle is
operationalized anywhere in the 294 pages the card sampled (the card's own count — "contest" twice,
"recourse" three times — suggests the operative sections are still protective). The IEEE P7010
well-being-metrics standard is the obvious follow-up; I did not verify its record today and it is
paywalled **[check; optional]**.

## 4. Restructuring "Prevailing Frameworks," and where the moved material goes

**Five accounts, each with the steel-man / prescriptive-limit pair, at two weights.** Winner, Vallor

Nissenbaums" sentence attached. Five pairs at 300–450 words each is 1,800–2,000 words, which the

## 6. The single biggest risk to the new argument

Verbeek is the account most likely to already carry a promotive orientation that a reviewer will
raise against a clean "harm reduction, not flourishing" charge across all five — and the risk is not
that he mentions the good life in passing but that his book is framed against the protective stance
by design. *Moralizing Technology* opens by rejecting a "humanist" ethics whose job is to defend the
autonomous subject against technology and proposes instead to "accompany" technological mediation,
which is why chapter 4 asks for "techniques of technology use" through which one's relations "acquire
a desirable shape" and chapter 5 endorses moralizing artifacts — the water-saving showerhead, persuasive
feedback — to steer behaviour toward good ends (`verbeek2011.md`, pp. 83–87, 95, snippets). That is a
promotive design ethics in form, and the paper cannot say he lacks one. What the paper can say, and
what the fairness matrix must establish on the text, is that the promotion Verbeek designs for is the
designer's conception of the good, materialized once, with one user in front of it — the arm's own
adaptivity finding (fixed, not adaptive; no second party on the page) restated as a finding about
whose flourishing the moralized artifact serves. Vallor is the second risk for the opposite reason —
her telos is flourishing and her instruments are protective, which the section can state as a partial
exception — and VSD is the third because Umbrello & van de Poel already draw the promote/respect
distinction inside the method. The reformulation that survives all three is not "the five accounts
are protective" but "whatever the account's telos, none of the five assigns the promotive work to the
design of the relation between the two coordinated people" — the same shape as the census finding,
which is what makes it a second diagnosis of one error rather than a separate complaint. If F3 finds
that Verbeek's chapter 4 assigns that work to the relation, the section loses its "all five" and the
paper says "four of five, and the fifth designs for one person's good," which is still an argument.
