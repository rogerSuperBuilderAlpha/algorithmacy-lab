**Title:** Accountability Needs Three Places: AI Systems That Judge Two People

## Abstract
Algorithmic accountability has inherited a two-place form. Bovens (2007) defines accountability as a relation between an actor and a forum: the actor must explain and justify its conduct, and the forum can question it, pass judgement and impose consequences. Proposals for accountable AI keep this form when they turn to the people a system affects. Contestability frameworks give a decision subject the chance to challenge a decision before a human controller (Alfrink et al., 2023). Stakeholders, asked what contestability requires, mostly describe an appeal after the fact (Lyons et al., 2021). In each case one person faces one system, or the people who answer for it.

Many AI systems do not stand in that relation. A booking platform ranks a host partly on guests' reviews, and a marketplace demotes a seller on buyers' ratings. An AI appraisal tool can draft what a manager tells an employee. In each case the system evaluates the conduct of two people who depend on each other, and its verdict binds both. This paper argues that such systems stand in a three-place relation, and that two-place accountability, applied to them, misplaces responsibility.

The argument has three steps. The first is logical. Peirce held that a genuinely triadic relation cannot be analysed into dyadic ones: "a triad cannot be analyzed into dyads" (Peirce, c. 1890, CP 1.363). Whatever the final status of his reduction thesis, which later work shows to depend on how definability is construed, it gives a precise test. A verdict about two people's joint conduct is triadic if it cannot be rebuilt from two separate verdicts, one about each person. A ranking that combines a guest's review with the platform's weighting of it fails that test. So does a seller's standing that depends on a buyer's rating and on the operator's rule.

The second step is sociological. Simmel's analysis of the third shows what changes when a third decides. The arbitrator ends the parties' control over their own conflict. A third can also benefit from the parties' division, and can produce that division in order to dominate (Simmel, 1950, pp. 154, 162). Platforms occupy these positions. In Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024, p. 14) terms, they leverage the agency of participants rather than delegating to them, and accountability becomes "distributed, deflected, and denied".

The third step is normative. Two-place mechanisms hear each party alone. A host may contest a ranking, and a guest may complain about a host, but no forum hears the joint conduct that the verdict judged. Responsibility then settles on whichever party is nearest the outcome. Elish (2019) calls this the moral crumple zone, in which the nearest human absorbs the blame for a system over which they had limited control. In a triad the crumple zone has two candidates, and the operator can choose between them. Better explanations do not remedy this, because disclosure is not the same as traceability (Kroll, 2021), and an explanation given to one party still leaves the other unheard. Nor does a right of appeal, which lets each party reopen its own verdict but not the joint one. The host who wins an appeal against a ranking leaves the guest's review, and the platform's weighting of it, exactly where they were.

The paper concludes that accountability for AI systems that judge two people needs a three-place form. It needs a forum before which both parties and the operator answer together, with a record that both parties can read. It also needs a standard of justification that addresses the joint conduct, not each party's conduct separately. The paper sets out the minimal conditions for such a forum and asks which existing institutions come close: two-sided review disputes, mediation services in online dispute resolution, and labour tribunals.
