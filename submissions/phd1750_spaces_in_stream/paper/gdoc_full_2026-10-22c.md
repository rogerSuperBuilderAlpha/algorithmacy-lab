**Core Thesis.** Commercial generative AI interfaces create a *literacy trap*, treating users as passive readers of a machine-paced digital *scriptio continua*. While commercial platforms occasionally pause generation for plan approvals, permission prompts, or interactive menus, they place these stops to maintain platform throughput and minimize user friction rather than support decision-making. I propose *algorithmacy*—the procedural and perceptual competence required to navigate, evaluate, and steer opaque, co-optive, triadic intermediaries. At its perceptual layer, algorithmacy depends on interface marking of *decision spaces*: the latent points in an automated computational process where supervisory intervention is functionally necessary to control system intent.

### **Spaces in the Stream: Algorithmacy and the Perception of Decision Spaces**

#### **Abstract**

Conversational interfaces for large language models are engineered around an implicit cognitive premise: that supervising generative systems is fundamentally an act of literacy. By outputting tokens incrementally as an advancing visual stream, current interfaces simulate the temporal emergence of static text, positioning the user as a serial proofreader scanning prose. Platforms now pause the stream for plan approvals, permission prompts, and clarifying menus, but they set these decision points to serve the platform's optimization goals rather than the user's oversight. This theoretical article contends that treating algorithmic supervision as reading constitutes a fundamental category error. In classical antiquity, continuous script (*scriptio continua*) mimicked the acoustic cadence of speech, chaining the visual system to the auditory loops of oracy and delaying the emergence of autonomous, silent literacy until scribes introduced the space between words. Today, streaming conversational interfaces trap users in a parallel "literacy trap"—formatting high-dimensional computation to resemble the linear emergence of print. This design depletes working memory through surface-level textual decoding, conflates syntactic fluency with validity, and obscures latent algorithmic branching. To resolve this skeuomorphic limitation, I formulate *algorithmacy*: the competency required to navigate, evaluate, and steer triadic, opaque, and co-optive algorithmic environments. I examine the computational equivalent of the scribal word space, define *decision spaces* as the latent points in an automated process where human intervention is functionally necessary to control intent and anticipate counterpart dynamics, and identify interface interventions that make these spaces perceptible. Modeling supervisory navigation as the joint operation of perceptual detection and the user's mental model of the intermediary, I articulate six formal propositions, derive thirteen directional predictions, specify a three-condition eye-tracking research agenda, and outline interaction design principles for generative interfaces that transcend literate reading to establish structural oversight.

**Keywords:** Algorithmacy; Decision spaces; Text streaming; Cognitive ergonomics; Eye movements; Literacy trap; Scriptio continua; Automation supervision.

### **1. Introduction**

When an artificial intelligence system generates a response in contemporary conversational interfaces, it does not provide a completed communicative artifact; instead, it outputs text incrementally, token by token, scrolling downward in real time. Industry discourse routinely frames this presentation style as a natural accommodation of turn-taking dynamics or an engineering compromise to mask real-time compute latency. In practice, however, this dynamic display functions as an engineered simulation of the sensorimotor process of reading. The interface constructs an animated illusion of human thought taking written shape, commanding the user's visual system to track an automated cursor across the visual field. In empirical investigations evaluating eye movements during real-time generation, this continuous display fundamentally disrupts typical oculomotor routines: bottom-generated scrolling text inflates total fixation counts, prolongs cumulative gaze duration, and broadens saccadic amplitudes, with initial fixations tightly clustered at the kinetic leading edge where new tokens emerge (Park & Lee, 2024). Rather than letting human vision function as an autonomous, self-paced sensory engine that allocates attention across a stable spatial array, the interface constrains the eye to an externally paced pursuit task determined by server computation (Liu et al., 2024; Xiao & Yang, 2025). In effect, the interface compels the user to perform a prescribed reading.

Conceptualizing algorithmic supervision as an act of reading constitutes a significant category error. Reading is a cultural technology developed to decode static, dyadic inscriptions: inanimate texts that transmit an author's meaning across space and time without autonomous intervention. When an individual reads a printed volume, document, or static report, the orthographic marks remain unchanged; the medium lacks agency, and the reader's cognitive objectives center on linguistic comprehension, semantic synthesis, and internal reflection. In contrast, interacting with a generative language model is not a dyadic reading task, but an irreducible triadic coordination. The user engages with an active, autonomous, and opaque algorithmic intermediary that samples high-dimensional probability distributions, optimizes for proprietary programmatic objectives, and continuously executes computational state transitions. By packaging this computational process as an unbroken ribbon of text, conversational front-ends establish a "literacy trap". The human operator is cast in the role of an editor or proofreader, consuming working-memory buffers to evaluate grammatical coherence and surface plausibility. When the platform pauses the ribbon, the role shifts from proofreader to approver, but the task remains reading: the user reads a plan or menu the system has written and selects from it. This posture diverts finite executive resources into an uncompensated "verification tax" on hallucination-prone tokens, blinding the user to the system's underlying procedural logic, statistical branching, and epistemic boundaries (Kim et al., 2025; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

This interface pathology directly mirrors the historic civilizational transition from oracy to literacy. In classical Greco-Roman antiquity, manuscripts were inscribed in *scriptio continua*—continuous sequences of letters devoid of word spaces, punctuation, or paragraph divisions (Saenger, 1997). This format endured because people initially conceptualized writing not as an autonomous medium of thought, but as a passive acoustic transcription intended exclusively for oral recitation. The absence of visual boundary markers prevented the human visual system from segmenting syntax in parallel, forcing readers into slow, labor-intensive auditory loops in which they vocalized or subvocalized syllables aloud to infer word boundaries through sound. Consequently, the persistence of the oral script shackled cognition to the tools of oracy, severely constraining the emergence of autonomous, reflective, and silent literacy. That bottleneck was broken only when seventh-century Insular scribes introduced physical white space between words (Parkes, 1992; Saenger, 1997). By offloading lexical segmentation from internal working memory onto the parchment's physical white space, the scribal word space unhooked reading from vocal performance, unlocking modern silent reading and the vast cognitive architecture of literate culture.

Contemporary human-computer interaction is reversing this historical progression. By designing foundation model interfaces that mimic the linear progression of print, system designers are enforcing a digital *scriptio continua*. Users are expected to supervise probabilistic computation using traditional tools of textual literacy—such as editorial discernment, critical reading comprehension, post-hoc proofreading, and approval of system-written plans—to detect erroneous or misaligned outputs (Bhat, 2025; Kim et al., 2025). However, expecting users to secure algorithmic reliability through heightened vigilance and faster reading is cognitively equivalent to demanding that ancient monks sound out *scriptio continua* with greater volume and clearer diction. This approach misattributes an architectural interface flaw to a deficiency in human stamina. The solution lies not in enhanced literacy, but in developing *algorithmacy*: the cognitive competency to perceive, direct, and supervise autonomous algorithmic intermediaries within opaque, co-optive, triadic environments. Achieving algorithmacy requires identifying and visually rendering the latent *decision spaces* embedded within generative processes. These are the critical junctures where an algorithmic model commits to verifiable claims, executes computational branching, or shifts operational goals—points where human capabilities to control internal intent and imagine counterpart intent among otherwise idempotent, conversational content become indispensable.

This article establishes a strict analytical perimeter. The macro-sociological, labor, political, and ethical/moral layers of algorithmacy—including platform labor governance, market power asymmetries, and transformer training paradigms—are bracketed to focus exclusively on the perceptual layer of real-time interaction. This layer governs how visual attention, oculomotor routines, and event cognition detect and isolate operational decision spaces as text forms on the display. An essential boundary condition is established at the outset: marking a decision space is necessary but not sufficient for supervisory navigation. Just as inserting white space between words created the physiological conditions for silent lexical processing without guaranteeing semantic comprehension, marking an algorithmic stream indicates where the system makes a state-altering commitment without resolving how the human operator ought to respond. Perceptual marking provides the visual and temporal affordance the user needs to disengage from passive, fluent reading. Once a decision space is perceived, the user's capacity to act depends on their internal mental model of how the intermediary optimizes objectives, routes data, and transforms prompts. By isolating the perceptual mechanics of decision-space detection, this article identifies the interface conditions that let human operators conserve working memory otherwise spent monitoring idempotent conversational syntax, preserving cognitive capacity to steer intent and interrogate the intermediary's computational trajectory.

Current strategies to address generative overreliance and automation bias fall short because they treat the streaming response as an undifferentiated linguistic surface, overlooking the structural distinction between benign, idempotent token generation and state-altering commitments. Prevailing engineering and design paradigms intervene almost exclusively at the stream's periphery rather than within its evolving structure. Pacing interventions adjust token delivery velocity to balance computational load against subjective user reading comfort (Liu et al., 2024; Xiao & Yang, 2025; Zhu et al., 2026). Yet modulating delivery speed does not indicate where assertions demand empirical validation or where algorithmic branching diverges from human intent. Concurrently, human-factors research introduces artificial cognitive friction—such as mandatory delays, confirmation modals, or pre-task reflection prompts—to disrupt passive compliance (Arun et al., 2026; Buçinca et al., 2021; Collins et al., 2024; Cox et al., 2016). Because these obstacles are implemented outside the generative flow (either before model access or after task completion), they impose an indiscriminate cognitive tax on routine synthesis while leaving the generation process's internal decision points unexamined. Similarly, explainability dashboards and source-attribution panels provide retrospective evidence only after generation is complete (Kim et al., 2025; Tatasciore et al., 2026). These mechanisms position the human operator as a post-hoc auditor reviewing an inert transcript, rather than an active supervisor engaged in an ongoing triadic exchange. Commercial agents go furthest, stopping the stream for plan approval and multiple-choice questions. Their own documentation shows what these stops serve: the plan-approval menu leads with an option that also switches off later prompts, and the vendor presents its autonomous mode as a way of "reducing prompt fatigue" (Anthropic, 2026a). These are decision spaces specified for the platform's intent. Across human-computer interaction and automation engineering, I found no framework that identifies, models, or visually marks decision spaces within the generative stream where the user's intent is bound. The interface continues to present continuous textual output, depriving operators of the perceptual scaffolding needed to distinguish moments that require strategic intervention from routine conversational content.

To model how human supervisors perceive and navigate operational decision spaces in real time, this article introduces the propositions governing the perceptual layer of algorithmacy theory: (1) automated computational streams contain latent decision spaces that exist independently of interface representation, distinguishing critical operational commitments from otherwise idempotent generation; (2) continuous token generation induces stimulus-driven attentional capture toward the kinetic leading edge, thereby suppressing the saccadic regressions and cognitive event boundaries necessary for evaluating system trajectories; (3) presenting generative supervision as an act of reading co-opts human agency, leveraging surface syntactic fluency to obscure structural branching; (4) the introduction of \[structural marks - or whatever design solution I create later\] at semantic decision spaces restores visual saccadic targets, initiates event segmentation in working memory, and provides an unhurried window for users to verify claims, assert intent, and anticipate counterpart dynamics; (5) oculomotor fixation on a marked decision space is a necessary physical prerequisite for oversight, yet effective supervisory navigation depends on the joint operation of perceptual detection and the user's mental model of the intermediary; and (6) the efficacy of \[marking, or whatever\] is non-linearly sensitive to structural placement, temporal duration, and visual form.

Section 2 establishes the cognitive and psychophysical baseline by reviewing the historical transition from *scriptio continua* to word separation as an instance of cognitive offloading (2.1), the oculomotor mechanics of saccadic targeting and regressive repair (2.2), visual search priority maps under abrupt onsets (2.3), event segmentation theory and situation-model updating (2.4), and ecological perspectives on text as a spatially stable material object (2.5). ### **2. Foundational Concepts: Scriptio Continua, Reading, Attention, and Events**

Reading constitutes a cultural invention that operates on pre-existing evolutionary neural structures. Because writing emerged too recently in human history to shape the brain's genetic evolution, reading lacks an innate, dedicated biological organ. Instead, it relies on the neuronal recycling hypothesis as formalized by Dehaene and Cohen (2007). Under this framework, novel cultural activities use evolutionarily older brain circuits that are sufficiently plastic and functionally proximate to support new practices, occupying a "neuronal niche" whose ancestral architectural constraints are "never entirely erased" (pp. 384–385). In literate individuals, visual word recognition consistently occupies the left ventral occipitotemporal cortex, a region originally evolved for visual object and scene recognition. This process reorganizes the ventral visual pathway to enhance responses to orthographic characters, while face-recognition processing shifts to the right hemisphere (Dehaene & Cohen, 2007; Dehaene et al., 2015). Because reading repurposes rather than replaces primate visual hardware, linguistic meaning remains closely linked to the physical layout and spatial geometry of the visual display. The human eye processes text by coordinating ballistic oculomotor routines, including locating peripheral targets, executing stable fixations, and launching regressive saccades to address syntactic and interpretive breakdowns. In traditional reading environments, these oculomotor routines depend on a spatially invariant, static surface. Streaming generative interfaces disrupt this sensorimotor baseline by substituting a stable spatial field with an autonomously advancing, server-paced kinetic stream.

#### 2.1. Historical Precedents: Scriptio Continua, Word Separation, and Cognitive Offloading

In classical Greco-Roman antiquity, scribes developed literary texts using *scriptio continua*. This format presented text as an uninterrupted succession of letters, omitting word spaces, modern punctuation, and internal paragraph divisions. In standard Greek prose bookrolls, scribes arranged calligraphic characters into narrow columns of fifteen to twenty-five letters, relying primarily on an occasional horizontal stroke in the margin, known as the paragraphos, to indicate sentence transitions. Early Roman literary texts initially employed interpuncts to separate words, but imperial Latin scribes intentionally abandoned word division to emulate the esteemed Greek convention of seamless textual continuity (Johnson, 2000; Saenger, 1997). For decades, historical accounts attributed this practice to the belief that ancient readers could not read silently, suggesting that unspaced script compelled them to vocalize or subvocalize syllables to reconstruct word boundaries through phonological auditory loops (Saenger, 1997). Although philological evidence demonstrates that skilled ancient readers could and did read silently when necessary (Johnson, 2000; Knox, 1968), literary engagement remained predominantly an oral performance for an aristocratic elite. The bookroll's material format was meant to be vocalized and heard in real time rather than visually inspected and browsed. As a result, *scriptio continua* functioned as an interface that shifted the computational burden of lexical parsing entirely onto the reader's internal memory and acoustic processing, depriving the human visual system of the external spatial anchors required for rapid, autonomous, and reflective examination.

Regardless of how classicists resolve the debate over ancient vocalization, both perspectives converge on a fundamental ergonomic principle: any functional reading system must provide the visual apparatus with reliable structural landmarks to guide saccadic landing and maintain spatial orientation (Johnson, 2000). Modern eye-movement psychophysics clarifies the specific contributions of physical word spacing to sensorimotor coordination. When Rayner, Fischer, and Pollatsek (1998) systematically removed spaces from English text or replaced them with uninformative characters, reading rates among skilled adults declined by 44% to 54% for single sentences and by 58% for extended passages. Without interword spacing, readers no longer landed at their typical viewing position, just left of a word’s center, but instead clustered near word onsets. Additionally, regressions in sentence reading nearly doubled from 9.7% to 17.3% of all oculomotor events. Rayner and colleagues determined that word spaces serve two concurrent ergonomic functions: they accelerate lexical identification by delineating word boundaries and provide peripheral cues that enhance upcoming words as physical targets for ballistic saccades. When these targets are unavailable, readers do not compensate by distributing processing across multiple unspaced words. Instead, as Drieghe, Fitzsimmons, and Liversedge (2017) observed, the visual system actively searches for substitute optical boundaries, such as orthographic irregularities or script variations, to constrain its focus within a narrow processing window. In cognitive terms, the scribal space exemplifies the *representational effect* (Zhang & Norman, 1994) and *cognitive offloading* (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Without adding semantic information, it modifies the surface's physical geometry, shifting the computational burden of segmentation from internal working-memory calculation to rapid, pre-attentive visual processing.

A superficial counterargument immediately presents itself: modern conversational and agentic interfaces do not merely print spaces between words; they increasingly introduce explicit pauses, permission prompts, and plan-approval checkpoints. A developer using an agentic coding tool is routinely prompted to approve script execution, choose an implementation option, or review a proposed diff. Why, then, should ancient *scriptio continua* serve as an operative framework for generative AI?

This objection identifies an interactive interruption while ignoring its underlying structure. The platforms did add decision spaces, but they specified them for a different intent. The plan is displayed, and the user may edit it, yet the first option on the approval menu also turns off the prompts that would follow (Anthropic, 2026a). The default choice is idempotent in the sense that matters here: selecting it leaves the trajectory where the system had already set it. Current interface stops operate within a dyadic fiction: they ask the user to approve localized, downstream actions—authorizing a specific file write or confirming a command—as if the system were a transparent tool executing user-defined tasks. What they systematically omit is the triadic, co-optive intermediary. The critical algorithmic commitments—the pruning of alternative solution paths (one agent's planning instructions ask for "only your recommended approach, not all alternatives"; Ronacher, 2025) and the uninspected assumptions embedded in a generated plan—remain locked within the model's latent space, having occurred before the confirmation dialog appears. The user is invited to select from a system-authored multiple-choice menu about what they are doing, but is denied any perceptual mechanism to locate where the intermediary has bound their intent. The structural failure of *scriptio continua* persists: just as ancient continuous script lacked the macro-structural white space needed to isolate units of thought, modern generative streams visually lack the algorithmic white space required to inspect and steer the intermediary's latent decision spaces. 

This historical analogy has distinct operational limitations, and clarifying these boundaries reinforces algorithmacy's theoretical foundation. First, while classical and medieval readers controlled their reading speed and visual navigation, users of generative language models experience an externally regulated process determined by server computation cycles and network bandwidth (Liu et al., 2024; Xiao & Yang, 2025). Second, historical research shows that silent reading predates the widespread use of interword spacing; proficient ancient readers could engage in silent reading when necessary (Johnson, 2000; Knox, 1968). Scribes' introduction of word spacing did not create the neurological basis for silent reading or guarantee textual comprehension. Instead, it significantly transformed reading's cognitive demands by shifting responsibility for lexical segmentation from internal working-memory phonological processes to pre-attentive visual mechanisms (Rayner et al., 1998; Risko & Gilbert, 2016; Zhang & Norman, 1994).  The theoretical assertion presented here maintains a comparable scope. Marking decision spaces within a generative stream does not turn an uncritical operator into an expert supervisor, nor does it replace human domain expertise. Instead, it changes the interface's representational structure, shifting the cognitive effort of identifying critical, state-changing decisions from overburdened working-memory resources to the interface's visual design. Analogous to how introducing spaces between words established a perceptual foundation for silent, independent reading without ensuring literacy, marking decision spaces offers essential perceptual support for algorithmacy without prescribing the human supervisor's actions.

#### 2.2. Oculomotor Mechanics: Saccade Targets, Regressions, and Structural Repair

Physiologically, reading alternates between discrete fixations, during which the eye remains relatively stationary to extract visual information, and ballistic saccades, rapid conjugate jumps that reposition the fovea across the text (Rayner, 1998). In skilled adult readers of alphabetic orthographies, fixations average 200-250 ms, while forward saccades typically span 7-9 letter spaces (Rayner, 1998; Rayner et al., 1998). Because saccadic movement suppresses perceptual intake, linguistic decoding occurs exclusively during fixational pauses. This process extends into an asymmetric perceptual span that extracts parafoveal preview from upcoming words to prime subsequent lexical identification (Schotter, Angele, & Rayner, 2012). Reading comprehension is not a unidirectional, feedforward sensory process. Between 10% and 15% of all saccadic movements in mature reading, and up to 20% to 25% under complex syntactic or conceptual demands, are regressions: backward saccades launched to re-inspect previously encountered text (Booth & Weger, 2013; Rayner, 1998; Schotter, Tran, & Rayner, 2014). These regressive saccades are not random motor errors; rather, they are systematically aimed. Readers retain robust spatial memory of the layout and launch targeted backward movements directly toward the specific clause or phrase where comprehension broke down (Rayner, 1998). However, this essential error-recovery routine assumes the underlying text remains anchored to stable, predictable spatial coordinates.

Regressive saccades perform an essential cognitive repair function by enabling rereading and resolution of syntactic and interpretive breakdowns, rather than simply cueing spatial locations in working memory. Booth and Weger (2013) demonstrated the functional necessity of look-backs by showing that when previously fixated text becomes illegible upon regressive landing, regression rates decrease and backward saccades no longer predict comprehension. This finding confirms that the visual system depends on backward saccades to re-extract orthographic information directly from the display. When interfaces systematically restrict this capacity, such as through a trailing-mask paradigm that converts previously fixated words into uninformative characters, reading comprehension deteriorates significantly for both complex garden-path constructions and canonical declarative sentences (Schotter, Tran, & Rayner, 2014). Forward reading alone cannot compensate for this repair process. By comparing eye movements with neural language models, Timkey et al. (2026) identified an empirical dissociation in language processing: forward token surprisal explains early fixational latencies but does not account for the cognitive cost of rereading, which specifically reflects the detection, re-evaluation, and structural correction of semantic errors. Concurrent oculomotor and electroencephalographic recordings further reveal that encountering anomalous content triggers immediate regression and a P600 event-related potential.

In contrast, readers without regression opportunities default to shallow, "good-enough" heuristic processing that systematically overlooks errors (Metzner et al., 2017). Presentation formats that eliminate unconstrained spatial review, including rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) systems that suppress both parafoveal preview and regressive saccades (Benedetto et al., 2015) and kinetic scrolling displays that continually displace earlier claims beyond the reader's perceptual field (Harvey & Walker, 2018; Haverkamp et al., 2023), deprive the human supervisor of this structural repair mechanism. As a result, the visual system lacks a sensorimotor avenue to verify or resolve anomalous assertions as they arise.

#### 2.3. Visual Search, Abrupt Onsets, and Priority Maps

Beyond the localized mechanics of reading, competitive selection within the human visual search architecture governs how visual attention is allocated across dynamic displays. According to the Guided Search 6.0 framework (Wolfe, 2021), an integrated priority map coordinates attentional deployment. This map is a topographical representation of the visual field, sampled about 20 times per second to determine the next fixation point. The priority map integrates five guidance streams: bottom-up feature salience, top-down task demands, scene syntax and semantics, value-driven reward associations, and recent selection history (Wolfe, 2021). Within this competitive architecture, sudden environmental changes exert an immediate and disproportionate influence. Yantis and Jonides (1984) showed that an item with an abrupt visual onset is prioritized for cognitive processing over other elements, provided it represents a unique transient in the visual field. Sensorimotor accounts corroborate this effect, indicating that abrupt luminance or motion transients trigger low-level alerting mechanisms that involuntarily summon focal attention (O'Regan & Noë, 2001). However, bottom-up salience provides powerful but short-lived guidance. An isolated onset creates a momentary increase in the priority map that briefly wins attentional selection before rapidly decaying, which allows top-down, goal-directed control to reassert itself (Wolfe, 2021). In static reading environments, this temporal decay permits disengagement from visual novelties and the deployment of deliberate, top-down search strategies. In contrast, in an unbroken generative stream, the serial emission of new tokens functions as a continuous succession of abrupt onsets, persistently refreshing the bottom-up peak at the kinetic leading edge and suppressing the user's capacity to reassert top-down supervisory control.

The priority landscape's dynamic evolution over time increases the complexity of supervisory inspection. In visual search paradigms, the visual system can adaptively deprioritize previously inspected information through top-down attentional inhibition, a phenomenon formalized in experimental psychology as visual marking (Watson & Humphreys, 1997). When static visual elements are previewed before the arrival of new stimuli, observers can intentionally suppress older items to prioritize novel targets, provided that a sufficient temporal onset interval occurs and the spatial coordinates of the older items remain visually undisturbed (Kunar et al., 2006; Watson & Humphreys, 1997). Although this novelty bias facilitates exploratory search for newly emerging targets, it directly opposes an analytical supervisor's goals: inspecting, contextualizing, and verifying previously generated claims. Moreover, disengaging from newly arriving items and reconfiguring attentional priorities fundamentally depends on temporal pauses within the sensory stream. As Wolfe (2021) notes, an active priority map is not fixed: "if there is enough of a break in the action, the map may get reset" (p. 1069), with cognitive event boundaries serving as a plausible trigger for this structural clearance. In an uninterrupted token stream, however, the interface lacks this necessary break. By continuously emitting tokens and scrolling previous text upward, the unbroken display maintains an ongoing visual transient at the kinetic leading edge, preventing the priority map from resetting and hindering the attentional disengagement required for deliberate, backward supervisory inspections of preceding text.

#### 2.4. Event Segmentation and Situation Model Updating

While visual search priority maps govern spatial orientation, higher-order cognitive control over dynamic information depends on temporally parsing continuous experience into discrete units. Event Segmentation Theory (EST) posits that human perceptual systems structure sensory input by continuously generating forward predictions about imminent events (Zacks et al., 2007). These predictions are based on internal *event models, which are* working-memory representations of "what is happening now" that remain dynamically stable despite transient fluctuations in external stimuli (Zacks, 2020; Zacks et al., 2007); as long as incoming data align with predictions, the active event model persists, buffering cognitive control against sensory noise. When environmental contingencies change and prediction error increases, an attentional gate opens: the prevailing model resets or updates, external sensory input briefly exerts greater influence, and perceivers register a discrete event boundary (Zacks, 2020; Zacks et al., 2007). In textual discourse, this segmentation process operates across multidimensional situation models that track changes in protagonists, spatial locations, temporal intervals, causal chains, and intentional goals (Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998). Zacks, Speer, and Reynolds (2009) demonstrated that shifts across these narrative dimensions reliably predict where readers place event boundaries. Each situational change adds measurable cognitive processing time as working memory reconstructs its model of the discourse. Rather than representing passive comprehension costs, event boundaries function as an executive gating mechanism that regulates attentional deployment, coordinates working-memory updating, and resets cognitive control (Kurby & Zacks, 2012; Zacks, 2020). In static text, orthographic white space, paragraph breaks, and sectional headings externally anchor this cognitive reorganization by signaling semantic transitions. In an unbroken token stream, however, the continuous emergence of text deprives the reader of the visual boundaries required to trigger situation-model consolidation, forcing working memory to maintain an undifferentiated, unpunctuated representation of an evolving computational process.

The cognitive capacity to segment continuous discourse into discrete episodes does not depend on a static, self-paced page. Human event segmentation persists even under externally imposed temporal constraints. In a key experimental manipulation, Zacks, Speer, and Reynolds (2009) assessed text comprehension in a visual-sequential condition, presenting narrative prose clause by clause at an automated rate of approximately 3.35 words per second, without the possibility of spatial look-back. Even when deprived of temporal control and regressive review—a laboratory condition that closely approximates the sensorimotor constraints of a token stream—readers continued to segment discourse hierarchically, with both fine and coarse boundaries aligning significantly above chance (t(26) = 2.97). Recent computational studies show that these cognitive transition points can be modeled and predicted from the text's statistical dynamics. When comparing story comprehension with language-model predictions, Kumar et al. (2023) found that human event boundaries systematically track transient surges in Bayesian surprise, defined as how much an observation updates the model's posterior probability distribution over future states, rather than simple lexical surprisal. This result confirms that event boundaries reflect structural shifts in the underlying state space of a communicative process. However, updating an event model is an executive, resource-intensive operation that temporarily disrupts secondary target monitoring (Zacks, 2020). An uninterrupted generative stream therefore creates a supervisory trade-off. Although the human cognitive architecture attempts to segment discourse at state-altering transitions, the continuous influx of new tokens prevents working memory from consolidating its model, forcing the operator to either forgo critical verification or lose sensory contact with new output.

#### 2.5. Ecological and Sensorimotor Perception: Text as a Spatially Stable Object

Both oculomotor targeting and event cognition are grounded in a broader perceptual principle: reading functions as an embodied physical exploration of a stable material artifact. Within Gibson's (1979) ecological framework, perception involves directly picking up affordances, the functional possibilities for action an environmental layout provides to an active perceiver. As humans frequently alter the composition and arrangement of their physical environments, external graphic representations act as engineered affordances that directly anchor cognitive performance to spatial constraints (Gibson, 1979; Zhang & Norman, 1994). This ecological perspective aligns with O'Regan and Noë's (2001) sensorimotor contingency theory of vision, which challenges the notion that visual perception depends on constructing a detailed internal photographic replica of the world. Instead, vision is conceptualized as an exploratory action governed by lawful dependencies between bodily movement and sensory feedback, with the external environment serving as an external memory buffer accessible to the sensory apparatus as needed (O'Regan, 2011; O'Regan & Noë, 2001). The subjective experience of an accessible, coherent text does not require the reader to retain previous paragraphs in biological working memory; rather, it depends on the display's physical stability, which enables immediate re-sampling of any prior clause through a ballistic saccade (O'Regan & Noë, 2001). However, this sensorimotor proficiency assumes that the material surface remains stationary. Even on a static page, visual fixation does not guarantee cognitive registration; readers often fixate on anomalous words without consciously processing their errors (O'Regan & Noë, 2001). This perceptual breakdown becomes inevitable when an interface continually displaces its text and removes the spatial stability necessary for exploratory visual inspection.

Empirical investigations of text processing across diverse reading media demonstrate the cognitive necessity of spatial stability. When readers navigate extended discourse, their ability to execute targeted regressive saccades relies on an intact spatial mental representation of the physical layout. This representation enables the oculomotor system to direct backward eye movements precisely toward the structural locus of interpretive failure (Rayner, 1998). In comparative assessments of reading substrates, this spatial dependence is evident in the measurable utility of physical landmarks. In evaluating narrative recall across print and digital media, Mangen, Olivier, and Velay (2019) found that readers of printed texts reconstructed plot chronology significantly better than those reading on digital e-readers (t(48) = 2.03, 2‬  = .08). They attributed this performance advantage to the invariant "material placeholders" and tactile boundaries provided by the physical page. Similarly, a comprehensive meta-analysis of 54 empirical comparisons by Delgado et al. (2018) identified a persistent comprehension advantage for print over digital displays (Hedges’ g = -0.21). This advantage increased under enforced time constraints and was more pronounced in scrolling texts (g = -0.25) than in non-scrolling digital displays (g = -0.13). However, scrolling as an isolated categorical moderator did not reach statistical significance. Delgado and colleagues argued that scrolling text imposes an extraneous cognitive burden on readers by continually disrupting spatial orientation and visual reference points.

Although previous research has examined static pages, paged e-readers, and reader-controlled scrolling, I found no study that tests an active streaming display. Instead of allowing the reader to control when, where, and how rapidly the visual field shifts, an active token stream moves preceding text upward and appends new tokens at a pace determined by server computation. Even when agentic interfaces mechanically halt execution to demand user confirmation, they do not restore the display as a stable, inspectable environment; the preceding generative reasoning remains scrolled out of view, truncated, or buried beneath execution logs, while the prompt presses for forward action; until July 2026, an unanswered question in one widely used coding agent closed on its own, and the agent proceeded without the user (Anthropic, 2026b). In sensorimotor terms, each progression along this continuum—from static print, to paged screens, to reader-manipulated scrolling, and ultimately to autonomous token streaming—incrementally removes the environmental invariants that enable written text to function as an external memory store. By pushing users to the end of this destabilization, current conversational front-ends would dismantle the perceptual scaffolding needed for supervision and critical intervention.

### **3. A Worked Case: How Claude Code Stages Decisions**

To examine how decision points are structured on contemporary interaction surfaces, this analysis focuses on the terminal-based programming agent Claude Code (Anthropic, 2026b). Although staged decision points did not originate with this system—earlier implementations appeared in commercial tools such as GitHub Copilot Workspace and OpenAI Deep Research, as well as research systems like WaitGPT (Xie et al., 2024)—Claude Code offers an unusually transparent empirical record. Its public version history, dated changelog, and open documentation enable precise tracing of primary sources, including the introduction of intermediate checkpoints, the wording of options, and the evolution of the platform's interaction model. In interactive mode, users see an advancing stream where natural-language reasoning alternates with automated tool calls, including file edits, codebase searches, and shell executions. While the command-line interface (CLI) paused for individual permission prompts from its initial release, the continuous token stream does not pause during generation unless the user explicitly presses Escape to abort the active turn mid-execution (Anthropic, 2026c). If an operator types an instruction while the agent is running, the interface queues the message rather than interrupting the turn, requiring the user to wait for a system-determined breakpoint (Anthropic, 2026c). As outlined in Section 2, this architecture imposes an externally paced pursuit task on the human operator: the text scrolls continuously, previous terminal reasoning disappears from view, and the supervisor's sustained vigilance alone determines the operational juncture for manual interruption.

Between 2025 and 2026, the platform introduced a series of granular decision points into its workflow. An explicit task list designed to help the agent "stay on track" was introduced in April 2025 (v0.2.93), followed by a dedicated "plan mode" first documented in June 2025 (v1.0.33; Anthropic, 2026b). By late 2025, the vendor added an optional configuration to partition cognitive labor across models—deploying Opus for planning and Sonnet for execution (v1.0.77, August 2025)—and introduced discrete state checkpoints via a /rewind command (v2.0.0, September 2025), as well as more frequent solicitation of clarifying input during planning (v2.0.21, October 2025; Anthropic, 2026b). Despite these additions, user autonomy at these junctures remained tightly constrained. Until July 2026, any unanswered question posed by the agent to the user would automatically time out and resume execution independently (v2.1.200; Anthropic, 2026b, 2026d). Subsequently, in late 2026, the platform introduced default pathways to bypass intermediate interruptions: in August 2026, permission prompts for Bash execution included a "Yes, and switch to auto mode" option (v2.1.247), culminating in September 2026 with auto mode established as the default starting mode for enterprise and team subscription tiers (v2.1.283; Anthropic, 2026a, 2026b). Over sixteen months, the interface evolved from basic operational permission prompts through an expanding framework of planning and questioning checkpoints to an autonomous mode engineered to minimize manual intervention.

The structural logic of these checkpoints is evident in what the interface expects from user decision-making. When the agent halts for plan approval, the user encounters a terminal menu with "Yes, and use auto mode" as the leading option, followed by "Yes, manually approve edits" and "No, keep planning" (Anthropic, 2026a). Selecting the leading option accepts the proposed sequence and suppresses subsequent granular checkpoints during execution. Notably, the plan itself is already narrowed: as of December 2025, system instructions for plan generation directed the model to "Include only your recommended approach, not all alternatives" (Ronacher, 2025). The latent decision space—such as competing architectural paths, rejected libraries, and uninspected trade-offs—is collapsed within the model before any text is rendered on screen. When the agent seeks clarification mid-task, it presents a multiple-choice prompt that the model authors entirely, limiting the user to system-generated options or a custom response via an "Other" entry (Anthropic, 2026d). Although the platform maintains a formal boundary of human oversight—permission prompts and plan approvals "never auto-resolve on idle," ensuring the agent cannot proceed past a staged gate without explicit user input (Anthropic, 2026d)—it frames intermediate friction as an operational cost to be minimized. Auto mode is explicitly positioned as a solution for "reducing prompt fatigue" that "nudges Claude to keep working without stopping for clarifying questions" (Anthropic, 2026a). An automated classifier evaluates execution actions, ultimately leaving the user with a binary choice between enduring manual prompt fatigue and approving a trajectory whose latent alternatives remain concealed.

Viewed through Johnson's (2000) historical lens, these interface mechanisms show how contemporary tools reinforce a passive reading practice rather than promoting algorithmic oversight. Johnson conceptualizes reading as a "sociocultural system in which the individual participates" (pp. 602–603), rather than an isolated cognitive act. In Claude Code, the interaction model constructs a system in which a solitary expert developer observes an automated agent at work. This dynamic closely resembles classical bookrolls, which scribes formatted as unpunctuated scripts to be "played" like a stage performance or videotape, leaving pacing, pauses, and rhetorical inflection to the "reader-performer" (Johnson, 2000, p. 620). The token stream is similarly staged as a performance: the user observes the agent’s execution in real time and is prompted to react only at operational breakpoints. Prompts, menus, and status indicators that interrupt this stream function as performance cues rather than structural spaces—analogous to modern email emoticons that reading cultures adopt to sustain existing practices without altering their material architecture (Johnson, 2000, p. 608). These cues signal when to approve but do not provide a stable spatial point for inspection, review, or reconsideration of latent commitments. This performance logic extends to state restoration: just as excerpts of an ancient roll could be sequentially "replayed."

In contrast, "nothing in the ancient bookroll was designed to facilitate retrieval in any other way" (Johnson, 2000, p. 620); Claude Code’s /rewind command indexes historical checkpoints strictly to the sequential prompts sent by the user—one entry per prompt (Anthropic, 2026e)—enabling chronological replay by turn rather than structural retrieval based on the agent’s internal decision spaces. Additionally, similar to technical documents that function as "closed systems" written specialist-to-specialist (Johnson, 2000, p. 612), Claude Code’s menus presume an expert user who understands each option; for non-experts, selecting from system-authored choices does not substitute for identifying where the stream commits them. The platform provides cues for an unfolding performance but offers no spaces for governance.

This staged interaction model is not unique to Claude Code. Intermediate decision junctures are present across both commercial and research systems: GitHub Copilot Workspace structures agentic development around two explicit steering moments—editing the initial task specification and subsequently modifying the proposed execution plan—while OpenAI's Deep Research pauses before initiating long-horizon information retrieval to solicit clarifying user guidance. Concurrently, research prototypes such as WaitGPT (Xie et al., 2024), which parses streaming code into editable syntactic blocks, investigate real-time inspection, even as industry implementations diverge, with some developer tools eliminating planning checkpoints entirely (Ronacher, 2025). The critical ergonomic question these designs raise does not require attributing commercial motives to platform architects. Following Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024) analysis of platform coordination, evaluate the issue by its structural impact on agency and choice architecture: regardless of vendor intent, when an interface determines the placement of decision points, authors the available alternatives, and ranks their selection, it exercises coordinative co-optation. The user's supervisory authority is enlisted to ratify the system's operational momentum but is limited to selecting among options pre-filtered by the intermediary. The vendor's stated rationales—reducing "prompt fatigue" and deploying automated classifier "nudges" to bypass clarifying pauses (Anthropic, 2026a)—document this structural consequence within engineering discourse. By treating user intervention as operational friction to minimize rather than an essential supervisory affordance, contemporary front-ends systematically sacrifice the structural conditions necessary for algorithmic oversight for uninterrupted execution.

#### 3.1. Streaming and Pacing

Research on streaming and pacing in conversational interfaces primarily frames temporal delivery as an engineering throughput problem, a social presence issue, or a reading-comfort concern. Modulating response latency or simulating conversational typing reliably alters user perception: simulated sentence typing delays inflate perceived competence, trustworthiness, and persuasiveness without changing perceived task effectiveness (Bhat, 2025), while initial response latency prompts users to attribute "AI deliberation" to the system (Tan et al., 2026) and shifts perceived social presence (Gnewuch et al., 2022). However, evidence that dynamic text delivery impairs supervisory discernment remains limited and confounded. Anderl et al. (2024) observed that presenting information as a screencast of a typing chatbot reduced participants' ability to distinguish accurate from inaccurate claims in one experiment, but not in a preceding trial. Because the typing animation was embedded within a chatbot interface, dynamic pacing was bundled with conversational framing, preventing the authors from isolating whether the impairment stemmed from the kinetic display or the conversational medium itself.

When systems research addresses the token stream directly, it treats temporal delivery as a problem of aggregate velocity. Infrastructure frameworks calibrate token emission against estimated human consumption baselines: Andes estimates silent reading at about 4.8 tokens per second to optimize serving schedules (Liu et al., 2024), while Xiao and Yang (2025) measure comfortable reading-speed thresholds to throttle commercial APIs that output at more than double those rates. This rate-based perspective parallels earlier psycholinguistic research on moving displays: while smoothly scrolling text can support comprehension comparable to rapid serial visual presentation (Kang & Muter, 1989), continuous movement disrupts higher-level inferential comprehension and makes returning to previously encountered text significantly more difficult (Harvey & Walker, 2018), while user-manipulated scrolling yields fewer, more dispersed look-backs than discrete paging (Haverkamp et al., 2023).

Crucially, this literature treats pacing as the digital equivalent of poetic meter—a continuous property of the system's performance. Whether adjusting server throughput or measuring comfortable reading velocity, existing work varies only in how fast or how smoothly tokens arrive. It never asks where in a forming response the stream should stop, who has the authority to set that boundary, or how a reader might locate a latent operational commitment in natural-language prose. By framing delivery entirely as a question of rate, pacing research leaves the stream's internal architecture unexamined, giving the user no structural affordance beyond a blunt abort command.

#### 3.2. Automation Supervision and Trust

Unlike research on reading or interface pacing, human-factors scholarship on automation explicitly treats the human operator as a supervisor monitoring an autonomous system (Endsley, 2017; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Within this tradition, supervisory failures are explained as breakdowns of attentional allocation under multiple-task workload (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). In their foundational synthesis, Parasuraman and Manzey invoked "looking-but-not-seeing" to explain commission errors, noting that in one study they reviewed, about half of the participants who followed a faulty automated diagnosis had sampled all the information needed to detect the error (p. 402). In a systematic review of the automation bias literature, Goddard et al. (2012) identified workload, task complexity, and time constraints as environmental mediators of complacency, while identifying the physical screen position of advice and the provision of raw information rather than recommendations as key design mitigators.

Presenting system output as an explicit recommendation alters user scrutiny. In simulated control tasks, automated recommendations improve overall task accuracy while slightly reducing an operator's ability to discriminate between accurate and inaccurate system guidance (Gegoff et al., 2025). Granting operators control over transparency does not resolve this bias. When given the flexibility to adjust information detail, operators selected high transparency only in 39% to 43% of trials, and adaptable transparency did not improve automation-use accuracy (Tatasciore et al., 2026)—moreover, fixed high transparency increased agreement bias without improving detection of faulty advice. In interactive agent workflows, oversight fails decisively on temporal coordination: examining user supervision of computer-use agents, Chen et al. (2026, preprint) found that users intercepted only 29 of 138 problematic actions, with 56% of failures involving a timing mismatch where operational risks emerged before or without an explicit opportunity for the user to intervene. As Chen and colleagues concluded, effective oversight requires aligning authority, timing, and attention to "trigger judgment before a consequential transition." Over time, operators adapt by learning the system's operational "boundary conditions" and stop treating the automation as a general advisor (Lamb et al., 2026; Lee & See, 2004). Yet as system autonomy and reliability increase, situation awareness systematically decays (Endsley, 2017). This produces an automation conundrum directly relevant to generative supervision: the more reliable the automated execution, the less equipped the human supervisor becomes to reassert manual control when an anomaly occurs.

When evaluated against the governing question of algorithmacy—who places the decision point, and what does the user do there—this literature reveals a shared structural assumption: the decision point is placed by the designer, fixed in advance on a static display. Whether through the screen placement of an alert (Goddard et al., 2012), prescribed transparency toggles (Tatasciore et al., 2026), or Chen et al.'s (2026) call to pause before consequential state transitions, the interface predetermines where the human is summoned to evaluate the system. The operator responds to marks an engineer authored, choosing to accept, reject, or modify a discrete proposal. This tradition leaves out continuity. Automation research models machine assistance as a series of isolated events—an alarm, a diagnostic label, or a recommendation modal. A streamed generative response, by contrast, is an unfolding temporal performance in which moments requiring critical judgment emerge *within* the language as it forms. When platforms suppress intermediate prompts to eliminate "prompt fatigue"—as in Claude Code's progression toward a default auto mode (§3.1)—they enact Endsley's (2017) conundrum directly on the screen: streamlining execution speed while denying the supervisor any structural affordance to locate where the advancing stream binds their intent.

#### 3.3. Large Language Model Overreliance, Fluency, and Cognitive Friction

Research on human interaction with large language models locates supervisory failure in the persuasive effect of textual fluency and the cognitive economics of user effort. Fluent explanations consistently increase reliance on model outputs, even when the underlying assertions are inaccurate (Kim et al., 2025). When models accompany their answers with persuasive rationales, users systematically overestimate system accuracy, with longer explanations increasing confidence without improving correctness (Steyvers et al., 2025). At the model level, larger architectures more often generate plausible yet deceptive errors (Zhou et al., 2024), while preference optimization can make justifications for false answers seem convincing without improving error verifiability (X. Zhu et al., 2026, preprint). This uncritical reliance reflects a rational cognitive trade-off: users weigh the substantial effort of verifying complex text against the low-cost heuristic of accepting fluent prose (Vasconcelos et al., 2023), often resorting to coarse scanning and wholesale copying of generated answers (Fok & Weld, 2024; Liu et al., 2026).

To disrupt this passive compliance, human-factors researchers introduce cognitive friction. Cognitive forcing functions—such as mandatory waiting periods before advice appears or prompts to examine underlying assumptions—can reduce overreliance on erroneous recommendations, though they often come with user dissatisfaction (Buçinca et al., 2021; Ghosh et al., 2026, preprint). Peripheral obstacles, such as selective access barriers (Collins et al., 2024, preprint) or preparatory microboundaries (Arun & Fischer, 2026; Cox et al., 2016), can increase perceived workload without reliably sharpening critical evaluation, while prompting users to reflect can provoke resistance from operators seeking immediate task completion (Forshaw et al., 2026). Where interventions do operate directly on model output, they alter its semantic content—such as injecting first-person expressions of uncertainty (Kim et al., 2024) or framing explanations as questions (Danry et al., 2023)—or modify generation at the model level via concept suppression (Disley & Khan, 2026).

When evaluated against the governing question of algorithmacy—who places the decision point, and what does the user do there—the friction literature exposes a distinct structural boundary. Friction sits either before an interaction begins, at the threshold of model access, or within the model's weights; when interventions appear in generated text, they alter the linguistic substance of what the system says. None of these approaches marks the points where an unchanged response calls for critical judgment. Interface designers place decision points uniformly, or automated confidence heuristics trigger them, leaving the user to react to system-curated obstacles rather than learn to locate latent commitments independently. Furthermore, this research tradition directly conflicts with commercial interface trajectories. While empirical findings show that unreflective fluency degrades supervisory vigilance and that cognitive forcing preserves critical scrutiny, contemporary platforms treat friction as mere "prompt fatigue" to be eliminated. As demonstrated in Claude Code's shift toward default auto mode (§3.1), vendors resolve user friction by suppressing intermediate checkpoints and delegating risk classification to secondary models. By designing obstacles outside the text while platforms engineer away the remaining pauses, both paradigms leave the generative stream as an unpunctuated performance, locking the operator in a literacy trap that conflates syntactic fluency with verified intent.

#### 3.4. Gaze Allocation as an Ambiguous Metric of Oversight

In empirical evaluations of generative and automated systems, researchers frequently rely on eye-tracking metrics under the implicit premise of the strong eye–mind hypothesis (Just & Carpenter, 1980)—assuming that fixational dwell, saccadic targeting, and visual transitions serve as faithful proxies for cognitive scrutiny and supervisory engagement. Applied to streaming text, eye tracking shows how radically dynamic delivery disrupts visual exploration: continuous token generation inflates overall fixation counts, prolongs cumulative gaze duration, and concentrates initial fixations almost exclusively at the kinetic leading edge where new characters appear (Park & Lee, 2024). Under certain structured conditions, eye gaze does correlate with supervisory reliance: fixations on automated explanations can track task accuracy and advice uptake in diagnostic settings (Cao & Huang, 2022; Wu et al., 2025). Yet across interactive generative displays, ocular fixation repeatedly dissociates from supervisory discernment and critical verification. Reorganizing the layout of conversational outputs changes how quickly users first fixate on source attributions, but longer gaze dwell on those citations does not predict whether users calibrate their trust (Cho & Lim, 2026). More fundamentally, as sensorimotor psychophysics shows, visual gaze captures low-level alerting and stimulus-driven capture as readily as it reflects active deliberation; an operator can look straight at an anomalous or erroneous token without cognitively registering the mistake (O'Regan & Noë, 2001; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

When evaluated against the governing question of algorithmacy, gaze tracking exposes a decisive analytical limitation: it records where the fovea was directed. Still, it cannot establish whether the user located an operational decision space or merely tracked a system-authored visual cue. In an unmarked token stream, the dense clustering of fixations at the advancing edge reflects the oculomotor visual system being pulled along by a succession of abrupt onsets (Section 2.3), rather than deliberate supervisory navigation. Even when an interface pauses execution to display an interactive dialog or permission prompt, gaze dwell on that menu confirms only that the user is reading the text the model authored; it does not indicate that the operator has perceived the latent alternatives or architectural commitments that the system collapsed before the prompt's appearance. Gaze tracks the user's participation in an interface's reading performance. It cannot measure whether the human has identified where the computation binds their intent. By treating ocular inspection as synonymous with supervisory control, existing evaluation paradigms remain confined within a literacy-based reading model, obscuring the structural distinction between reading an automated output and locating its latent decision spaces.

#### 3.5. Synthesis: The Missing Variable in Current Accounts

Across these four research traditions and contemporary agentic platforms, a consistent structural paradigm emerges: supervisory agency is framed as a reaction to system-authored representations. Pacing research modulates delivery velocity without altering the stream's internal topography (3.2). Human-factors models of automation treat machine assistance as discrete advice on static displays, requiring operators to respond within boundaries predetermined by designers (3.3). Cognitive friction interventions place hurdles around the perimeter of model access or modify lexical phrasing, leaving the structural progression of an unchanged response unpunctuated (3.4). Gaze tracking measures ocular pursuit along kinetic onsets, conflating fixational dwell with supervisory scrutiny (3.5). Even commercial agents that introduce intermediate pause points confine the operator to selecting among system-authored options at junctures dictated by the platform's operational momentum (3.1). Across all these paradigms, the human operator is positioned as a reader who decodes, waits, or chooses. None provides a structural affordance that enables the user to locate where the unfolding computation commits their intent.

### 4\. Algorithmacy Theory: Decision Spaces and Their Marking

To address the gap identified in Section 3—namely, that existing accounts across human–computer interaction and automation engineering either confine supervisory intervention to system-curated menus embedded within an active stream (3.1) or restrict oversight to designer-placed advice on static displays (3.3)—I propose algorithmacy theory. While Oberschelp’s (1995) earlier use of the term in informatics teaching is acknowledged, the present theory articulates the cognitive and perceptual competencies required to supervise, evaluate, and steer autonomous algorithmic intermediaries within opaque, co-optive, triadic environments. In contrast to prevailing paradigms that treat supervisory failure as an editorial deficit to be resolved through heightened vigilance or peripheral friction, or treat delivery as an engineering rate to be calibrated through pacing adjustments, algorithmacy theory posits that effective oversight in streaming generative systems depends on identifying and rendering perceptible the latent *decision spaces* where human intervention is functionally necessary to control intent and anticipate counterpart dynamics. To formalize this framework, this section defines the core constructs and positions algorithmacy within the historical lineage of foundational human competences (4.1), presents the nomological network of the theory (4.2), formalizes its core propositions (4.3) and thirteen directional predictions (4.4), specifies an operational rule for locating decision spaces in natural-language streams (4.5), and details the three perceptual mechanisms restored by interface marking (4.6–4.8), before examining key ergonomic moderators (4.9), the dissociation between gaze and supervisory action (4.10), and boundary distinctions from adjacent interventions (4.11).

#### 4.1. Triadic Coordination, Co-optation, and the Competence Lineage

*Algorithmacy* is the competency required to navigate, evaluate, and direct autonomous algorithmic intermediaries in triadic, opaque, and co-optive environments. Although algorithmacy encompasses institutional and organizational contexts (Section 5.3), this article focuses on its *perceptual dimension*: the capacity of a human supervisor to detect, evaluate, and act upon latent decision spaces within ongoing computational outputs. Conceptualizing this capacity necessitates moving beyond the dyadic assumptions prevalent in the reading and streaming-interface literatures reviewed in Sections 2 and 3. In these traditions, interaction is modeled dyadically, where a reader encounters an invariant inscription that remains unchanged during inspection. In contrast, interaction with a generative language model constitutes a triadic coordination. Here, the supervisor engages the task environment through an autonomous intermediary that samples high-dimensional probability distributions, optimizes for platform-level objectives such as throughput and prompt-fatigue reduction (Section 3.1), and delivers output at a rate determined by the serving infrastructure (Section 3.2).

To characterize how this relationship shapes supervisory agency, Stark and Vanden Broeck’s (2024) analysis of platform coordination is extended to the interaction surface. While actors in hierarchies command, in markets contract, and in networks collaborate, platforms co-opt and enroll them through the system's structural design. By formatting high-dimensional computation as an advancing ribbon of text, the streaming interface co-opts the supervisor into a literate reading performance. Outside the platform's staged pauses, and even within them where plans arrive already narrowed to a single recommended path (Section 3.1), points that functionally require human judgment are presented as completed recommendations. This configuration channels finite cognitive resources into evaluating surface-level textual fluency, obscures the intermediary's latent commitments, and leaves the supervisor to choose among system-curated alternatives.

To clarify its structure, algorithmacy fits within the historical lineage of foundational human competences: oracy, literacy, and numeracy (Table 4.1). Just as Wilkinson (1965, as cited in Mercer et al., 2017) coined "oracy" to grant the oral skills of speaking and listening comparable status with reading and writing, and Gal et al. (2020) argue that numeracy should hold a similar standing to that of literacy, algorithmacy asserts equivalent status for governing computational mediation. Each of these competences resolves a structural gap between what is provided by a medium and what human action requires: oracy bridges the gap between spoken sound and contextual meaning; literacy bridges static orthographic marks to linguistic sense; numeracy bridges mathematical notation to quantitative relations; and algorithmacy bridges an opaque computational stream to the human judgments required to govern it.

In reading science, Hoover and Gough's (1990) Simple View of Reading models reading comprehension as the product of decoding and linguistic comprehension; in their formulation, comprehension is zero if either component is absent. Although Duke and Cartwright (2021) demonstrate that these components overlap in practice and interact with active, self-regulatory processes, the underlying logic applies directly to supervisory interaction. Supervisory navigation at the perceptual layer requires both detecting decision spaces and maintaining an operative mental model of the intermediary. Both capacities are functionally necessary, and neither is sufficient on its own. A supervisor who visually locates a marked decision space but lacks an internal model of how the intermediary optimizes or fails perceives a pause but has no conceptual basis on which to direct it. Conversely, an expert who understands the system's operational boundaries but is denied the perceptual scaffolding needed to locate where the advancing stream binds them has no practical opportunity to intervene.

Algorithmacy diverges from these older competences in four decisive ways. First, whereas traditional orthography and mathematical notation rely on stable, public symbolic systems, mathematics "sometimes disappears within technological systems," and machine-learning processes are "black boxes, even for those who produce them" (Gal et al., 2020, citing Michael & Lupton, 2016). Second, while written text evolved interword spaces and punctuation to externalize its syntax (Section 2.1), the medium of the generative stream provides no native visual syntax to indicate where latent decision spaces reside. Third, whereas traditional education ensures that basic competence precedes participation—individuals learn to read before signing binding contracts—users of generative systems are integrated into operational workflows before any instruction is required (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Fourth, while surface fluency in reading reflects mastery, fluency in generative interaction may signal cognitive capture, wherein the supervisor becomes unconsciously fitted to the intermediary's automated regularities.

This distinction separates algorithmacy from prevailing conceptions of algorithmic literacy. Ridley and Pawlick-Potts (2021) define algorithmic literacy as "the skill, expertise, and awareness" to understand, recognize, create, apply, and assess algorithms, positioning the individual as a "co-constituent in algorithmic decision-making" (p. 3). Their formulation takes algorithms themselves as its primary object—their societal functioning, creation, and governance. Algorithmacy, by contrast, focuses on the latent decision spaces within an intermediary's active output, as perceived during the output stream. While algorithmic literacy provides declarative and critical knowledge about algorithms, algorithmacy equips the supervisor to identify where the computation binds their intent and to intervene before the agent commits.

To keep this competency framework from lapsing into the theoretical overreach that has historically compromised functional accounts of literacy, we must establish an explicit scope boundary. For decades, literacy scholarship has contested the "autonomous" model of skill acquisition, which presumes that acquiring an inscription system imparts general, transformative cognitive enhancements across domains (Street, 2003). As Street (2003) argued, this autonomous model "disguises the cultural and ideological assumptions that underpin it," treating historically situated social practices as neutral, universal technologies of mind. Cross-cultural psychological investigations corroborate this limitation: examining non-schooled literate populations, Scribner and Cole (1978) established that the cognitive consequences of literacy among the Vai were tied to specific practices rather than general intellectual transformations. Following this empirical precedent, no general restructuring of human cognition is posited. Neurocognitive research demonstrates that reading acquisition reorganizes specific, measurable visual and phonological systems rather than transforming general intellectual faculties (Dehaene et al., 2015). Similarly, algorithmacy theory claims only specific perceptual consequences arising from the concrete practice of supervising streamed computational text. Algorithmacy governs not abstract cognition, but the situated supervisory practice of navigating an intermediary's output in real time (Section 5.4 returns to this critique).

#### 4.2. Nomological Network of Algorithmacy at the Perceptual Layer

To formalize how these dynamic forces interact during real-time interaction, Figure 4.1 specifies the nomological network of algorithmacy at the perceptual layer, delineating the causal pathways connecting interface properties, perceptual mechanisms, and supervisory action.

**Figure 4.1.** Nomological network of the perceptual layer of algorithmacy. *(Note: Schematic layout for drafting; final manuscript submission will render this framework as a publication-grade vector graphic).*

The model posits that latent decision spaces (DS) exist within the computational process before and independently of interface representation. In an unmarked stream, the process is obscured by exogenous stream properties (A), comprising server-regulated emission velocity, continuous token generation, and automated display scrolling (Sections 2.3 and 3.2). Whereas visual-search paradigms establish that an abrupt visual onset captures selective attention when it represents an isolated transient (Yantis & Jonides, 1984; Section 2.3), the model proposes that in streaming interfaces, the train of continuous token appearances operates as an ongoing perceptual draw, generating an overt forward pull at the leading edge (C; Park & Lee, 2024). The model predicts that this forward momentum constrains the look-back window (E), depriving the supervisor of the spatial stability and temporal leeway necessary to execute regressive saccades and repair comprehension failures (Section 2.2). Operating concurrently, platform-level co-optation (H)—drawing on evidence that persuasive rationales and recommendation framing inflate reliance without improving error discrimination (Sections 3.3 and 3.4)—is hypothesized to depress supervisory navigation (G), biasing the supervisor toward accepting uninspected output.

Interface marking (M)—parameterized across structural placement, temporal duration, and visual form—is hypothesized to operate as the architectural counterweight examined here along three causal paths (Sections 4.6–4.8): attenuating forward pull (C), establishing an event boundary that prompts cognitive situation-model updating (D), and preserving an unhurried look-back window (E). In combination, event-boundary induction (D) and look-back availability (E) are predicted to channel overt visual attention (F)—operationalized as fixations and regressive saccades—to the locus of the decision space. Visual inspection (F) is an oculomotor prerequisite rather than a guarantee of supervisory control: following the joint competence formulation articulated in Section 4.1, gaze allocation becomes supervisory navigation (G) only when it multiplies with the supervisor's operative mental model of the intermediary (MI). A supervisor may look directly at a marked decision space, yet fail to intervene if they lack an internal representation of the intermediary's boundary conditions and typical failure modes.

#### 4.3. Formal Propositions

To synthesize the functional relationships depicted in the nomological network (Figure 4.1), the perceptual layer of algorithmacy theory is formalized into six core propositions. These propositions define the ontological status of decision spaces, articulate how continuous machine-paced streaming suppresses oversight, and specify the cognitive conditions under which interface marking facilitates supervisory navigation.

**Proposition 1 (Ontological Independence of Decision Spaces).** *An automated computational process contains latent decision spaces—points where human intervention is functionally necessary to control intent and anticipate counterpart dynamics—that exist before and independent of their representation on the interaction surface.*

Proposition 1 establishes that decision spaces are properties of the underlying computational and coordinating process rather than artifacts of graphic interface design. In an agentic workflow, an intermediary makes architectural commitments, selects execution paths, and evaluates trade-offs regardless of whether the interaction surface displays these junctures or conceals them beneath an unbroken stream. Decoupling a decision space from its graphic representation provides the theoretical basis for evaluating interface fidelity: an interface does not create decision spaces; it either renders them perceptible or obscures them.

**Proposition 2 (Perceptual Suppression in Streaming).** *Continuous token generation exerts a persistent, stimulus-driven draw toward the advancing leading edge, suppressing regressive saccades and constraining the temporal and spatial window required for updating the situation model.*

Proposition 2 articulates the primary sensory cost imposed by machine-paced kinetic delivery. In visual search, an abrupt visual onset summons selective attention when it constitutes an isolated transient in the visual field (Yantis & Jonides, 1984; Section 2.3). In an active token stream, the rapid succession of new characters functions as an ongoing perceptual draw, concentrating visual fixations at the advancing boundary (Park & Lee, 2024) and interpreting Wolfe’s (2021) guided-search framework with caution, and recognizing that bottom-up salience represents a transient weighting within a priority map rather than an involuntary, total seizure of attention, Proposition 2 posits that this forward momentum depresses regressive saccades. Because skilled reading relies on regressions to detect comprehension failures and execute structural repairs (Booth & Weger, 2013; Schotter et al., 2014; Section 2.2), continuous generation deprives the supervisor of the unhurried perceptual landing points needed to evaluate the text as it forms.

**Proposition 3 (Co-optation via Explanations and Framing)*****—model-generated*** *explanations and recommendation framing attenuate critical evaluation, biasing the supervisor toward accepting uninspected computational output.*

Proposition 3 identifies the cognitive counterpart to perceptual suppression. In static evaluations, presenting generated explanations increases user agreement with both correct and incorrect model outputs (Kim et al., 2025; Section 3.4), while longer explanations inflate user confidence without improving decision accuracy (Steyvers et al., 2025). Additionally, framing conclusions as recommendations can slightly degrade a supervisor's ability to distinguish accurate from inaccurate guidance, even as recommendations increase overall task accuracy (Gegoff et al., 2025; Section 3.3). Presenting state-altering commitments in the syntax of a completed answer positions the supervisor in an editorial reading role, encouraging compliance before the supervisor can scrutinize the text's operational implications.

**Proposition 4 (Perceptual Restoration via Marking).** *Introducing structural marks at latent decision spaces attenuates forward attentional pull, is predicted to induce cognitive event boundaries, and preserves an unhurried look-back window.*

Proposition 4 outlines the threefold perceptual mechanism hypothesized when an interface marks a decision space (Sections 4.6–4.8), directly reflecting the causal pathways MC‬‭‬ , M D, and M E in Figure 4.1. First, marking temporarily arrests the forward draw at the leading edge, restoring a stable visual landing site for saccadic targeting, which is analogous to the spatial targeting role of interword spacing in reading (Rayner et al., 1998; Section 2.1). Second, according to Event Segmentation Theory (Zacks et al., 2007), the structural discontinuity introduced by an interface mark is predicted to serve as an external perceptual cue that induces an event boundary, prompting the cognitive system to segment ongoing activity and update its working situation model (Zacks et al., 2009; Section 2.4). Third, by pausing emission or visually demarcating completed segments, marking widens the look-back window, preserving the temporal and spatial leeway required to execute regressive saccades and verify commitments before the agent proceeds (Section 2.2).

**Proposition 5 (Multiplicative Competency of Oversight).** *Visual inspection of a marked decision space is a necessary oculomotor prerequisite for oversight. Still, successful supervisory navigation requires the joint, interdependent coordination of perceptual detection and an operative mental model of the intermediary.*

Proposition 5 formalizes the relationship between perceptual attention and supervisory action. Patterned on the Simple View of Reading (Hoover & Gough, 1990; Section 4.1), supervisory navigation is not an additive accumulation of independent behaviors, but a joint capacity: oversight collapses if either perceptual detection or conceptual comprehension is missing. Although ocular gaze can track levels of reliance under structured conditions (Wu et al., 2025), visual dwell alone does not guarantee critical scrutiny or trust calibration across interactive interfaces. For example, an operator may fixate on source attributions without calibrating trust (Cho & Lim, 2026), or sample relevant diagnostic data without cognitively registering an anomaly (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010; Section 3.5). To evaluate an uninspected branch or redirect an agentic trajectory, the supervisor must coordinate perceptual inspection with an operative mental model of the intermediary's boundary conditions, failure modes, and platform incentives (Lamb et al., 2026; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024).

**Proposition 6 (Ergonomic Alignment Sensitivity).** *The efficacy of interface marking in facilitating supervisory navigation depends on structural placement, visual form, and temporal duration; misaligned marks fragment cognition and degrade task performance.*

Proposition 6 specifies the ergonomic constraints governing interface intervention. By analogy to task-interruption research, interruptions at coarse task boundaries cause minimal disruption, whereas interruptions within ongoing subtasks increase completion times and error rates (Bailey & Konstan, 2006; Powers & Scerbo, 2023). The benefit of a mark depends on its alignment with meaningful operational units. Reading studies show that arbitrary or syntactically misaligned breaks increase processing time (Tsoukala et al., 2025). However, preliminary evidence suggests readers may gradually recalibrate with repeated exposure. Temporal duration also requires careful calibration (Gould et al., 2016): brief lockouts can encourage checking behavior in controlled settings, but prolonged delays risk inducing task switching and operational friction, and marking aids supervisory navigation only when its placement, duration, and visual form coincide with the functional decision spaces of the underlying process.

#### 4.5. Operationalizing Decision Spaces in Natural-Language Streams

To let algorithmacy theory inform actionable interface architectures and support falsifiable empirical tests, researchers must define the decision space concretely rather than leave it as an abstract phenomenological construct. If the theory posits that the supervisory benefit of marking is contingent upon its structural alignment with meaningful task units (Proposition 6; Prediction P7), researchers and interface designers must have an explicit operational rule for identifying decision spaces within unstructured natural-language output. Current systems research typically calibrates delivery pacing for throughput or comfortable reading velocity, rather than for content (Liu et al., 2024; Xiao & Yang, 2025; Section 3.2). In static printed documents, visual hierarchies such as paragraph indents, section headings, and white space provide spatial layouts that externalize discourse organization and event structure (Section 2.4). Although these typographic elements render progressively as tokens arrive in a stream, the interface provides no look-ahead; supervisors lack visual cues for upcoming transitions before they occur. Therefore, identifying decision spaces necessitates an explicit rule that determines where supervisory judgment is functionally required within the computational process, independent of graphical display artifacts.

A decision space operational rule is defined here, comprising four structural criteria, each corresponding to an observable transition in the intermediary's task progression:

1.  **Consequential Commitment Points:** These are junctures where the intermediary is about to execute an external state change, invoke a tool, modify an environment, or perform an irreversible operational action (e.g., executing a terminal command, modifying code files, or transmitting external communications). Chen et al. (2026, preprint) reported that most supervisory failures in agent oversight result from a timing mismatch, where operational risk emerges before or without an opportunity for intervention—placing a mark immediately before such an execution boundary identifies an operational decision space in which the supervisor must accept, reject, or redirect the action. Although commercial developer tools have historically paused at some of these points through permission prompts, platforms are increasingly removing these stops to enable uninterrupted execution (Section 3.1).
2.  **Branching Plurality Junctures:** These are moments when the intermediary consolidates multiple viable architectural paths, competing design strategies, or alternative hypotheses into a single trajectory without presenting the rejected options. As documented in Claude Code's plan-mode prompt, system instructions may explicitly direct the model to provide only its recommended approach rather than listing alternatives (Ronacher, 2025; Section 3.1). Since the intermediary collapses these alternatives internally before generating output, this convergence does not produce a natural punctuation mark in the text. Therefore, an operational coding rule must anchor either to explicit lexical recommendation markers in the text (such as *"I will use" or* *"the recommended approach is"*) or to the agent's underlying reasoning trace where an architectural choice is finalized, indicating where the supervisor's strategic intent becomes bound by the intermediary's unstated selection.
3.  **Verifiable Core Assertion Boundaries:** These are the terminal boundaries of truth-apt, checkable propositions upon which subsequent reasoning or task execution depends. In technical and analytical discourse, marking every factual statement as a decision space would over-segment, leading to the attentional fragmentation predicted by Proposition 6. Therefore, this criterion is limited to foundational premises, key parameter choices, mathematical derivations, or external citations whose invalidity would compromise downstream logic. The supervisor identifies a decision space at the boundary where such an assertion ends, giving them time to verify accuracy before subsequent claims introduce potential errors.
4.  **Situation Model Transitions:** These are structural shifts in the intermediary's operational narrative, indicated by a change in goal, cause, character, object, space, or time (Zacks et al., 2009). Event Segmentation Theory posits that human cognition naturally parses continuous activity into discrete events at points where these core situational dimensions change. This dynamic aligns with evidence that event boundaries in story listening correspond to Bayesian surprise as computed by a language model (Kumar et al., 2023). These semantic transitions create a decision space, indicating where working situation models require updating.

This operational rule clearly differentiates algorithmacy-based marking from both syntactic chunking and existing commercial interfaces. Syntactic segmentation divides text along formal grammatical units, such as clauses or sentences (Tsoukala et al., 2025). However, syntactic form does not necessarily align with supervisory necessity: a grammatically complex sentence may lack state-altering commitments, whereas a brief clause may determine an entire engineering architecture. Regarding commercial platforms, the distinction is twofold. While some tools have implemented pauses at a subset of Criterion 1 commitment points (such as Bash permission prompts and plan approvals), current front-ends are increasingly removing these stops to reduce prompt fatigue, and no commercial platform marks Criteria 2 through 4 (Section 3.1).

Additionally, applying these four criteria requires an explicit coding procedure. In experimental applications, decision spaces are annotated by independent coders using this rubric, with structural placement validated through inter-coder agreement (e.g., Cohen's; Section 5.1). This explicit operationalization establishes a reproducible basis for placing interface marks and enables empirical contrasts with continuous streams and arbitrary interruptions (Predictions P5–P7).

#### 4.6. Restored Perceptual Mechanism 1: Attenuation of Forward Pull and Restoration of Saccade Targets

The first perceptual mechanism restored by interface marking operates on oculomotor targeting and visual priority mapping (‭MC‬‭‬; Figure 4.1). In skilled reading across static text, interword spaces facilitate both lexical identification and saccadic programming—with the deficit of unspaced text falling more heavily on word identification (Rayner et al., 1998, p. 1143; Section 2.2). Crucially for oculomotor control, spaces provide spatial guides that demarcate lexical units and enhance upcoming words as targets for ballistic saccades (Rayner et al., 1998). When spaces are absent, saccadic targeting is disrupted: initial fixations shift systematically from near a word's center toward its beginning, reading rates drop by 44% to 58%, and regressions rise from 9.7% to 17.3% of eye movements (Rayner et al., 1998; Drieghe et al., 2017; Section 2.2). In visual search, an abrupt visual onset robustly captures selective attention when it appears as an isolated transient in an otherwise stable visual field (Yantis & Jonides, 1984; Section 2.3). Extending Wolfe's (2021) Guided Search model from visual search to text streaming, bottom-up salience operates as a transient weighting within an integrated visual priority map rather than an involuntary, irreversible seizure of gaze. Bottom-up salience is brief and depletes rapidly; as Wolfe (2021, p. 1069) suggests, if an environment affords a sufficient "break in the action," the visual priority map may reset, opening an opportunity for top-down supervisory goals to guide visual attention.

In an active token stream, this perceptual architecture changes fundamentally. Although a stream presents a rapid succession of characters rather than an isolated onset—a condition that in unstructured displays might dilute bottom-up capture (Yantis & Jonides, 1984)—streaming text confines these successive onsets to a single, highly localized spatial coordinate: the advancing leading edge. Because this kinetic frontier is the display's sole locus of continuous visual change while preceding text remains stationary, the model posits that the leading edge serves as an ongoing perceptual draw, repeatedly renewing bottom-up priority at the point of generation. In an empirical evaluation of chatbot text movement, Park and Lee (2024) observed that incrementally generated text at the bottom of the screen concentrates fixation starts at the point of token appearance, while inflating total fixation time and saccadic amplitudes relative to text presented statically at the top (Section 3.5). Here lies the decisive material divergence from static reading: on a printed page or static screen, orthographic targets remain stationary, leaving the reader free to pace inspection and target saccades according to internal comprehension needs. In a machine-paced stream, by contrast, the newest visual target continuously advances across the display, locking the supervisor into an externally paced tracking task that the theory posits depresses exploratory visual inspection.

Interface marking restores this mechanism by temporarily arresting the kinetic frontier. When the interface introduces a temporal pause at an operational decision space (‭MC‬‭‬), it halts the succession of abrupt onsets. With the cessation of new token appearances, the forward draw at the advancing edge is predicted to attenuate. This pause breaks the visual action and may allow the supervisor's visual priority map to reset (Wolfe, 2021), releasing gaze from the leading edge so top-down supervisory goals can take precedence. Furthermore, by establishing a stationary visual anchor on the display, marking borrows the saccade-targeting function of interword spacing (Rayner et al., 1998; Section 2.2). When an interface uses a persistent spatial delimiter without an accompanying pause, that mark must have sufficient visual contrast to compete bottom-up against the leading edge on the priority map; however, the primary attenuation of forward momentum comes from temporal pausing. Formally, the theory predicts that introducing an interface mark will attenuate the forward draw at the leading edge. In contrast, the mark persists, dispersing gaze away from the kinetic boundary and allowing the priority map to reset (Prediction P2), counteracting the forward capture described in Prediction P1. 

#### 4.7. Restored Perceptual Mechanism 2: Inducing Event Boundaries and Updating Situation Models

The second perceptual mechanism restored by interface marking involves cognitive event segmentation and working-memory updating (M → D; Figure 4.1). According to Event Segmentation Theory (EST; Zacks et al., 2007), human perceptual systems parse continuous activity into discrete units by maintaining internal event models, which are working-memory representations of the current state that generate forward predictions about incoming stimuli (Kurby & Zacks, 2008). During reading, perceivers construct multidimensional situation models that track core narrative dimensions such as characters, goals, causes, objects, spatial locations, and temporal intervals (Zacks et al., 2009). When these dimensions shift, prediction error increases, prompting the cognitive system to register an event boundary. Zacks, Speer, and Reynolds (2009, Exp. 3) demonstrated that shifts across these situational dimensions reliably predict where readers place event boundaries. Each additional situation change increased reading time by 92.8 ms, and each 10% rise in boundary probability added approximately 108 ms. We interpret this dwell as reflecting the cognitive cost of updating the situation model. These boundaries serve structural cognitive functions: readers update their situation models both globally at major episode boundaries and incrementally within episodes. Goals are mentioned more frequently at fine-grained boundaries, even when no explicit goal change has occurred (odds ratio = 2.29; Kurby & Zacks, 2012). Additionally, in story listening, human event boundaries track Bayesian surprise computed from a language model (Kumar et al., 2023).

The cognitive ability to segment continuous discourse does not depend on a self-paced, static page. In Zacks et al.'s (2009, Exp. 1) visual-sequential condition, participants read narrative clauses presented one at a time at an automated pace of approximately 3.35 words per second, without the possibility of review. Readers continued to place event boundaries above chance, demonstrating that segmentation persists under externally paced presentation. However, in that experiment, text was displayed clause by clause, with each clause onset introducing a distinct visual change. In contrast, an active generative stream presents tokens in a continuous, high-frequency sequence. The theory posits that updating an event model is cognitively demanding (Kurby & Zacks, 2008). Although segmentation persists under automated clause pacing, no empirical study has evaluated event boundary formation in an uninterrupted token stream or tested whether introducing structural marks facilitates model updating. The model proposes that an unbroken stream creates a cognitive trade-off: without explicit structural markers, the continuous influx of new characters may compete with the effortful updating of the situation model, leading the supervisor to focus on the kinetic leading edge rather than consolidating preceding meaning.

This dynamic clearly departs from traditional reading media. In static documents, discourse architecture is visually externalized through paragraph breaks, section headings, and white space, which provide spatial layout cues signaling semantic transitions. In contrast, streaming interfaces render headings and paragraph breaks only as they appear, offering no forward preview. As a result, the supervisor cannot anticipate upcoming structural transitions before the tokens are displayed. The supervisor must therefore identify operational transitions in real time under machine pacing, without advance layout cues, while the kinetic leading edge continuously draws visual attention forward.

Interface marking addresses this issue by introducing an explicit structural discontinuity into the stream (M → D). Although an interface mark functions as an external graphic or temporal delimiter rather than an intrinsic narrative shift, the theory posits that placing a mark at a functional decision space serves as an external perceptual cue that induces an event boundary. Temporarily pausing token delivery or visually demarcating the completed unit is predicted to prompt situation-model updating, signaling to the supervisor that an operational transition has occurred. This marking provides a perceptual break in the action, which may allow the visual priority map to reset (Wolfe, 2021) and may allow working memory to consolidate the intermediary's preceding assertions. Formally, the theory predicts that marks placed at latent decision spaces will function as event boundaries, prompting situation-model updating and boundary-token dwell (Prediction P4), and directing overt visual attention to the decision space (Prediction P5).

#### 4.8. Restored Perceptual Mechanism 3: Maintenance of an Unhurried Look-Back Window

The third perceptual mechanism restored by interface marking concerns spatial permanence and regressive saccadic review (M → E; Figure 4.1). In skilled reading, backward saccades to previously encountered text occur in approximately 10% to 15% of cases (Rayner, 1998, as cited in Schotter et al., 2014). Booth and Weger (2013) demonstrated that readers perform these regressions specifically to reread earlier text and address comprehension difficulties, rather than relying on location as a spatial memory cue. This rereading has measurable consequences for comprehension. In an experimental study of single-sentence reading, Schotter, Tran, and Rayner (2014) introduced a trailing mask that replaced words with strings of x's once the eyes moved past them; this manipulation reduced overall comprehension (b = -0.92, z = 5.09). Even on trials without a regression, comprehension declined from 78% in normal reading to 71% when the trailing mask eliminated the option to look back.

Additionally, Timkey et al. (2026) showed that forward fixations reflect localized word predictability (as indexed by language-model surprisal). In contrast, regressive rereading at points of structural disambiguation reflects the cognitive cost of detecting and repairing sentence-level structural representations. Therefore, evaluating complex claims in an unfolding discourse requires the spatial availability of preceding context to enable retrospective verification.

In dynamic text displays, the regressive repair mechanism encounters distinct physical constraints. Studies of horizontally scrolling text indicate that higher-level inferential comprehension is impaired and returning to earlier text becomes more difficult (Harvey & Walker, 2018). In research on document navigation, Haverkamp et al. (2023) found that readers who scrolled backtracked less frequently to earlier pages, but over greater distances, than those who paged. Conversational generative interfaces do not present static pages; instead, they continuously follow the leading edge. As new tokens appear, automated scrolling shifts preceding text upward, potentially pushing earlier assertions beyond the visible viewport or outside the reader's functional regression span. This creates an operational dilemma for the supervisor: either manually scroll backward to verify an earlier claim and lose sensory contact with incoming tokens, or follow the advancing leading edge to monitor ongoing execution while leaving preceding commitments unexamined.

This contrast highlights a key difference between static inscription and dynamic streaming. A printed page or static digital document remains spatially invariant, allowing the supervisor to initiate an unhurried regression at any time without temporal penalty. In contrast, an active token stream advances continuously. Because delivery pacing is set to an average reading rate rather than the individual reader's comprehension (Section 3.2), looking back imposes an immediate sensory cost: inspecting an upstream assertion requires relinquishing oversight of newly emitted tokens.

Interface marking addresses this tension by stabilizing the interaction surface and preserving the look-back window (M → E). When an interface introduces a temporal pause at an operational decision space, it halts both token emission and automated scrolling. This stabilization keeps preceding assertions visible and gives the supervisor the temporal flexibility to execute regressive saccades without incoming text scrolling out of view. If an interface uses a persistent spatial delimiter without a corresponding pause, preceding text remains visually accessible, but looking back still incurs the sensory cost of disengaging from the moving leading edge. The look-back window is fully stabilized only when emission pauses. Temporarily halting forward generation restores the physical stability necessary for retrospective structural repair (Booth & Weger, 2013; Schotter et al., 2014). The theory formally predicts that introducing an interface mark will widen the look-back window, preserving both the spatial availability and temporal duration required to inspect upstream text (Prediction P3), and that marks placed at functional decision spaces will direct more targeted regressions and fixations to those locations than an equivalent number of arbitrary breaks (Prediction P5).

#### 4.9. Ergonomic and Competence Moderators of Supervisory Navigation

The effectiveness of interface marking in restoring supervisory navigation varies across implementations. According to Proposition 6 and the nomological network (Figure 4.1), three structural properties of the intervention moderate the efficacy of an interface mark: placement, duration, and visual form, as well as the supervisor's mental model of the intermediary (MI).

The supervisory benefit of a mark is contingent on its alignment with meaningful task units. In psycholinguistics, introducing line breaks that arbitrarily divide clauses increases first-pass reading duration (d = 0.32) and total inspection time (d = 0.41). However, this effect was no longer significant after multiple-comparisons correction (false discovery rate), and statistical power was 52%, with readers recalibrating after repeated exposures (Tsoukala et al., 2025). In contrast, presenting text in sentence-by-sentence segments improves comprehension for beginner readers, an effect not attributable to wrap-up time (Koornneef, 2024). Similar patterns are observed in task-interruption research: interruptions during ongoing execution double task error rates, while interruptions at coarse task boundaries cause minimal disruption (Bailey & Konstan, 2006; Powers & Scerbo, 2023). Algorithmacy theory posits that marks aligned with functional decision spaces facilitate event boundary induction and look-back targeting (Predictions P4 and P5). Conversely, misaligned marks are hypothesized to disrupt attentional continuity, causing localized refixations and increasing cognitive workload without enhancing error interception (Prediction P7).

Temporal duration also constrains supervisory intervention. In a study of interface lockouts, Gould et al. (2016) found that longer lockouts increased checking in laboratory settings, while on a crowdsourcing platform, they increased task switching. The authors concluded that duration "needs to be carefully calibrated." Algorithmacy theory conceptualizes this trade-off as an inverted-U relationship between mark duration and supervisory navigation (Prediction P10): brief temporal halts are predicted to interrupt forward momentum and provide the necessary window for verification, whereas excessively long delays may induce secondary task switching and operational friction.

Visual form determines whether a mark effectively signals an operational juncture without imposing additional reading burden. A mark should indicate the presence of a decision space without requiring the supervisor to interpret supplementary text. In an eye-tracking study of static AI-assisted news reading, Prajod et al. (2026, preprint) found that a concise, single-line AI-disclosure label attracted greater total fixation duration (58.70 s vs. 49.46 s, d = 0.34) and elicited more saccades than a detailed, paragraph-length disclosure. Similar to typographical signaling devices and section headings, which accelerate first-pass reading and guide look-backs (Hyönä & Lorch, 2004; Lorch, 1989), the visual form of an interface mark—whether as a calibrated pause, horizontal rule, or minimal status icon—is hypothesized to direct visual attention to the decision space without introducing extraneous text.

Beyond the mark's ergonomic properties, the supervisor's mental model of the intermediary (MI) fundamentally shapes the interaction surface. Algorithmacy diverges from traditional literacy in this respect. In reading science, fluency marks competence: skilled readers decode orthography effortlessly, freeing working memory for comprehension (though Duke & Cartwright, 2021, caution that decoding fluency does not guarantee comprehension). However, when users rely on an algorithmic intermediary, fluency may indicate cognitive capture. Experienced users adapt to system regularities, but in current commercial front-ends, features such as paced emission velocity (Section 3.2), confident syntax, and finished recommendation framing (Section 3.3) serve platform-level objectives, including maintaining workflow and reducing prompt fatigue (Section 3.1). The theory suggests that in an unmarked stream, fluency aligns with co-optation: users accept output rapidly, follow the moving cursor, and remain passive to the intermediary's implicit commitments.

Interface marking is hypothesized to address this tension by providing a structural grammar to which supervisory fluency can adapt, rather than enforcing slow, laborious checking at every turn. Natural reading offers a foundational precedent: skilled adult readers consistently rely on interword spaces, and when these are removed, reading speed is halved and regressive eye movements nearly double (Rayner et al., 1998; Section 2.2). In a marked stream, supervisory fluency is expected to adapt to the structural marks. Rather than requiring exhaustive scrutiny, explicit marks let supervisors automatically orient to decision spaces, executing rapid, selective fixations and targeted regressions to diagnostic tokens. The model proposes that for novice supervisors with developing mental models, a marked decision space prompts deliberate decoding. In contrast, for expert supervisors, marking provides the visual anchors necessary for rapid, calibrated steering.

This developmental account reframes what counts as empirical evidence of algorithmacy and clarifies the interpretation of fixational dwell. Supervisory navigation (G) is operationalized as acting upon a decision space—accepting, verifying, rejecting, or redirecting the agent—not deliberating indefinitely. A brief, transient elevation in dwell at a mark reflects initial orienting to an induced event boundary (Prediction P4). By contrast, extended fixational dwell at a mark is predicted to indicate effortful novice decoding or localized processing disruption rather than supervisory mastery. Functional orienting differs from processing difficulty in the subsequent action: transient dwell yields targeted look-backs and successful navigation, whereas prolonged dwell without intervention signals gaze dissociation or an inadequate mental model. Consequently, algorithmacy theory predicts that supervisors who possess a more accurate mental model of the intermediary will convert ocular fixations into corrective navigation faster, deploying brief, highly targeted regressions rather than accumulating extended dwell time (Prediction P11). Finally, by providing external visual scaffolding that renders latent decision points perceptible, interface marking is hypothesized to narrow the performance gap between novices and experts, partially compensating for an incomplete internal model of the intermediary (MMIG‬‭‬‭‬ Prediction P12).

#### 4.10. Dissociating Attention from Navigation

Algorithmacy theory separates two constructs that evaluations in human–computer interaction risk conflating: visual inspection (F) and supervisory navigation (G). Where evaluations rely on the eye–mind hypothesis (Just & Carpenter, 1980) without qualification, researchers risk treating foveal fixations, dwell time, and saccadic targeting as direct proxies for cognitive scrutiny and supervisory vigilance. The nomological network (Figure 4.1) rejects this equivalence. Fixations and regressive saccades directed to a decision space (F) provide evidence that the supervisor's visual system has engaged the boundary; supervisory navigation (G) is what the human does with that perceptual encounter: accept, verify, reject, or redirect the intermediary's trajectory.

This conceptual separation has empirical warrant across automation and interface research. In an eye-tracking evaluation of four static chatbot layouts, Cho and Lim (2026) found that manipulating citation placement moved the latency of first fixations on source attributions from 0.96 s to 15.65 s, yet gaze duration did not predict trust; as they concluded, "Attention moved without moving judgment" (§3.4). In automation supervision, Parasuraman and Manzey (2010, p. 403) invoked the concept of "looking-but-not-seeing" to explain complacency and commission errors, drawing on findings that human operators frequently sample all diagnostic information necessary to identify a system failure yet fail to register the anomaly (e.g., Manzey et al., 2008, where roughly half of participants who accepted an incorrect automated diagnosis had sampled all required instruments). In pedestrian interaction with automated vehicles, Yang et al. (2027) observed that augmented-reality cues shortened fixational dwell on approaching vehicles and on the cues themselves (d = -0.80), but cautioned that shorter dwell "may also indicate overreliance" (§5)—an external visual mark that sped crossing decisions without ensuring calibrated inspection. Furthermore, in a systematic review of 23 eye-tracking investigations across AI-generated content (spanning attention, cognitive processing, and engagement), Resulbegoviq et al. (2026) observed that "no modality-independent gaze pattern emerged," noting that longer viewing more often reflected cognitive processing difficulty or checking than user preference. Under certain conditions, ocular gaze can distinguish over-, under-, and appropriate reliance at runtime (Wu et al., 2025). In dynamic generative streams, however, the theory holds that foveal dwell alone does not demonstrate supervisory discernment: looking at an advancing token or prompt confirms that the human visual system is engaged, but does not confirm that the supervisor has perceived the latent commitments embedded in the computation.

Decoupling visual inspection from supervisory navigation renders the perceptual layer of algorithmacy theory explicitly falsifiable without circular escape clauses. To prevent supervisory failures from being tautologically attributed to an unmeasured mental model or persuasive co-optation, experimental evaluations must measure or control for both factors in advance (Section 5.1). The navigational claim (FG‬‭‬ ) is definitively falsified if introducing marks at decision spaces significantly increases fixations and regressions at those loci (F) but fails to elevate supervisory navigation (G) among supervisors with a verified, accurate mental model of the intermediary (MI) under low-co-optation baseline conditions (e.g., raw information presentation, disfluent syntax, and the absence of recommendation framing; Predictions P8, P9, and P11). Conversely, if interface marks improve error detection and corrective steering (G) on trials where the supervisor never fixates the marked decision space—operationalized as the absence of any foveal fixation within an area of interest subtending 2° of visual angle around the mark—the theory's core causal architecture collapses. Such a result would directly falsify Proposition 5 and Prediction P13 (which establish visual inspection as a necessary oculomotor prerequisite), demonstrating that supervisory gains must be driven by an alternative mechanism, such as non-specific temporal delay or unmeasured peripheral detection, rather than the perceptual restoration of foveal saccadic targets and event boundaries.

#### 4.11. Distinguishing Marking Decision Spaces from Related Interventions

To delineate theoretical boundaries, interface marking should be differentiated from four adjacent classes of design intervention in human–computer interaction and automation oversight: cognitive forcing functions, microboundaries, selective frictions, and concept suppression. Although each approach aims to disrupt unreflective human compliance, their mechanisms differ from the perceptual restructuring proposed by algorithmacy theory.

First, interface marking differs from cognitive forcing functions (CFFs) primarily in where the delay occurs, not in its mere presence. Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos (2021) demonstrated that cognitive forcing functions disrupt heuristic processing by introducing an extraneous task or a mandatory delay, such as a 30-second wait labeled "AI is processing" (§3.2). Under these conditions, overreliance on incorrect model predictions decreased (0.48 vs. 0.64, *d* = 0.36), although participants reported lower satisfaction and trust, and perceived the designs as more complex (Table 3). While a pause-form mark also introduces a temporal delay (Section 4.9), Buçinca et al.'s wait condition is the closest design analog to algorithmacy-based marking. The essential distinction is where the delay occurs: cognitive forcing functions impose an interruption at a point the interface designer selects, whereas interface marking pauses delivery at an operational decision space inherent to the computational process (Proposition 1). By anchoring the pause to the intermediary's state commitments, branch points, or core assertions (Section 4.5), marking reinstates an existing structural boundary rather than introducing an extrinsic delay. This distinction is directly assessed by comparing semantic marks to arbitrary periodic pauses (Predictions P5–P7).

Second, marking is distinct from microboundaries. Cox et al. (2016) define a microboundary as "a small obstacle before an interaction" intended to introduce a brief time cost that shifts behavior from automatic to deliberate. Microboundaries typically appear before a user action, such as confirmation dialogs before tool execution, to prompt reflection before the user commits input. In contrast, interface marking occurs where the intermediary makes an architectural commitment, collapses competing paths, or completes a checkable assertion (Section 4.5). This approach structures the perception of unfolding machine output, coordinating visual attention and situation-model updating as the computational process advances.

Third, marking is distinct from selective frictions. Collins et al. (2024, preprint) investigated interface-level obstacles, such as requiring an additional button click to access model output, and found that friction reduced click-through rates with minimal impact on decision accuracy, with effects extending to other topics. Similarly, Arun et al. (2026) introduced preparatory friction before AI completion, noting that mental demand and subjective trust increased, but self-reported reliance remained unchanged. These selective frictions create procedural barriers to accessing the model or requesting completion. In contrast, interface marking does not obstruct access to the model; instead, it changes the visual presentation of the generated stream, turning an undifferentiated kinetic sequence into a punctuated interaction surface.

Finally, marking diverges from model-level semantic interventions such as concept suppression. In an ideation task, Disley and Khan (2026) showed that suppressing a target concept at the model level (for example, the "Unlearning to Rest" condition) changed the system's output and increased user ownership compared to an unmodified model. In their discussion, Disley and Khan explicitly contrasted model-level interventions with interface-level friction ("an added step, a delay," §2.3) and advocated for empirical comparisons between the two (§7.9). Interface marking responds to this call at the interface layer: it preserves the intermediary's generative weights, vocabulary, and generated output. It does not alter the model's output or the validity of its claims; it only changes how the supervisor receives that output.

Across these distinctions, algorithmacy-based marking retains the same property. Marking does not impose a process-unanchored penalty, restrict access before human action, or alter the model's output. Instead, it imparts a structural grammar to the interaction surface, making visible the decision spaces inherent in the process and determined by the intermediary's assertions rather than by fixed rates or arbitrary intervals.

### 5\. Discussion: Implications and Research Agenda

The perceptual layer of algorithmacy theory advances previous accounts of human–AI interaction and supervisory control in three distinct ways. First, it shifts the theoretical locus of competence from retrospective knowledge and procedural awareness of algorithms—the historical province of algorithmic literacy (Ridley & Pawlick-Potts, 2021)—to real-time supervisory navigation of an opaque, autonomous intermediary, grounding this capability in the perceptual dynamics of gaze allocation and event segmentation. Second, it redirects analytical focus to the internal structure of the generative stream: rather than treating token delivery solely as a problem of emission velocity, conversational typing skeuomorphism, or post-hoc disclosure, the framework isolates where latent decision spaces reside within the computational process and examines whether the interface renders them visible. Third, by conceptually separating visual inspection (F) from supervisory action (G=FMI‬‭‬‭‬ , before the depressive drag of co-optation H), the theory decouples looking from seeing. It transforms the gap between foveal engagement and critical judgment from a methodological nuisance into an empirically tractable, measurable phenomenon.

Technical systems do not merely deliver information; they structure human attention, allocate authority, and set the default terms of judgment. When an interface presents computational generation as an uninterrupted, frictionless cascade, it does not merely accelerate delivery; it enacts an architectural politics that disarms critical scrutiny in the very moment it simulates effortless assistance. What follows translates this theoretical perspective into an empirical, design, and sociotechnical agenda. Section 5.1 formulates an empirical protocol to rigorously test the theory’s central predictions using eye-tracking and seeded-error paradigms. Section 5.2 translates the framework into specific interface design principles for generative systems. Section 5.3 examines the theoretical scope of algorithmacy beyond natural-language streaming, extending to agentic and embodied decision environments. Finally, Section 5.4 confronts two dialectical counterpoints: user resistance to interface-imposed friction and the critique of autonomous competence models from New Literacy Studies.

#### 5.1. Severe Testing: An Empirical Evaluation Strategy

Following Cutting and Deterding (2022, §5.1), who prioritized the "severe testing" of their own theoretical framework, algorithmacy theory's decisive obligation is to specify the precise empirical conditions under which its core claims risk refutation. The central hypothesis does not simply assert that pauses help people think; it asserts that supervisory vigilance depends strictly upon *structural alignment*. If introducing temporal pauses at latent decision spaces facilitates supervisory navigation no more effectively than an identical number of pauses inserted at arbitrary points, the benefits of interface intervention must be attributed to non-specific temporal delay rather than structural event segmentation. Such an outcome would cleanly falsify the theory’s claims about perceiving decision boundaries (Predictions P5, P6, and P7).

To put these propositions in empirical jeopardy, I specify a three-condition, within-subjects eye-tracking protocol (N = 42) that compares supervisor oversight across an evolving token stream containing seeded factual, logical, and procedural errors. An *a priori* power analysis for a repeated-measures design (within-subjects, 3 measurements, assumed medium effect size f = 0.22, ‬ = 0.05, statistical power 1 - ‬ = 0.85, and repeated-measures correlation r = 0.50) indicates a minimum required sample of N = 32 participants. Recruiting N = 42 provides a planned buffer for up to 20% data loss resulting from eye-tracking calibration drift, track loss, or blink artifacts in dynamic reading tasks (cf. Cho & Lim, 2026; Park & Lee, 2024). Participants monitor text generated in response to complex technical, analytical, and planning tasks. To isolate interaction design effects from generative variability, experimental trials replay pre-generated model outputs, keeping linguistic content, syntactic complexity, and error placement invariant across conditions. The experiment compares three presentation conditions:

1.  **Continuous Stream (Baseline):** Text materializes incrementally at a calibrated token velocity without interruption, reflecting standard commercial streaming front-ends.
2.  **Arbitrary Pauses (Yoked Syntactic Control):** The stream pauses at syntactically aligned points that are *not* functional decision spaces (e.g., standard sentence or clause boundaries lacking state commitments, branching choices, or core assertions). These pauses are yoked to match Condition 3 precisely in total pause count and cumulative duration, isolating the functional relevance of the boundary from both generic temporal delay and syntactically disruptive fragmentation.
3.  **Decision-Space Pauses (Theorized Intervention):** The stream pauses exclusively at operational decision spaces identified using the four-part operational rule formulated in Section 4.5 (*Consequential Commitment Points*, *Branching Plurality Junctures*, *Verifiable Core Assertion Boundaries*, and *Situation Model Transitions*).

Stimulus domains are counterbalanced across conditions using a 3 x 3 Latin square design to prevent order and habituation effects. Stimuli contain two distinct structural classes of seeded errors: local factual distortions (e.g., inverted figures or misattributed findings within a single clause) and cross-paragraph inferential contradictions (e.g., an architectural conclusion conflicting directly with a premise established upstream). To prevent Condition 3 from winning trivially by placing pauses immediately adjacent to verifiable assertions, error placement is structurally balanced: half of the seeded errors occur within designated decision spaces, while the other half are positioned at non-decision intervals, with the physical distance between errors and the nearest pause strictly matched across Conditions 2 and 3.

Before the experimental trials, we measure each participant's baseline mental model of the intermediary (MI) using an objective diagnostic instrument that assesses familiarity with LLM generation characteristics, typical failure modes (e.g., fluent hallucinations, unstated trajectory pruning), and system boundary conditions. Measuring MI in advance prevents supervisory failures from being tautologically attributed to missing system knowledge, enabling a direct empirical test of gaze dissociation (Prediction P9), conversion velocity (Prediction P11), and compensatory scaffolding (Prediction P12).

The experiment addresses four formal research questions:

  - **RQ1 (Attentional Allocation):** Do pauses placed at functional decision spaces elicit a higher proportion of fixations and regressive saccades to those specific loci compared to an identical number of yoked pauses at non-decision syntactic boundaries (Testing Prediction P5)?
  - **RQ2 (Supervisory Accuracy):** Do decision-space pauses improve the detection and correction of seeded factual and inferential errors relative to both an unbroken stream and yoked syntactic pauses (Testing Prediction P6)?
  - **RQ3 (Cost of Syntactic Misalignment):** In an exploratory nested contrast comparing syntactically aligned pauses with arbitrary mid-clause pauses, do misaligned breaks impose penalties in detection latency, subjective workload (NASA-TLX), and localized refixations at the pause locus (Testing Prediction P7)?
  - **RQ4 (Mental-Model Moderation):** Does the participant's prior mental model of the intermediary (MI) moderate the speed with which fixations at marked decision spaces convert into corrective action, and does marking disproportionately scaffold participants with lower baseline MI (Testing Predictions P11 and P12)?

Process and outcome measures are recorded concurrently. We capture eye movements using a high-sampling-rate eye tracker with velocity-based fixation classification (I-VT). Process metrics include first-pass fixation duration, total dwell time, and the spatial landing density of regressive saccades within dynamic areas of interest (AOIs) subtending 2° of visual angle around each decision space, seeded error, and pause token. Behavioral outcome measures comprise error-detection hit rates, false alarm rates, detection latency, subjective workload on the NASA-TLX, and calibrated trust, calculated as the alignment between subjective confidence ratings and objective model accuracy across trials (cf. Cho & Lim, 2026). Measuring both channels is essential: shorter fixational dwell on an automated cue may indicate uncritical overreliance rather than efficient inspection (Yang et al., 2027); paired outcome data are required to determine whether brief dwell reflects rapid expert conversion (Prediction P11) or supervisory inattention.

The baseline emission rate must be calibrated to avoid confounding structural effects with basic sensory overload. In testing conversational chatbots, Xiao and Yang (2025) measured the "Streaming-Reading Alignment Rate," the rate at which token delivery stays ahead of 99% of readers' comfortable reading speeds, to optimize server compute, and found thresholds of 11.97 words per second for complex prose and 21.20 words per second for simple text. An emission rate of 11.97 words per second exceeds the comfortable reading speed of almost all readers, compared to typical silent reading rates of approximately 4.8 tokens per second (Liu et al., 2024) and automated pacing baselines of 3.35 words per second in event-segmentation research (Zacks et al., 2009). Presenting text at this ceiling would induce severe reading discomfort. Conversely, the approximately 45 words per second emitted by commercial APIs (such as GPT-4o; Xiao & Yang, 2025) far exceeds human perceptual limits. In this protocol, we calibrate each participant's comfortable reading velocity before experimental trials using an adaptive Parameter Estimation by Sequential Testing (PEST) staircase procedure (Xiao & Yang, 2025), establishing a personalized baseline that tests the stream's kinetic structure without confounding results with reading discomfort.

Two methodological caveats define this protocol's boundary conditions. First, the empirical literature on text presentation has primarily evaluated static displays or experimenter-paced sequential text; only two studies of conversational chatbot streaming displays have recorded eye movements or perceptual evaluations (Bhat, 2025; Park & Lee, 2024). Studies of related phenomena, such as typing delays (Anderl et al., 2024; Gnewuch et al., 2022) and system response latency (Tan et al., 2026), examine pre-response waiting times rather than the internal structure of the emitted stream. Furthermore, of the two streaming studies, only Park and Lee recorded eye-tracking data, and their design omitted measures of comprehension and supervisory accuracy. Second, replaying pre-generated text isolates interaction structure from generative variance but temporarily suspends the bidirectional interactivity of live use, where supervisors can intervene mid-sentence, scroll backward manually, or request alternative completions. Testing the framework under fully interactive conditions will constitute the next empirical stage once the baseline perceptual effects of structural marking are established.

#### 5.2. Interface Design Implications for Generative Systems

To move from diagnostic critique to generative interface design, algorithmacy theory demands a fundamental shift in how human–AI interaction surfaces are architected. Current commercial front-ends treat text generation as a theatrical performance of thought. By emitting tokens in an unbroken, kinetic cadence, the interface mimics the immediacy of human conversation while concealing the machine's true operational commitments. Power in these interfaces hides precisely in the smoothness of the delivery. The design imperative that follows from this framework is straightforward: **an interface must render the latent structure of computation visible to human perception.**

Rather than allowing serving infrastructure and network buffering to dictate the tempo of interaction, interfaces should embody the four operational criteria formulated in Section 4.5 through distinct interaction patterns:

1.  **Staging Operational Commitments (Criterion 1):** Where an intermediary prepares an irreversible state change—invoking an external API, writing code to a disk, or dispatching an external communication—the interface must not bundle the execution inside an auto-scrolling terminal window. Today's commercial agentic environments often engineer away confirmation prompts under the guise of "reducing friction" or "mitigating prompt fatigue". In doing so, they convert the supervisor from an active director into a passive spectator. Marking these junctures requires a mandatory temporal halt coupled with a visual delimiter, ensuring the supervisor's look-back window remains uncorrupted before execution proceeds.
2.  **Exposing Collapsed Plurality (Criterion 2):** When a model encounters multiple divergent architectural or analytical paths, standard commercial practice directs the model to silently collapse these alternatives into a single recommended trajectory (Ronacher, 2025). This design choice conceals discarded options behind a facade of synthetic confidence. An algorithmate interface must mark these branching points explicitly. Instead of printing a flat declarative assertion (*"I will refactor the database schema using migration pattern B"*), the interface should introduce an interactive branching anchor that renders the collapsed options inspectable on demand, converting a hidden systemic commitment into an explicit choice point.
3.  **Stabilizing Verifiable Assertions (Criterion 3):** When a stream articulates foundational empirical claims, quantitative parameters, or algorithmic equations upon which subsequent logic depends, the interface must grant the supervisor the perceptual leeway to inspect them. Under current designs, automated scrolling continually displaces these critical assertions upward, forcing the user to chase the advancing cursor or surrender oversight entirely. An algorithmic interface stabilizes the display around core assertion boundaries, preserving spatial permanence until the supervisor either actively releases the stream or continues reading.
4.  **Signaling Situation-Model Transitions (Criterion 4):** As an agentic workflow transitions across goals, agents, or analytical domains, the interface should visually punctuate these cognitive boundaries. Following Event Segmentation Theory (Zacks et al., 2009), human understanding relies on structural delimiters to clear working memory and consolidate situational models. Incorporating subtle typographic rules, white space, or thematic breaks at points of great Bayesian surprise restores the organizing visual syntax that traditional print provided for centuries.

Crucially, implementing these marks does not require cluttering the display with verbose disclaimers or intrusive modal dialogs. As Prajod et al. (2026) demonstrated, dense, explanatory disclosures often induce visual avoidance, whereas minimal, well-calibrated typographical cues successfully direct gaze without inflating cognitive fatigue. Just as ancient readers did not require explicit instructions to interpret the blank spaces between words, modern supervisors do not require complex meta-commentary to interpret an interface mark. They require only an interaction surface that pauses its relentless forward motion long enough for human judgment to intervene.

#### 5.3. Beyond the Text Stream: Agentic and Embodied Decision Environments

While this paper formalizes algorithmacy at the perceptual layer of conversational text, the theoretical constructs of latent decision spaces, perceptual suppression, and structural marking extend far beyond the chat window. The token stream is merely the most visible contemporary manifestation of a broader, more pervasive sociotechnical transformation: the delegation of human agency to autonomous computational intermediaries operating in real time.

Consider autonomous software engineering agents (e.g., multi-file agentic coding environments; Chen et al., 2026). In these systems, the interaction surface is no longer a simple prose dialogue; it is a rapid, multi-threaded workspace where an autonomous intermediary navigates dependencies, alters configuration files, and executes shell scripts. The failure of human supervision in these environments rarely stems from an inability to read code diffs. It stems from a temporal and structural mismatch: the agent executes a rapid succession of operations, collapsing strategic trade-offs behind a single aggregated status update. By the time the human supervisor notices an erroneous architectural decision, the agent has built multiple dependent layers upon that flawed foundation.

Here, algorithmacy operates as the capacity to detect and steer the agent’s trajectory *before* execution cascades into irreversible commitments. The four-part operational rule applies directly to this domain:

  - The system must mark tool invocations (Criterion 1).
  - It must expose architectural trade-offs where the agent chose one library or pattern over another (Criterion 2).
  - It must isolate foundational parameter choices (Criterion 3).
  - It must punctuate transitions between high-level planning and low-level refactoring (Criterion 4).

The reach of algorithmacy extends further still—from digital terminal windows to the physical architecture of daily life. Corporate enterprises increasingly deploy computational intermediaries into physical space under the commercial moniker of the "phygital". In modern logistics warehouses, gig-economy dispatch platforms, and algorithmic retail spaces, opaque, real-time computational systems orchestrate human participants' physical movements. These hybrid environments embody four operational marks of decision experience design:

  - **Mark 1 (Distinct Intermediary):** An administratively and economically distinct algorithmic third party stands between counterparties (e.g., workers and consumers, or drivers and passengers) who hold divergent interests in the outcome (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024; Simmel, 1902b).
  - **Mark 2 (Bilateral Telemetry):** The system continuously ingests operational data from both human counterparties during the encounter, monitoring spatial coordinates, acceptance latencies, and physical biometrics in real time rather than relying on static historical records.
  - **Mark 3 (Mutual Binding):** The intermediary commits an outcome—a pricing match, a dispatch route, or an administrative evaluation—that neither party can unilaterally or costlessly alter within the physical setting (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016).
  - **Mark 4 (Dynamic Rule Drift):** The matching formulas, allocation weights, and pricing multipliers drift continuously across encounters based on machine learning retraining cycles and broader platform optimization goals, presenting a perpetually shifting administrative target.

In a curbside ride-hail transaction or an algorithmic distribution center, the worker experiences the same perceptual and structural suppression that confronts the reader of a token stream. The platform emits instructions at a pace calibrated to platform throughput; it frames dispatch decisions as finished recommendations; and it conceals the latent alternatives that were discarded by the matching algorithm. Under these conditions, the worker loses both voice and exit (Hirschman, 1970). In this embodied realm, algorithmacy is nothing less than the literacy required to navigate an environment governed by automated arbiters. It is the competence to recognize when an algorithmic intermediary has bound one's physical body, to perceive the latent decision spaces where human discretion can still be asserted, and to resist the subtle co-optation of the automated flow.

#### 5.4. Dialectical Counterpoints

To prevent algorithmacy theory from lapsing into uncritical advocacy, this framework must confront two formidable counter-arguments. The first arises from human–computer interaction and behavioral economics: the claim that users will actively resist interface friction in favor of seamless convenience. The second arises from New Literacy Studies and critical sociology: the charge that positing "algorithmacy" as a general cognitive competence commits the very "autonomous model" fallacy that historically compromised functional accounts of literacy. Both objections deserve a rigorous, dialectical defense.

Counterpoint 1: Friction Reactance and the Tyranny of Convenience

The first objection presses an uncomfortable commercial and behavioral reality: *users do not want friction.* For half a century, personal computing and interface design have marched toward seamlessness. Every microsecond shaved from page loads, every keystroke eliminated by autocomplete, and every confirmation prompt suppressed by auto-approval has been celebrated as an unambiguous victory for user experience. If algorithmacy theory requires inserting pauses, breaks, and inspection windows into an otherwise fluent generative stream, will users not experience this as an intolerable regression?

The evidence for user reactance against friction is substantive. In evaluating cognitive forcing functions, Buçinca et al. (2021) found that while mandatory delays reduced overreliance, participants liked and trusted the forcing designs significantly less, rating them as frustrating and complex. Similarly, Gould et al. (2016) demonstrated that when interfaces impose artificial lockouts, users on crowdsourcing platforms frequently abandon the task or switch to secondary browser tabs. If an interface deliberately pauses generation to create an event boundary, impatient supervisors may simply install browser extensions to bypass the pauses, open a secondary chat window, or migrate to commercial platforms that promise frictionless speed. By introducing friction into an ecosystem built on the worship of velocity, does algorithmacy theory not design against human nature?

This objection identifies a genuine failure mode of naive interface design, but it misconstrues the nature of interface marking. Algorithmacy theory does not advocate cognitive forcing functions, access barriers, or artificial delays arbitrarily appended to an interface. As Section 4.11 shows, cognitive forcing functions impose delays at points the designer chooses arbitrarily, often requiring users to perform secondary, irrelevant tasks that interrupt cognitive momentum. Interface marking, by contrast, pauses exclusively at the operational decision spaces inherent to the process itself.

The historical precedent of interword spacing provides the decisive rebuttal. When medieval scribes introduced blank spaces between words, they did not intend to slow readers down. To a reader accustomed to the continuous oral chant of *scriptio continua*, blank spaces might initially have seemed like an unnecessary visual interruption. Yet interword spacing did not create friction; it created *legibility*. It offloaded the exhausting cognitive labor of phonological decoding from working memory to the eye, ultimately enabling silent, reflective reading at velocities unimaginable in antiquity.

The same logic governs algorithmacy-based marking. In an unmarked stream, the supervisor is trapped in an exhausting cognitive double-bind: forced to maintain sensory contact with a moving cursor while simultaneously attempting to evaluate complex computational claims. By pausing at meaningful decision boundaries, marking does not impose friction; it provides structural grammar. It gives the supervisor's visual system a stable place to land and working memory an opportunity to consolidate meaning. Seamlessness is not neutral; in an asymmetrical interaction with an automated intermediary, seamlessness is the very mechanism of co-optation. When an interface strips away every visible seam, it does not liberate the human supervisor; it disarms them—marking restores the seam, transforming an overwhelming kinetic chase into a legible, navigable collaboration.

Counterpoint 2: The "Autonomous Model" Critique from New Literacy Studies

The second counterpoint challenges the framework's theoretical foundation. In defining "algorithmacy" as a joint competence (G=FMI‬‭‬‭‬) patterned on Hoover and Gough’s (1990) Simple View of Reading, does this theory revive the discredited "autonomous" model of literacy?

For decades, New Literacy Studies scholarship has dismantled the assumption that literacy is a neutral, universal cognitive technology that automatically elevates human intellectual capacity (Street, 2003). As Street (2003) demonstrated, treating literacy as an "autonomous" skill disguises the cultural, economic, and institutional power relations that determine what counts as legitimate reading. Cross-cultural psychological investigations decisively corroborate this critique: in their landmark study of the Vai people of Liberia, Scribner and Cole (1978) proved that acquiring an indigenous script did not produce general cognitive enhancements, abstract reasoning skills, or intellectual transformations. The cognitive consequences of literacy were strictly tied to specific, socially situated practices—such as letter writing, trade accounting, or Quranic recitation—rather than a universal restructuring of the human mind.

If literacy failed as an autonomous, universal model of cognitive empowerment, why should algorithmacy succeed? Does positing that supervisors require a distinct "algorithmacy" to govern AI streaming interfaces not risk committing the identical theoretical overreach? Does it not treat what is fundamentally a political struggle over algorithmic authority as a matter of perceptual training and graphic layout?

Algorithmacy theory accepts Scribner and Cole's (1978) and Street's (2003) empirical findings as essential boundary conditions. The framework posits no general, transformative restructuring of human intelligence. It does not claim that learning to supervise an AI stream transforms general logical reasoning or elevates abstract problem-solving capacity. As modern neurocognitive research shows, reading acquisition reorganizes highly specific, dedicated visual and phonological circuits rather than altering general intellect (Dehaene et al., 2015); similarly, algorithmacy theory predicts only specific perceptual and operational consequences from the concrete practice of supervising streamed computational text.

Crucially, the theory's formal architecture explicitly guards against technological determinism. Proposition 5 and the multiplicative formula (G=FMI‬‭‬‭‬) establish that visual inspection of an interface mark (F) is an oculomotor prerequisite, but is utterly incapable of producing supervisory control on its own. If an operator lacks an internal representation of the intermediary's failure modes, platform incentives, and ideological biases (MI), looking at a marked decision space yields zero supervisory navigation. The mark cannot provide competence; it can only make the decision space perceptible.

Far from disguising power relations beneath a veneer of cognitive neutrality, algorithmacy theory begins from the premise that the interaction surface is an inherently political arena. In triadic platform arrangements, the automated intermediary is not a neutral assistant; it is an interested third party designed to optimize corporate objectives, maintain engagement, and extract compliance (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024; Simmel, 1902b). By concealing decision spaces beneath a continuous, fluent stream, the platform actively depresses human voice and closes off meaningful exit (Hirschman, 1970). Demanding interface marks at latent decision spaces is not fetishizing a graphic design pattern; it is demanding that the technical artifacts governing our cognitive lives reveal where they make binding choices. Algorithmacy is the situated practice of locating those choices, refusing blind compliance, and reclaiming human agency within an automated world.

### 

### 6\. Conclusion

Human oversight of generative artificial intelligence cannot be achieved solely by increasing literacy, such as providing more post-hoc explanations, automated confidence scores, or user education in machine learning capabilities (Ridley & Pawlick-Potts, 2021). These interventions remain constrained by the literacy trap, positioning the human operator as a retrospective reader of a completed output. In contrast, a streamed response is not a finished text during supervision; it is a real-time performance executed by an autonomous intermediary at a pace determined by server computation (Xiao & Yang, 2025). This process progressively removes three sensorimotor affordances essential for skilled reading: a stationary visual target for saccadic programming, an explicit event boundary for updating the situation model, and an unhurried window for regressive inspection. As a result, within this continuous flow, operational junctures requiring human judgment become indistinguishable from those that merely require passive acceptance.

Algorithmacy theory offers the conceptual vocabulary and empirical framework necessary to address this dilemma. It characterizes opaque, state-altering junctures within automated processes as latent decision spaces and defines algorithmacy as the capacity to detect, evaluate, and influence them. At the perceptual level, the theory formalizes how kinetic token delivery suppresses oversight by creating a continuous forward draw at the advancing edge (Park & Lee, 2024). It also specifies how interface marking can restore visual saccadic targets, induce cognitive event segmentation (Zacks et al., 2009), and maintain an unhurried look-back window (Schotter et al., 2014). The framework further establishes that visual engagement is a necessary oculomotor prerequisite but does not guarantee control. Gaze allocation translates into supervisory navigation only when coordinated with the supervisor's operative mental model of the intermediary (G=FMI‬‭‬‭‬), while persuasive fluency and recommendation framing can undermine critical evaluation. Thus, the theory accounts for the empirical puzzle identified at the outset: supervisors systematically attribute elevated authority to kinetic text (Bhat, 2025) and endorse fluent assertions regardless of factual accuracy (Kim et al., 2025), because the uninterrupted stream does not indicate where to intervene.

Just as medieval readers could not overcome the oral constraints of *scriptio continua* until scribal innovations introduced spaces between words (Saenger, 1997), contemporary supervisors cannot effectively govern autonomous systems through traditional literacy alone. When an interface presents computation as a seamless, effortless stream, it does not democratize access; instead, it conceals the intermediary's commitments behind synthetic fluency. While human–computer interaction research has long questioned whether users can comprehend artificial intelligence outputs, algorithmacy theory shifts the focus to whether interfaces enable users to identify where decision points are presented.
