## 3. Streaming in Current HCI, Automation, and LLM-Reliance Accounts

Given what Section 2 establishes about reading — that it depends on stable saccade targets, on boundaries that prompt the updating of event models, and on an unhurried path back to earlier text — one would expect the internal structure of a stream to figure prominently in current accounts of how people supervise generative systems. It does not. Four literatures come closest: research on streaming and pacing in generative interfaces, research on the supervision of automation, research on overreliance on large language models and on the friction designed to counter it, and research that treats gaze as evidence of supervision. Each studies something real about streamed or automated output. Each, as the subsections below show, leaves out the same variable.

### 3.1 Streaming and Pacing in Generative Interfaces

Streaming changes where people look. Park and Lee (2024) recorded the eye movements of 36 participants reading Korean expository text in four chatbot display types, drawn from the interfaces of five commercial chatbots, with half the sample seeing text generated letter by letter and half seeing it generated in sub-sentence chunks. When text was generated at the bottom of the screen, participants made more fixation starts, spent more total time fixating and made larger saccades than when the same kind of text appeared whole at the top, and their fixation starts clustered in the lower areas of the screen where new text emerged (Tables 9, 11–12). It remains the only eye-tracking study of chatbot text movement I found. Its limits are stated by its design: topic was confounded with display type, and the study measured neither comprehension nor regressions nor any decision.

Streaming also changes what people believe about the source. In a within-subjects experiment with 103 participants, Bhat (2025) compared responses from GPT-3.5 Turbo displayed with a simulated typing delay of two to three seconds per sentence against responses from the same model displayed at once. The typed display raised perceived competence (F = 40.89), trustworthiness (F = 35.21) and persuasiveness (F = 76.89), all p < .0001, while perceived effectiveness did not change (F = 0.16, p = .69). Every measure was a rating, and simulated typing is not token streaming. Anderl et al. (2024) went a step further and measured whether people could tell accurate information from partially inaccurate information. Across two preregistered experiments with 1,222 participants, identical content was presented as a screencast of a chatbot typing out its answer, as a voice assistant, or as a static encyclopedia entry. Both conversational presentations raised perceived credibility above the static text. Discernment between accurate and inaccurate content was lower for the typed-out chatbot than for static text in the second experiment (p = .029) but not in the first (p = .212), and the authors attribute the effect to the "conversational nature" of the presentation rather than to typing as such. The evidence that dynamic delivery alters credibility is therefore consistent; the evidence that it impairs error detection is suggestive, confounded with conversational framing, and not yet replicated.

Timing is a design variable, not a given. Gnewuch et al. (2022) found that a delayed chatbot response had opposing effects on social presence and usage intentions, moderated by users' prior experience with chatbots (N = 202). Tan et al. (2026) varied the time to the first token of a language model's response at 2, 9 and 20 seconds. Users' interaction behaviour was robust to the delay, but participants who waited two seconds rated the outputs as less thoughtful and useful than those who waited longer, and they read the delay as "AI deliberation." The authors conclude that latency is "not simply a cost to reduce but a tunable design variable with ethical implications."

The pace of delivery, meanwhile, is set by compute. Liu et al. (2024) estimated an average reading speed of 4.8 tokens per second by converting an age-based words-per-minute table at about 1.3 tokens per word, adopted that figure as a delivery target, and reported that one serving system delivered 11.2 tokens per second on a real trace. Xiao and Yang (2025) measured comfortable streaming speed directly with an adaptive staircase procedure: the 99th-percentile comfortable speed was 21.20 words per second for an easy passage and 11.97 for a complex one, against roughly 45 words per second from the GPT-4o API. Comfort is not comprehension, and neither study measured comprehension. Both studies, and subsequent work on adaptive token pacing (Zhu et al., 2026), treat the problem as one of rate.

Research on moving text predates generative AI and gives a mixed picture. Kang and Muter (1989) found that smoothly scrolling "Times Square" text, moving right to left in a single line, supported comprehension at least as high as rapid serial visual presentation at 100 to 300 words per minute. Moving text, in other words, need not destroy literal comprehension. The costs appear at higher levels. Harvey et al. (2017) found that readers of horizontally scrolling text made fewer and longer fixations and shorter regressions, and that word-level processing was preserved while the benefit of sentence-level predictability was lost. Harvey and Walker (2018) found that literal comprehension of scrolling text was largely maintained while inferential comprehension suffered, and noted that "returning to previously encountered text is also more difficult with this format." In speech-to-text interpreting, where text emerges letter by letter as it is transcribed, 90.8% of regression landings fell on content words, against a base rate of 57.1%, and the authors traced a typical cause of regressions to incoherence arising from omissions (Sharmin et al., 2016). When readers control the scrolling themselves, the pattern shifts rather than disappears: Haverkamp et al. (2023) found that 145 students who scrolled through a text backtracked less often than those who paged through it (2.49 vs. 3.53 instances) but over greater distances, with only a trend toward better integrated understanding for paging (p = .066). None of these displays was an AI stream, but each removes part of the stability that reading uses, and the cost falls on integration and look-back rather than on recognizing words.

One system has begun to structure a stream from within. WaitGPT (Xie et al., 2024) parses the code a language model generates during data analysis into discrete operations as the code streams in, and draws each operation as a node in a growing diagram that users can inspect, interrupt "at any point," and refine individually. Participants found raw code streams mentally taxing; one reported, "I felt stressed viewing the code stream, but it's a pleasure to watch the diagram grow." The authors call for streaming methods that let users "control their understanding speed rather than passively following a predefined playing timeline." WaitGPT re-represents a code stream rather than marking points of judgment in prose, adds no pauses, measures no gaze, and does not test where structure should be placed. It nonetheless establishes that a stream can be segmented as it is generated, and that users welcome the segmentation.

What this literature omits is placement. Pacing research adjusts how fast tokens arrive, and timing research adjusts when they begin. Neither asks where, inside a forming response, a reader could stop, and the one system that segments a stream does so for code, by operation, not for natural-language claims by the judgments they call for.

### 3.2 Automation Supervision and Trust

Human-factors research on automation explains failures of supervision as failures of attention. Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) located automation complacency in the allocation of attention under multiple-task load, when manual tasks compete with the automated task for the operator's attention (p. 381), and they used "looking-but-not-seeing" (p. 403) to explain commission errors, in which operators follow incorrect automated advice. Their evidence came from information sampling rather than fixations: in one study they review, about half of the participants who missed a wrong automated diagnosis had sampled all the information needed to catch it (p. 402). Goddard et al.'s (2012) systematic review of 74 studies of automation bias identified workload, task complexity and time constraint as environmental mediators, and listed among design mitigators "the position of advice on the screen" — an early indication that where automated output appears, and not only what it says, affects whether it is scrutinized. Lee and See (2004) framed the design goal as appropriate reliance, in which trust tracks what the automation can actually do, and their notion of an automation's "boundary conditions" has since been taken up in studies of expert adoption (Lamb et al., 2026).

Advice that arrives as a recommendation changes scrutiny, if slightly. Gegoff et al. (2025) gave 142 participants an uninhabited-vehicle management task with and without decision recommendations. Recommendations raised selection accuracy (.89 vs. .85, ηp² = .16) but lowered participants' ability to discriminate accurate from inaccurate system information, measured in the 96 participants who received additional information (.93 vs. .94, ηp² = .05). A streamed answer arrives in recommendation form: complete, confident and ready to accept.

Giving operators control over transparency does not solve the problem. Tatasciore et al. (2026) allowed operators to select their own level of automation transparency across two experiments (N = 73 and 88). Operators chose high transparency on 39% and 43% of adaptable trials, and adaptable transparency did not improve the accuracy of automation use (p. 22). Aggregating across the authors' six studies of the same task, fixed high transparency raised hit rates and sensitivity but also increased a bias toward agreeing with automated advice, with no gain in correct rejections (p. 21).

Supervision of language-model agents fails on timing. Chen et al. (2026) observed 48 participants overseeing 192 live sessions of a computer-use agent, each containing one embedded problematic action. Users blocked 29 of the 138 problematic actions that reached execution, and 61 of 109 failures (56%) involved a timing mismatch in which risk emerged before, or without, a point at which the user could decide. Oversight, the authors conclude, "depends less on maximizing control than on aligning authority, timing, and attention with decision-critical moments," and "should therefore trigger judgment before a consequential transition when possible" (§6.2). These are the nearest statements in the literature to the claim that decision points must be made perceptible.

Expertise changes the relationship over time. In a longitudinal study of defence intelligence analysts adopting an object-detection overlay on live video, Lamb et al. (2026) found that seven of nine analysts stopped treating the tool as an "advisor" (McNemar p = .023) and that analysts sought to learn the tool's boundary conditions — when and why it would fail. Endsley (2017) frames the broader difficulty as an automation conundrum: as autonomy and reliability increase, operators' situation awareness falls, and they become less likely to be able to take over manual control when it is needed.

What this literature omits is continuity. It models automated advice as discrete events — an alert, a diagnosis, a recommendation presented on a screen. A streamed response is not an event but a performance that unfolds while the reader reads it, and the moments at which judgment is required arrive inside that performance rather than at its end.

### 3.3 LLM Overreliance, Fluency, and Cognitive Friction

Fluent explanation raises reliance on language models whether or not the answer is correct. Kim et al. (2025) combined a think-aloud study (N = 16) with a preregistered experiment (N = 308) on difficult binary factual questions. Explanations raised agreement with both correct and incorrect answers, while sources and visible inconsistencies lowered agreement with incorrect answers; when the model's answer was wrong, accuracy was 17.2% with an explanation and 21.8% without. Few participants checked: 189 of 308 never clicked a source. The authors observe that "the properties that make such explanations intelligible and compelling may be precisely those that lead to overreliance" (§5.1). Steyvers et al. (2025) found that users overestimated the accuracy of model responses accompanied by default explanations, and that "longer explanations increased user confidence, even when the extra length did not improve answer accuracy."

Fluency can be trained in without verifiability. Zhu et al. (2026) found that post-training raised model accuracy without raising the verifiability of errors, and that rephrasing a justification without adding information did not help raters distinguish correct from incorrect answers. For one model on one benchmark, a false-positive verifiability score fell from 0.717 to 0.602 after preference optimization, which the authors interpret, with explicit hedging, as preference optimization making justifications for wrong answers look more convincing (§7.1); most of their raters were language models, and human raters' accuracy fell slightly when justifications were shown. At the scale of model families, Zhou et al. (2024) report that larger and more instructable models give "apparently sensible yet wrong" answers more often, including on difficult questions where "human supervisors frequently overlook" the errors. A survey of 319 knowledge workers points the same way from the user's side: higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking, and generative AI shifted critical thinking toward "information verification" (Lee et al., 2025).

Overreliance leaves traces in behaviour. Liu et al. (2026) logged the mouse and keyboard activity of 77 participants working with a GPT-4 platform into which misinformation had been injected. High overreliance went with copying whole texts, coarse navigation of prominent areas, and acceptance after visible hesitation (Table 3). Whether people engage at all may be a strategic choice. Vasconcelos et al. (2023) argue, across five studies (N = 731), that people weigh the costs and benefits of engaging with an AI explanation against those of simply relying on the AI, and that explanations reduce overreliance when engagement is made cheap enough or worthwhile enough. Fok and Weld (2024) add that explanations help only "to the extent that they allow a human decision maker to verify the correctness of the AI's prediction," which most tasks do not readily permit.

Interventions that slow or redirect the user reduce overreliance, at a cost. Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos (2021) tested three cognitive forcing functions with 199 participants, one of which imposed a 30-second wait before the AI's prediction appeared. On incorrect predictions, forcing functions reduced overreliance relative to simple explainable-AI designs (0.48 vs. 0.64, d = .36), but "people assigned the least favorable subjective ratings to the designs that reduced the overreliance the most," and the benefits accrued mainly to people higher in need for cognition. Ghosh et al. (2026) compared forcing functions for AI-generated writing plans (n = 214) and found that one prompting users to examine the plan's assumptions reduced overreliance most, without raising cognitive load. Two interventions place the intervention inside the model's output itself. Kim et al. (2024) found, in a preregistered experiment with 404 participants, that first-person expressions of uncertainty ("I'm not sure, but…") lowered agreement with a model's answers and raised participants' accuracy. Danry et al. (2023) found, with 204 participants, that framing an AI's explanation as a question improved people's discernment of logically flawed statements over causal explanations. Both, however, alter the *content* of what the system says.

Friction designs otherwise place their obstacles around the stream. Cox et al. (2016) defined a microboundary as "a small obstacle prior to an interaction," imposed through a short time cost, that shifts behaviour from automatic to deliberate, and they listed user control over such boundaries as an open question. Collins et al. (2024) tested selective frictions for language-model use, such as a button that impedes access to the model, which reduced click-through with little effect on accuracy but changed behaviour even on topics where no friction was applied. Arun and Fischer (2026) found that task-specific preparatory friction before AI access raised perceived mental demand while also increasing trust in the AI and its perceived usefulness, and did not reduce self-reported reliance or increase critical evaluation (N = 40). Disley and Khan (2026) built friction into the model itself by suppressing a concept during ideation (N = 37); the unmodified model lowered effort and perceived ownership (ownership 6.38, against 9.16 with suppression and 11.49 unaided). They contrast this model-level friction with interface-level friction — "an added step, a delay" — and call for comparisons between the two.

Asking users to reflect has its own price. Forshaw, Hale and Longmire (2026) gave eight managers coaching from a human and from an AI agent built on the same protocol. All eight "welcomed the probing questions" from the human coach, yet they "resisted the same protocol of questioning from the AI coach and wanted answers from it instead"; the coached behaviours nonetheless appeared after both sessions (§6.1). What users say about friction and what friction does can diverge.

What this literature omits is placement by meaning in the presentation of output. Friction is placed before an interaction, before access to the model, or inside the model's training or generation; the in-output interventions that do exist change what the model says. None marks, in the presentation of an unchanged response, the points at which that response calls for judgment.

### 3.4 Gaze as an Insufficient Proxy for Supervision

Gaze tracks reliance. In a time-critical drone-monitoring task with AI-suggested alarms, Wu et al. (2025) found that gaze patterns related to verification distinguished appropriate reliance from over- and underreliance, and they validated a model that detected over- and underreliance at run time. Cao and Huang (2022) found a strong positive correlation between the share of gaze directed at an AI's suggestion and users' agreement with it.

Attention, however, can move without judgment moving. Cho and Lim (2026) compared four source-attribution layouts for chatbot answers (N = 23). Time to first fixation on the sources ranged from 0.96 s to 15.65 s across layouts, yet no self-reported measure, including trust, differed, and gaze measures did not predict trust: "Attention moved without moving judgment" (§3.4). Their screens were static, and the sources could not be clicked.

The meaning of fixation duration is ambiguous. In a virtual-reality pedestrian simulator, Yang et al. (2027) found that augmented-reality cues shortened fixation durations on the approaching automated vehicle and the cue itself (d = −0.80 for two of the three placements, in trial-level tests) and brought crossing decisions earlier; the authors caution that "these patterns may also indicate overreliance." Resulbegoviq et al.'s (2026) systematic review of 23 eye-tracking studies of AI-generated content found that "no modality-independent gaze pattern emerged," and that longer viewing more often reflected difficulty, uncertainty or checking than preference.

What this literature omits is a normative account: where gaze should land during a stream, and under what conditions a fixation counts as checking rather than looking.

### 3.5 Summary: The Missing Variable

The four literatures share a blind spot. Pacing research studies the rate of the stream, automation research the advice it carries, overreliance and friction research the content of the output and the obstacles placed around it, and gaze research where the eyes go. None examines where, inside a response that is still forming, judgment is required, and whether the reader can perceive that it is. The nearest neighbours confirm the gap by their limits: WaitGPT segments a code stream by operation rather than marking judgments in prose (Xie et al., 2024); Chen et al. (2026) show that oversight fails when decision-critical moments pass unseen but do not propose how to make them visible inside the output; and Glassman, Gu and Kummerfeld (2024) argue that users can recover from an AI's choices only if they "notice them first," including choices "hiding in a wall of text," without specifying a perceptual mechanism for noticing. I searched arXiv, Consensus, OpenAlex and the web between 20 and 21 October 2026 and found no work that marks decision points inside a streamed natural-language response or tests where such marks should be placed. Algorithmacy theory, at its perceptual layer, supplies that variable.
