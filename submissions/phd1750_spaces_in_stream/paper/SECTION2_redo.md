## 2. FOUNDATIONAL CONCEPTS: SCRIPTIO CONTINUA, READING, ATTENTION AND EVENTS

Reading is a cultural invention running on older equipment. Writing is too recent to have shaped the evolution of the human brain, yet in literate people reading reliably occupies the same region of cortex. Dehaene and Cohen (2007) explain this regularity with a neuronal recycling hypothesis. Cultural inventions "invade evolutionarily older brain circuits and inherit many of their structural constraints" (p. 384). Each must find a "neuronal niche," circuits close enough to the new function and plastic enough to be reoriented to it, and the prior organization of those circuits "is never entirely erased" (p. 385). For reading, the precursor is visual: the region that comes to respond to written words "relates to object and scene recognition" (p. 393). Learning to read reorganizes that pathway, strengthening responses to written characters in the left hemisphere while responses to faces shift to the right (Dehaene, Cohen, Morais, & Kolinsky, 2015). [S1] Because reading borrows the visual system rather than owning one, it inherits that system's dependence on how the things it looks at are laid out. A reader takes nothing from a page without moving the eyes: finding a target, landing on it, going back to an earlier one. Each of these acts depends on the arrangement of the marks. A streaming interface arranges them differently from a page. [S2]

### 2.1 The historical precedent: scriptio continua, word separation and cognitive offloading

Greek literary books of the early Roman empire were written without spaces between words. Johnson (2000, p. 609) describes the typical prose bookroll:
- narrow columns of fifteen to twenty-five letters;
- letters clearly, often calligraphically, written, but otherwise undifferentiated;
- a horizontal stroke in the margin to mark the end of a sentence;
- "otherwise little or no punctuation," and no paragraph breaks or running headers.

The impression of "uninterrupted succession, of a coherent whole, seems paramount" (p. 609). Latin had a more varied history. "In early Roman literary texts, word separation is the norm," Johnson reports, and in the early empire "the Romans chose to discard word spaces in the writing of their literary manuscripts" (pp. 608–609). [S3] Rayner, Fischer and Pollatsek (1998, p. 1129) add that English, too, "was not always written with spaces."

Why did readers tolerate such script? For most of the twentieth century the standard answer was that they could not read it silently. Josef Balogh argued in 1927 that ancient readers read aloud almost always, and he tied the habit to *scriptio continua*. Faced with an undifferentiated run of letters, a reader had to sound it out to find the words (as reported in Johnson, 2000, p. 595). Saenger (1997) built the strongest modern version of this account. His thesis is that "the separation of words, which began in the early Middle Ages, altered the physiological process of reading and by the fourteenth century enabled the common practice of silent reading as we know it today" (p. ix). On Saenger's account the separation began in Insular manuscripts — his fourth chapter is titled "Insular Culture and Word Separation in the Seventh and Eighth Centuries" (p. 83) — and spread to the Continent over the following centuries. [NEEDS: Saenger chapters 1 and 4 for the argument itself and for why Irish and Anglo-Saxon scribes separated words.] [S4]

Classicists dispute the strong form of this story. Knox (1968) showed that ancient witnesses treat the silent reading of letters and documents "as an ordinary event" (Johnson, 2000, p. 593). [NEEDS: Knox's article itself; it was not in the batch.] Johnson (2000) concludes that "there was no cognitive difficulty when fully literate ancient readers wished to read silently to themselves" (p. 593). He also argues that reading literary texts aloud persisted for social reasons, not perceptual ones. Literary reading was usually a group performance by a trained reader, a practice of the cultured elite (pp. 615–619), and the bookroll's design served it. The roll "was 'played' by the reader much in the way that we play a videotape or witness a stage performance" (p. 620). [S5] Johnson also warns against treating writing as a technology with fixed cognitive consequences for a society, a view he finds "too simplistic, even reductionist" (pp. 604–605).

The debate does not settle the question this paper needs answered, but both sides agree on one point, and the theory rests on it. A reading system must give the eye somewhere to land and something to orient by, and it can do so in more than one way. Saenger's ancient reader, as Johnson summarizes him, had no "natural" point at which to fixate, and the result was "ocular regressions" in search of a word's beginning (Johnson, 2000, p. 611). Johnson replies that the bookroll supplied other points. Lines broke "at a rational point, either at word beginning or at the start of a syllable." A column was about as wide as the span the eye can take in around a fixation and keeps ahead of the voice. The line beginnings "provided natural points for the ocular fixation," with the sentence-end stroke in the margin keeping the reader oriented (p. 611). The disagreement is over *which* structure ancient readers used, not over whether they needed one.

Modern psychophysics measures what the word space is worth to a reader trained on it. Rayner, Fischer and Pollatsek (1998) removed the spaces from English text, or filled them with other characters, and recorded skilled adult readers' eye movements. Reading rate fell by about half: by 54% for single sentences and 44% for passages in the first experiment, and by 58% in the second (pp. 1132, 1138). Regressions in sentence reading rose from 9.7% of eye movements to 17.3%, and in passages from 11.2% to 16.4% (Table 1). Readers stopped landing just left of a word's centre and landed nearer its beginning. Rayner and colleagues frame the space as doing two jobs (p. 1129):
- It aids word identification, "by (among other things) making clear where a given word begins and ends."
- It guides the eyes: spaces around the words to the right of the fixated word "enhance them as 'targets' for an eye movement."

Their data showed both effects, and word identification suffered more (p. 1143). [S6]

Readers who lose the space look for another boundary. Drieghe, Fitzsimmons and Liversedge (2017) found little evidence that readers of unspaced English compensate by spreading their processing across several words. Instead, "the system will use any information (e.g., an orthographically illegal string of letters) available to help determine word boundaries," so that it can keep its focus narrow (p. 1713). They point to writing systems without spaces. Japanese readers of mixed kanji and hiragana use the visually distinct kanji as segmentation cues and aim their saccades near the centres of words, a pattern absent when the same readers read pure hiragana (p. 1713). The space is one way of supplying such boundaries, and readers trained on it rely on it. Readers without it find another cue if the script offers one.

Zhang and Norman (1994) give the principle that joins the history to the psychophysics. They call it the representational effect: "different isomorphic representations of a common formal structure can cause dramatically different cognitive behaviors" (p. 88), as Arabic numerals make multiplication easier than Roman numerals do. External representations, they found, can supply information that is "directly perceived and used without being interpreted and formulated explicitly," can "anchor and structure cognitive behavior," and can "change the nature of a task" (pp. 118–119). A word space is such a representation. It adds no information to the text, and every letter stays the same. What it changes is where the work of finding word boundaries is done: on the page, by the eye, rather than in the reader's head. Risko and Gilbert (2016) call the general move cognitive offloading, "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand" (p. 676). The word space is an unusual case of it, because the scribe performs the action once and every later reader receives the reduction. [S7]

[ROGER] A superficial objection immediately presents itself: conversational AI front-ends already print spaces between words. A streaming large language model does not output unspaced orthography; it delivers standard, punctuated text formatted in conventional typeface. Why, then, should ancient scriptio continua serve as an operative framework for generative AI? [/ROGER]

The objection is right about the word and wrong about the stream. A streamed response keeps word-level typography intact, but while it forms it lacks the structures above the word that let a reader orient. A finished page has paragraph breaks, headings and white space, and it stays still, so that a reader can return to a place and find what was there. Even the ancient bookroll, on Johnson's account, offered line beginnings to land on and marginal strokes to mark where sentences ended. A response that is still streaming offers neither. It extends continuously and scrolls itself, and nothing in it marks where a checkable claim ends, where the response commits to one course of action over another, or where it changes goal. [S8] The words are separated; the decisions are not. Johnson's comparison also fits more closely than he could have meant it to. The streamed response, like the bookroll, is "played" rather than consulted. The difference is that the bookroll's reader controlled the playing, and the stream's reader does not.

The parallel has limits, and stating them strengthens it. The pace of a stream is set by the provider's compute rather than by the reader (Liu et al., 2024; Xiao & Yang, 2025). Nor does the claim depend on Saenger's strong thesis. If, with Knox and Johnson, ancient readers could read silently whenever they wished, the lesson still holds. The space did not create the capacity to read, nor did any other device. What these devices did was move the work of segmentation and orientation from memory to the eye, and Rayner et al.'s readers show what that is worth to a reader trained on it.

The claim this paper makes is of the same kind. Marking a decision space will not make an uncritical user a competent supervisor. It moves the work of finding the point of judgment from the user's memory and vigilance onto the display. [ROGER] Just as the scribal word space did not automatically teach an illiterate person how to read, marking a decision space makes an operational boundary perceptible without dictating how the human supervisor should act. [/ROGER] The space between words supplied a perceptual condition for literate reading without supplying literacy. Marking decision spaces would supply a perceptual condition for algorithmacy in the same way, and no more than that.

### 2.2 Oculomotor mechanics: saccade targets, regressions and repair

Reading alternates fixations, brief pauses in which the eyes take in text, with saccades, rapid jumps between them. When adults read English, "eye fixations last about 200–250 ms and the mean saccade size is 7–9 letter spaces" (Rayner, 1998, p. 375). Rayner et al.'s (1998) spaced conditions fall in that range: fixations of 250 and 253 ms and forward saccades of 7.3 and 7.9 characters (Table 1). [S9] No useful information is taken in during a saccade, so everything a reader learns arrives during fixations (Schotter, Angele, & Rayner, 2012, p. 5). Some of it arrives early. While fixating one word, readers begin processing the next. In the gaze-contingent boundary paradigm, a word read faster after a valid preview than after an invalid one shows a "preview benefit" (Schotter et al., 2012, p. 9).

Readers also go back. "About 10–15% of the saccades are regressions," and longer regressions "occur because the reader did not understand the text" (Rayner, 1998, p. 375). Booth and Weger (2013, citing Rayner & Pollatsek, 1989) give a wider estimate of 15–25%. Regressions are aimed, not random. Readers "often have fairly good spatial memories for where they went wrong in comprehension and make fairly accurate saccades to that region of text" (Rayner, 1998, p. 387). A regression works only if the text is still where the reader left it.

Regressions exist to reread. Booth and Weger (2013) tested whether readers look back to take in earlier text again, or to the place where a word was as a cue to memory, whatever is now there. Across three experiments the evidence favoured rereading. When earlier text was made illegible, readers regressed less, and their regressions stopped predicting comprehension. Booth and Weger foresaw the problem for screens: for future readers "of scrollable text on small screens," "rereading is often impossible" (General Discussion).

Taking the way back away costs comprehension. Schotter, Tran and Rayner (2014) used a trailing mask, which turned each word into a row of x's once the eyes had passed it, so that a regression found nothing to reread. Forty readers read single-line sentences, some with temporary syntactic ambiguities and some without. The mask lowered comprehension (b = −0.92, z = 5.09, p < .001), for unambiguous sentences as well as ambiguous ones (p. 1221). Comprehension was 78% in normal reading on trials without a regression and 71% under the mask (p. 1223).

Rereading carries work that forward reading does not. Timkey et al. (2026) recorded the eye movements of 368 readers on syntactically challenging sentences and compared them with 409 kinds of language-model surprisal estimate. Surprisal captured the early effects of disambiguation. It did not explain an additional cost, "reflected in an increase in rereading," which the authors attribute to "detecting or correcting errors in the structure-building process" (abstract). Metzner, von der Malsburg, Vasishth and Rösler (2017) reached a similar result with brain potentials. Readers who met a syntactic or semantic violation either regressed, which went with a P600 response and good comprehension, or pursued a "good-enough" interpretation and comprehended worse. Free reading beat word-by-word presentation only on trials in which the eyes went back (abstract).

Displays that pace the reader remove the option. Rapid serial visual presentation shows words one at a time in a fixed place. Benedetto et al. (2015) found that a commercial version of it, Spritz, "suppresses parafoveal processing and regressions," which "negatively affected literal comprehension" (p. 352). Metzner et al.'s word-by-word condition is the same kind of display. In each case comprehension depended on a look back that the display allowed or prevented. A stream that pushes earlier text up and away, while new text appears at the bottom, approaches the same condition. [S10]

### 2.3 Visual search, abrupt onsets and priority maps

Something new in the visual field draws the eye, under conditions. Yantis and Jonides (1984) showed that in visual search an item appearing abruptly is identified first. They also bounded the effect: capture of this kind occurs "only when the visual field contains but one such event" (p. 617). O'Regan and Noë (2001) build the same sensitivity into their account of seeing. When a visual transient occurs, "an automatic, 'alerting' or 'attention-grabbing' mechanism appears to direct processing to the location where the transient occurred" (p. 947).

Wolfe's (2021) Guided Search 6.0 places such effects inside a model of where attention goes next. Five sources of guidance feed a single priority map: top-down and bottom-up feature guidance, prior history, reward, and scene syntax and semantics (p. 1060). Attention visits the most active location on the map "approximately 20 times per second." The sources differ in how long they last. Bottom-up salience "may be a very powerful but short-lived form of guidance." Wolfe adopts the view that "attention capture" may be a misnomer: a salient onset creates "bumps in the priority map" that often win the next deployment of attention without seizing it (p. 1069). [S11]

Priority also changes over time, and the evidence for that comes from visual marking. Watson and Humphreys (1997) presented half of a search display early and the rest, including the target, later. Search then became as efficient as if only the later items had been shown. Observers had deprioritized the old items by "top-down attentional inhibition" of their locations (p. 90). The effect has four limits:
- it needed a gap of about 400 ms between old and new items;
- it was abolished when onsets or offsets occurred at the old locations as the new items appeared;
- it was reduced when attention was drawn to a secondary task;
- it was, in the authors' words, "resource limited" and intentional (pp. 90, 116).

Kunar, Shapiro and Humphreys (2006) add that when old items disappear and then reappear together with the new ones, the preview advantage is abolished (p. 679). Wolfe draws the general lesson. Marking "shows that priority can evolve over time," but "if there is enough of a break in the action, the map may get reset," and he suggests testing whether event boundaries do the resetting (p. 1069). [S12]

Read together, these studies describe a reader facing a stream. Each new token is an onset, and while it appears alone against static text it meets Yantis and Jonides's condition. Its salience is brief, but the stream renews it many times a second. Visual marking shows that the visual system can favour what is new over what it has already seen, at a cost in attention. A searcher looking for a new target gains from this. A supervisor, whose task is to go back to what has already appeared and check it, does not. And a continuous stream never supplies the break in which, on Wolfe's proposal, the map might reset. [S13]

### 2.4 Event segmentation and situation-model updating

People perceive activity as a sequence of discrete events. Event Segmentation Theory explains how: "Perceptual systems continuously make predictions about what will happen next. When transient errors in predictions arise, an event boundary is perceived" (Zacks, Speer, Swallow, Braver, & Reynolds, 2007, p. 273). The predictions come from event models, working-memory representations of "what is happening now" that stay stable despite momentary changes in the input (p. 274). When prediction error rises, a gate opens. The model is reset, and sensory input briefly gains more influence over it. The gate then "quickly swings shut again," so the system "alternates between long periods of stability and brief periods of change" (p. 275). Zacks et al. draw two consequences:
- Segmentation regulates attention: "event segmentation does not itself require attention; rather, it implements a mechanism of attention."
- It controls the updating of working memory, which makes it "a core, domain-general mechanism of cognitive control" (p. 276).

Boundaries change what people can do. In his review, Zacks (2020) reports two things:
- "Predictive eye movements are also less prevalent near event boundaries" (p. 171).
- When people "monitor a complex situation for a visual target, the presence of an event boundary transiently disrupts performance," and in film viewing, mind-wandering was less likely at boundaries than in the middle of events (p. 172).

Updating, he concludes, is "resource intensive": at boundaries, resources "are less available for secondary tasks" (p. 172). [S14]

Readers segment text the same way. Text-comprehension researchers identify at least five dimensions of a described situation: time, space, causation, intentionality and protagonist (Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998, pp. 167–168). Zacks, Speer and Reynolds (2009) found that changes in characters, goals, causes, objects or locations predicted where readers placed event boundaries. Reading slowed at those changes: each additional situation change added 92.8 ms of reading time, and each 10% rise in the probability of a boundary added about 108 ms. Segmentation also survived pacing that readers did not control. In a visual-sequential condition, software presented one clause at a time at about 3.35 words per second, with no way back, and readers still segmented the text hierarchically, their coarse and fine boundaries aligning above chance (t(26) = 2.97). Of the conditions studied, this one comes closest to a stream. At boundaries readers rebuilt their situation models, and between boundaries they extended them incrementally. Even at fine-grained boundaries with no change of goal, they mentioned goals more often (OR = 2.29; Kurby & Zacks, 2012).

Boundaries can also be computed from the text itself. Kumar et al. (2023) used the GPT-2 language model to compute, word by word, the predicted distribution of the next word in three stories, and asked listeners to mark event boundaries. The boundaries went with transient rises in Bayesian surprise, a measure of how much the model's predictions changed. They did not go with surprisal, the simpler measure of how unexpected each word was (abstract). [S15] The finding supports prediction error as the signal that controls segmentation. For this paper it also matters practically: where a text's event boundaries fall can be estimated before anyone reads it.

### 2.5 Ecological and sensorimotor perception: text as a stable object

Gibson (1979, ch. 8) defined affordances as what the environment "offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes, either for good or ill." He proposed that "the composition and layout of surfaces constitute what they afford," so that to perceive the layout is to perceive what it affords. He also noted that "the human animal can alter the affordances of the environment." [NEEDS: page numbers; the excerpt you sent is unpaginated.] A scribe who separates words alters the affordances of a page. Zhang and Norman (1994, p. 119) make the link explicit: external representations "seem to provide affordances."

O'Regan and Noë (2001) carry the ecological view into an account of seeing itself. "Seeing is a way of acting. It is a particular way of exploring the environment," and "the outside world serves as its own, external, representation" (p. 939). The world "acts as an external memory that can be probed at will by the sensory apparatus" (p. 946). Two consequences matter for reading. The first is that the sense of seeing a whole scene rests on its availability: any part can be brought into view by "the slightest flick of the eye" (p. 946), which works only if the scene stays put. The second is that looking is not seeing. "Proofreading is notoriously difficult," O'Regan and Noë observe, because a reader looking at words processes words and can miss a wrong letter while looking straight at it (p. 955). Reading is action on a stable object, and even then the eyes can rest on an error without registering it.

Printed text supplies that stability, and readers appear to use it. Readers aim regressions using spatial memory for where they went wrong (Rayner, 1998, p. 387). Mangen, Olivier and Velay (2019) gave 50 readers a 28-page story in print or on a Kindle. Print readers were better at reconstructing the order of the plot's events (t(48) = 2.03, η² = .08), and the authors tied the advantage to the "material placeholders" a physical book offers. Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman and Salmerón's (2018) meta-analysis covered 54 comparisons and found a paper advantage in comprehension (Hedges' g = −0.21). The advantage was larger when reading time was limited than when it was self-paced (p. 23). The advantage was also significant when digital texts required scrolling (g = −0.25) and only marginal when they did not (g = −0.13). Scrolling did not reach significance as a moderator, however (p. 33). Delgado et al. suggest that scrolling "may add a cognitive load to the reading task by making spatial orientation to the text more difficult" (p. 35). [S16]

No study in this literature tests a streaming display. Each condition studied, though, sits on a scale of stability. A page and a fixed screen stay put. A scrolling screen keeps text in order but moves it at the reader's command. A streaming response moves its earlier text while still adding new text, on a schedule the reader does not set. On the sensorimotor account, each step down that scale removes part of what makes text an outside memory, and a stream sits at the bottom. That ordering is this paper's inference, and Section 5 proposes how to test it.
