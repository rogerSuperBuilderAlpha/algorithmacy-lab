### 2.4. Event Segmentation and Situation Model Updating

While visual search priority maps govern spatial orientation, higher-order cognitive control over dynamic information depends on temporally parsing continuous experience into discrete units. Event Segmentation Theory (EST) posits that human perceptual systems structure sensory input by continuously generating forward predictions about imminent events (Zacks et al., 2007). These predictions are based on internal event models, which are working-memory representations of "what is happening now" that remain dynamically stable despite transient fluctuations in external stimuli (Zacks, 2020; Zacks et al., 2007). When environmental contingencies change and prediction error increases, an attentional gate opens: the prevailing model resets or updates, external sensory input briefly exerts greater influence, and perceivers register a discrete event boundary (Zacks, 2020; Zacks et al., 2007). In textual discourse, this segmentation process operates across multidimensional situation models that track changes in protagonists, spatial locations, temporal intervals, causal chains, and intentional goals (Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998). Zacks, Speer, and Reynolds (2009) ⟨1⟩found that shifts across these narrative dimensions predict where readers place event boundaries, and each situational change adds measurable processing time as working memory reconstructs its model of the discourse. Rather than representing passive comprehension costs, event boundaries function as a gating mechanism that regulates attentional deployment and ⟨2⟩resets the event model (Zacks et al., 2007; Zacks, 2020). In static text, orthographic white space, paragraph breaks, and sectional headings externally anchor this cognitive reorganization by signaling semantic transitions ⟨3⟩.

The cognitive capacity to segment continuous discourse into discrete episodes does not depend on a static, self-paced page. Human event segmentation persists even under externally imposed temporal constraints. Zacks, Speer, and Reynolds (2009) ⟨4⟩presented narrative prose clause by clause at a fixed rate, without the possibility of look-back. Even when deprived of temporal control and regressive review—a laboratory condition that approximates the constraints of a token stream—readers continued to segment discourse hierarchically, with fine and coarse boundaries aligning above chance. ⟨5⟩A recent computational study shows that these transition points can be predicted from the text's statistical dynamics. Kumar et al. (2023) found that human event boundaries track transient surges in Bayesian surprise, ⟨6⟩the extent to which each new word changes the model's predictions, rather than surprisal, how unexpected the word itself is. [DRAFTED]Where a text's event boundaries fall can therefore be estimated before anyone reads it.[/DRAFTED] ⟨7⟩ However, updating an event model is a resource-intensive operation that temporarily disrupts ⟨8⟩the monitoring of a complex situation for a target (Zacks, 2020, p. 172). An uninterrupted generative stream therefore creates a supervisory trade-off. Although the human cognitive architecture attempts to segment discourse at state-altering transitions, the continuous influx of new tokens ⟨9⟩arrives while working memory is consolidating its model, forcing the operator to either forgo critical verification or lose sensory contact with new output.

### 2.5. Ecological and Sensorimotor Perception: Text as a Spatially Stable Object

Both oculomotor targeting and event cognition are grounded in a broader perceptual principle: reading functions as an embodied physical exploration of a stable material artifact. Within Gibson's (1979) ecological framework, perception involves directly picking up affordances, the functional possibilities for action an environmental layout provides to an active perceiver. As humans frequently alter the composition and arrangement of their physical environments, external graphic representations act as engineered affordances that anchor cognitive performance to spatial constraints (Gibson, 1979; Zhang & Norman, 1994). This ecological perspective aligns with O'Regan and Noë's (2001) sensorimotor contingency theory of vision, which challenges the notion that visual perception depends on constructing a detailed internal photographic replica of the world. Instead, vision is conceptualized as an exploratory action governed by lawful dependencies between bodily movement and sensory feedback, with the external environment serving as an external memory buffer accessible to the sensory apparatus as needed (⟨10⟩O'Regan & Noë, 2001, p. 946). The subjective experience of an accessible, coherent text does not require the reader to retain previous paragraphs in biological working memory; rather, it depends on the display's physical stability, which enables immediate re-sampling of any prior clause through a ballistic saccade. However, this sensorimotor proficiency assumes that the material surface remains stationary. Even on a static page, visual fixation does not guarantee cognitive registration; ⟨11⟩a reader can look straight at a misspelled word and miss the error (O'Regan & Noë, 2001, p. 955).

Empirical investigations of text processing across reading media ⟨12⟩suggest that readers use this spatial stability. Readers aim regressive saccades using a spatial memory of the layout, ⟨13⟩landing fairly accurately on the region where comprehension failed (Rayner, 1998, p. 387). Mangen, Olivier, and Velay (2019) found that readers of a printed story reconstructed its plot chronology better than readers of the same story on an e-reader, and attributed the advantage to the "material placeholders" ⟨14⟩of the physical book. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Delgado et al. (2018) identified a persistent comprehension advantage for print over digital displays, larger under time constraints and in scrolling texts, although scrolling as a moderator did not reach statistical significance. Delgado and colleagues ⟨15⟩suggested that scrolling may add a cognitive load by making spatial orientation to the text more difficult.

Although previous research has examined static pages, paged e-readers, and reader-controlled scrolling, [DRAFTED]I found no study that tests a streaming display.[/DRAFTED] ⟨16⟩ Instead of allowing the reader to control when, where, and how rapidly the visual field shifts, an active token stream moves preceding text upward and appends new tokens at a pace determined by server computation. In sensorimotor terms, each progression along this continuum—from static print, to paged screens, to reader-manipulated scrolling, and ultimately to autonomous token streaming—incrementally removes the environmental invariants that enable written text to function as an external memory store. By pushing users to the end of this destabilization, current conversational front-ends ⟨17⟩would dismantle the perceptual scaffolding needed for supervision and critical intervention.
