Spaces in the Stream: How People See AI

## Abstract

When users supervise text generated by large language models in real time, they read prose that is actively forming on the display. Natural reading depends on two environmental invariants: stable spatial coordinates on a static page and unconstrained regressive saccades to reread and resolve comprehension failures. Streaming conversational interfaces eliminate both, pacing token presentation at arbitrary server rates and scrolling text past the reader's perceptual field. This kinetic delivery continuously triggers stimulus-driven attentional capture, inducing automation complacency and suppressing the visual inspection of plausible errors. [F1] Drawing on the historical transition from continuous script (scriptio continua) to interword spacing, [EDITED] I propose marking decision spaces—the semantic and verifiable task boundaries where users must judge—with perceptual and temporal pauses that restore visual landmarks for human oversight. [/EDITED] [was: "I propose implementing decision spaces—perceptual and temporal pauses positioned at semantic and verifiable task boundaries—to restore visual landmarks for human oversight."] To evaluate this intervention, I present a three-condition eye-tracking protocol (N = 42) [F2] comparing continuous streams, arbitrary periodic pauses, and decision-unit pauses on fixation dwell, regressive saccades, and the detection of seeded factual and logical errors. Determining whether structural boundaries recover supervisory inspection without inflating cognitive workload provides an empirical foundation for algorithmacy: the capacity to perceive, evaluate, and steer automated systems in real time.

## 1. Introduction

[Context] When users watch a chatbot responding to a query/prompt, their eyes track the text as it appears. Words stream incrementally, expanding and scrolling, forcing the user to track the text as if following an automated cursor. In eye-tracking evaluations of streaming interfaces, this dynamic display fundamentally alters oculomotor routines: bottom-generated scrolling text increases total fixation counts, prolongs cumulative gaze duration, and expands saccade amplitudes, with initial fixations tightly clustered at the leading visual edge where new tokens emerge (Park & Lee, 2024). [F3] Under these conditions, the stream is paced by the server, not the reader. Controlled psycholinguistic measurements show that adult silent reading proceeds at an average rate of approximately 4.8 tokens per second (Liu et al., 2024). [F4] Commercial model APIs, however, routinely output text at rates exceeding 40 to 45 words per second, while front-end chat clients throttle generation to arbitrary intervals between 11 and 25 tokens per second (Liu et al., 2024; Xiao & Yang, 2025). [F5] When empirical comfort thresholds are measured, 99% of readers report comfortable comprehension only when delivery rates remain below 21.2 words per second for simple prose and below 12.0 words per second for complex technical reasoning (Xiao & Yang, 2025). [F6] The delivery cadence of current generative interfaces is an arbitrary artifact of server throughput and network packet streaming, fundamentally decoupled from the cognitive and oculomotor architecture of the person reading it.

[Complication] This kinetic presentation strips the reading environment of the visual stability required for cognitive control. As Rayner, Fischer, and Pollatsek (1998) established, visual reading relies heavily on low-spatial-frequency boundaries in parafoveal vision to guide saccadic targeting and word identification. [F7] Crucially, reading comprehension is not a strictly feedforward perceptual process; it requires regressions—backward eye movements launched to reread ambiguous clauses, integrate syntactic structures, and repair comprehension failures (Schotter et al., 2014). When word masking prevents readers from executing regressions, reading comprehension deteriorates significantly (Schotter et al., 2014). [F8] In streaming AI interfaces, auto-scrolling text continuously displaces preceding lines beyond the functional regression window, forcing users to choose between manually scrolling up to review earlier claims or trailing the moving cursor (Haverkamp et al., 2023). [F9] Simultaneously, every newly generated token acts as an abrupt visual onset and motion transient, repeatedly seizing bottom-up attentional priority across the visual field (Wolfe, 2021; Yantis & Jonides, 1984). [F10] The fovea is captured by the leading edge of generation, severing the sensorimotor regularities that link voluntary eye movement to stable sensory pickup (Gibson, 1979; O'Regan & Noë, 2001). [F11]

[Consequence] The cognitive consequence of this perpetual visual pursuit is the systematic suppression of supervisory scrutiny. As generative models emit syntactically polished, grammatical sentences in real time, they induce an illusion of validity: readers conflate superficial textual fluency with factual and logical accuracy (Bhat, 2025; Kim et al., 2025). [F12] In collaborative decision-making, providing generated explanations frequently increases user agreement with incorrect model recommendations, fostering uncalibrated automation trust (Kim et al., 2025). [F13] [DRAFTED] Gegoff et al. (2025) found the same trade outside language models: when automation supplied a decision recommendation, 142 participants chose faster and more accurately but discriminated accurate from inaccurate system information slightly less well (.93 vs .94). A streamed answer arrives as a recommendation. [/DRAFTED] Furthermore, streaming text displays are subjectively perceived as more trustworthy and authoritative than static presentations (Bhat, 2025). [F14] This dynamic reproduces what Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) identified as automation complacency under multi-task sampling demands: operators enter a state of "looking-but-not-seeing," fixating visually on an erroneous display without mobilizing the deeper cognitive processing required to detect anomalies. [F15] Because the streaming interface affords no natural stopping points, the entire cognitive burden of deciding when to pause, verify, or cross-check an assertion falls on working memory while the text rolls forward, driving users toward uncritical acceptance or cognitive exhaustion.

[Claim] I propose to examine this phenomenon as an issue of perception and action: specifically, how users visually process AI-generated output in real time and how this influences subsequent behaviors, such as accepting, disregarding, rereading, consulting sources, or intervening to redirect the AI. An informative historical parallel exists in the evolution of written language: Latin manuscripts were originally composed in scriptura continua, [F16] with letters joined together without word boundaries, forcing readers to read aloud or subvocalize to determine lexical units through phonological inference (Saenger, 1997). Beginning in the seventh century, Insular scribes introduced blank spaces between words, a structural innovation that offloaded lexical segmentation from internal working memory to pre-attentive visual processing, enabling silent, reflective reading (Parkes, 1992; Saenger, 1997). [F17] Analogous to the introduction of spaces in written language, [EDITED] I propose marking, with perceptual boundaries, the points within AI-generated streams where users need to make decisions: decision spaces. [/EDITED] [was: "I propose implementing perceptual boundaries within AI-generated streams at points where users need to make decisions, or decision spaces."] Grounded in Event Segmentation Theory (Zacks et al., 2007), [EDITED] marking these decision spaces with temporal pauses and low-contrast spatial landmarks at verifiable assertion boundaries serves three coordinated human-factors functions: [/EDITED] [was: "these decision spaces introduce temporal pauses and low-contrast spatial landmarks at verifiable assertion boundaries, serving three coordinated human-factors functions:"] (1) providing a stable visual target for saccadic targeting, (2) establishing cognitive event boundaries that trigger mental model updating, and (3) opening an unhurried perceptual window for regressive inspection. [DRAFTED] Wolfe (2021, p. 1069) treats bottom-up salience as powerful but short-lived and notes that "if there is enough of a break in the action, the map may get reset"; he asks whether event boundaries do the resetting. The interface places these boundaries; the user does not have to summon them. Tatasciore et al. (2026) let operators choose their own level of automation transparency and found, across two experiments, that the choice did not improve automation use; operators picked the higher level on about 41% of missions. Structure, by contrast, does change what people do with AI: across 244 participants, Najjar and Nasir (2026) found that an iterative-critique exchange produced more task-appropriate work than one in which the AI led with proposals. [/DRAFTED] [Najjar and Nasir: abstract only]

[Course] To evaluate whether structural boundaries restore supervisory vigilance, this paper specifies an empirical eye-tracking study (N = 42) [F2] comparing user supervision of streaming text containing seeded factual and inferential errors across three conditions: a continuous stream, a stream paused at arbitrary periodic intervals, and a stream paused at semantic decision units. [DRAFTED] Gaze alone cannot answer the question. Yang et al. (2026) found that augmented-reality cues shortened pedestrians' fixations on approaching automated vehicles and noted that the shorter fixations may also signal overreliance; the study therefore pairs every gaze measure with error detection. [/DRAFTED] The experiment investigates three formal research questions:

⚬ RQ1: Do breaks placed at decision units draw fixations and regressions to those points more than breaks placed at arbitrary intervals?
⚬ RQ2: Do decision-unit breaks improve detection of seeded errors relative to a continuous stream and to arbitrary breaks?
⚬ RQ3: Do arbitrary breaks impose a cost relative to a continuous stream in terms of detection latency, subjective workload, or local refixations?

[DRAFTED] RQ3 matters because friction has a price. Disley and Khan (2026) built friction into a language model and found, across 37 participants, that the unmodified model lowered mental demand and effort but also lowered perceived ownership. Forshaw et al. (2026) found that eight managers welcomed probing questions from a human coach and resisted the same questions from an AI coach, yet the coached behaviours appeared after both sessions. What people say friction costs and what it does can diverge, so RQ3 pairs workload ratings with detection latency and refixations. [/DRAFTED]

This question sits at the center of my dissertation, which examines how individuals navigate opaque and cooptive environments, a phenomenon I call algorithmacy. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the transition from scriptio continua [F16] to the historical "grammar of legibility"; Section 3 synthesizes foundational research in eye movements, event segmentation, and supervisory control; Section 4 operationalizes the architecture of decision spaces; Section 5 details the experimental methodology and statistical model; Section 6 analyzes possible empirical outcomes; Section 7 examines the theoretical implications for algorithmacy and interaction design; and Sections 8 and 9 address study limitations and conclusions.

## Flags (your decision on each)

**F1 — hypothesis stated as finding.** No study I read shows that streaming "induces automation complacency." Bhat (2025) found higher *perceived* trustworthiness for streamed text (self-report, 103 participants), and Park and Lee (2024) found more fixations, but neither measured complacency or error detection. Your own RQ2 tests this. Possible fix: "may trigger … and may suppress …".

**F2 — N = 42 has no justification.** Section 5 needs a power analysis or a stated rationale. The outline lists sample size as an open decision (`outline.md`). One model to copy: Tatasciore et al. (2026, p. 9) took d = .43 from their own prior within-subjects studies and computed a minimum of 73 for power .95.

**F3 — one study, stated as general.** "In eye-tracking evaluations of streaming interfaces" rests on one study: Park and Lee (2024), N = 36, Korean expository text, and topic confounded with condition (`notes/parklee2024.md`). Their bottom-generated types produced more fixation starts, more total fixation and larger saccade amplitudes than text appearing whole at the top (Tables 11–12), and fixation starts clustered in the lower AOIs where new text appeared (Table 9). The results are accurate; "fundamentally alters" and the plural "evaluations" overstate them.

**F4 — not a controlled measurement.** Liu et al. (2024, Andes, arXiv preprint) derive 4.8 tokens/s by converting an age-table average of words per minute, at about 1.3 tokens per word, from a secondary web source (v1 §2.1, Table 1). It is an estimate the authors use as a delivery target.

**F5 — two numbers unsupported.** (a) "40 to 45 words per second": Xiao and Yang (2025, §3.2) give about 45 WPS for the GPT-4o API; neither paper gives 40, and Liu et al. report no API speed in words per second. (b) "between 11 and 25 tokens per second": Andes v2 §2.2 reports one figure, 11.2 tokens/s average from vLLM on a real trace, which is a server, not a front-end client. I found no source for 25 or for client-side throttling.

**F6 — percentile read as a threshold.** Xiao and Yang (2025, §3.2) report the 99th-percentile *comfortable streaming speed* from a PEST procedure (79 valid Prolific participants): 21.20 WPS for an easy passage and 11.97 WPS for a complex one. "Comfortable comprehension only when … below" turns a speed preference into a comprehension limit; comprehension was not the measure. My notes record only an "easy" and a "complex" passage; check whether "complex technical reasoning" describes it.

**F7 — not in the source (now read in full).** Rayner, Fischer and Pollatsek (1998) say nothing about "low-spatial-frequency boundaries" or parafoveal vision in those terms. They found that removing spaces cut reading rate by 54% for sentences and 44% for passages (Exp. 1, p. 1132; 58% in Exp. 2, p. 1138). Regressions rose from 9.7% to 17.3% (sentences), and readers landed at the start of words instead of just left of centre (pp. 1136, 1141). They concluded that missing spaces interfere with both word identification and eye-movement control, the first more (p. 1143). The nearest sentence to yours is that spaces enhance words "as 'targets' for an eye movement" (p. 1129).

**F8 — supported (now read in full).** Schotter, Tran and Rayner (2014), N = 40: the trailing mask lowered comprehension (b = −0.92, z = 5.09, p < .001), and not only for garden-path sentences (p. 1221). Comprehension was 78% in normal reading without a regression and 71% under the mask (p. 1223). Note the scope: single one-line sentences. "Word masking" should be "a trailing mask".

**F9 — wrong study design.** Haverkamp et al. (2023) compared reader-controlled scrolling with paging in a PDF (N = 145). There was no auto-scroll and no cursor. Readers who scrolled backtracked less often (2.49 vs 3.53 instances) but farther (`notes/haverkamp2023.md`). The sentence describes streaming interfaces; Haverkamp can support only "scrolling reduced strategic backtracking."

**F10 — the source limits the claim (now read).** Yantis and Jonides (1984) show that an abrupt onset is identified first in visual search, but they add that an onset is not enough by itself: capture works "only when the visual field contains but one such event" (p. 617). A token stream fits that while one new word appears against static text. It fits less well when chunks of several words appear at once. They did not study motion transients. Wolfe (2021, p. 1069) adds that bottom-up salience is "short-lived" and that "attention capture" may be a misnomer for a bump in the priority map. "Repeatedly seizing ... across the visual field" goes beyond both papers.

**F11 — not read.** Gibson (1979) and O'Regan and Noë (2001) were not read this session (not open access). The claim is interpretive; it is yours to make, but check the wording against the texts.

**F12 — overstated.** Bhat (2025, now read in full) measured perceptions only, with no accuracy judgment. Perceived effectiveness did not differ between streamed and static display (F = 0.16, p = .69), and Bhat reads that as users separating presentation from "actual utility" (§5.1.1), which cuts against "conflate." Kim et al. (2025) showed whole responses, not streamed ones, and measured reliance on objective answers. Neither shows readers "conflate fluency with accuracy." Kim et al. do write that "the properties that make such explanations intelligible and compelling may be precisely those that lead to overreliance" (§5.1); that sentence would support a narrower claim.

**F13 — task mislabelled.** Kim et al. (2025) used hard binary factual questions (N = 308), not collaborative decision-making. Values: with an incorrect answer, accuracy was 17.2% with an explanation vs 21.8% without (Table 1). "Frequently" is not their word.
