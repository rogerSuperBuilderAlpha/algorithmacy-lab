## 2. Each model in brief

### Oracy
- **Origin.** Wilkinson coined "oracy" for "the ability to use the oral skills of speaking and listening." He coined it "to try to give spoken language skills comparable status" to reading and writing (Mercer et al., 2017, p. 2–3, citing Wilkinson, 1965, p. 13).
- **Structure.** Four skill areas: physical, linguistic, cognitive, and social and emotional. A teacher selects from them according to the task (Mercer et al., §1.4, §2.2.1).
- **Development.** Progress is "gaining increasing control over … language to a wider range of audiences, for a greater variety of purposes and in different settings" (Cinamon & Elding, 1998, quoted by Mercer et al., §1.3).
- **Assessment problems.** Speech is ephemeral. Speech acts depend on context. In talk, one person's performance is bound up with others', which makes it hard to isolate; PISA's workaround was to replace the other group member with a computer agent (Mercer et al., §1.3).
- **Stakes.** Talk is a cognitive tool ("interpsychological thinking is a prerequisite for intrapsychological thinking"), a requirement for school participation, and linked to social mobility (Mercer et al., §1.1). One motivation is explicitly political: oracy "is more likely to be recognised … if it can be assessed" (§1.1).

### Literacy
- **Cognitive model.** Reading comprehension = decoding × linguistic comprehension. Each is necessary and neither is sufficient (Hoover & Gough, 1990 [A]). Later work adds two things: the components overlap, and active, self-regulatory processes contribute (Duke & Cartwright, 2021 [A]).
- **Social model.** Street's autonomous model treats literacy as a neutral technical skill with built-in effects. His ideological model treats it as a social practice "embedded in socially constructed epistemological principles" and "always contested." Street distinguishes literacy *events* (occasions involving a text) from literacy *practices* (the cultural conceptions that give events their meaning) (Street, 2003, pp. 77–79).
- **Limits of the local.** Brandt and Clinton argue that literacy practices "are not typically invented by their practitioners." Instead, "consolidating technologies" originating outside the local scene "destabilize the functions, uses, values and meanings of literacy" (quoted in Street, 2003, p. 79). Street replies that distant literacies are "hegemonic," not neutral, and that local users "take hold" of them and produce hybrids (pp. 79–80).
- **The consequences debate.** The great-divide thesis (Goody & Watt; Havelock; Ong) held that literacy causes categorical cognitive change [2°]. Scribner and Cole studied Vai literates who had no schooling and found the consequences were localized to specific practices [A]. Recent neuroscience finds real but specific reorganization (Dehaene et al., 2015 [A]).
- **Plurality.** Literacies are multiple, situated, and contested over "whose literacies" count (Street, 2003, p. 77).

### Numeracy, including statistical and mathematical literacy
- **Definition.** PIAAC Cycle 2 defines numeracy as "accessing, using and reasoning critically with mathematical content, information and ideas represented in multiple ways in order to engage in and manage the mathematical demands of a range of situations in adult life" (quoted in Gal et al., 2020, §2.5).
- **Components.** Numeracy is "a set of diverse skills, knowledge-bases, dispositions and affect, communication abilities, and practices and behaviors" (Gal et al., 2020, §1). Gal's model of statistical literacy combines five knowledge elements with a dispositional element:
  - mathematical knowledge
  - statistical knowledge
  - context knowledge
  - literacy skills
  - critical questions
  - plus a critical stance, beliefs and attitudes

  (Sharma, 2017, summarizing Gal, 2004.)
- **Functional and critical.** The two are "intertwined," with "no hierarchy and no dichotomy." Functional numeracy is a "stepping-off point" for critical numeracy. Even so, "it is possible and necessary to be critical even without fully understanding the mathematical part first," and algorithmic processes are "black boxes, even for those who produce them" (Gal et al., 2020, §2.5).
- **Domains.** Health, financial, digital, civic and workplace numeracy (Gal et al., 2020, §2.2). Earlier: Steen's five purposes (practical, professional, civic, recreational, cultural), cited there.
- **Invisibility.** Mathematics "is becoming increasingly invisible, and sometimes disappears within technological systems" (Gal et al., 2020, §2.2, citing Marr & Hagston, 2007; Straesser, 2015).
- **Levels.** Watson and Callingham's six levels run from idiosyncratic through informal, inconsistent, consistent non-critical and critical to critical-mathematical (Sharma, 2017).
- **Interdependence.** "There is no literacy without numeracy, and no numeracy without literacy" (Gal et al., 2020, §2.3).
- **Competence as a dynamic structure.** Numeracy depends on "opportunities to practice a behavior (i.e., external affordances)" and on feedback (Gal et al., 2020, §5, citing Rychen & Salganik, 2003).
- **Affect.** Mathematics anxiety, prior experience and identity shape whether people engage at all (Gal et al., 2020, §3.3).

## 4. What is specific to algorithmacy

Each issue is stated as a contrast with the three older models. Each says whether it extends a common thread or breaks with it.

**A. The medium acts back.**
- *Contrast.* Script and number are static. Speech has an interlocutor, but one who shares a communicative interest. The algorithmic intermediary responds, adapts and pursues its own objectives. This is your triad: "users mediated by an opaque, optimizing, and recursive intermediary."
- *Relation to the threads.* Extends thread 2 and breaks it: the medium is a party, not just a channel.

**B. The code is hidden, proprietary and moving.**
- *Contrast.* Letters and numerals are public conventions that are stable for generations. An intermediary's regularities are undisclosed, change when it is retrained, and are personalised.
- *Consequence.* "Decoding" has no fixed target, so the D in D × C has to be redefined as perceiving where judgment is needed rather than knowing a code.
- *Relation.* Breaks thread 3's assumption of a learnable code. Extends thread 13: Gal's "invisible" mathematics and Brandt & Clinton's distant design.

**C. The code is opaque even to its makers.**
- *Source.* Gal et al. (2020, §2.5), citing Michael & Lupton (2016).
- *Consequence.* Algorithmacy cannot rest on full technical understanding. Its critical side has to work without it, which Gal says is "possible and necessary" for numeracy.
- *Relation.* Takes thread 5 to its limit.

**D. The gaps are unmarked: decision spaces.**
- *Contrast.* Script developed visible marks for its units: spaces and punctuation. Algorithmic processes leave their judgment points unmarked, and fluency and pace co-opt them.
- *Consequence.* This is the perceptual layer your HCI paper addresses: perceiving decision spaces is the "decoding" factor of algorithmacy.
- *Relation.* New. No older model has a component for locating where judgment is required, because in older media the reader sets the pace and the medium has no interest in hiding the gaps.

**E. Participation precedes competence: co-optation.**
- *Contrast.* In literacy, numeracy and oracy, competence comes first and participation follows; schools teach before people use. Platforms enrol participants "without requiring traditional prerequisites" (your Substack, citing Vallas & Schor, 2020), and learning is tacit and arrives through use. Your example: Eslami et al. (2015) found 62.5% of users unaware of the curation algorithm, yet adapted to it.
- *Consequences.*
  - The developmental model has to start from *co-opted, tacit* fluency, not from instruction.
  - Assessment cannot assume a teaching sequence.
- *Relation.* Inverts thread 8's acquisition order.

**F. Fluency can be capture.**
- *Contrast.* In reading, automaticity is unambiguously good. In algorithmacy, automatic fit can be fit to "hidden regularities" (your phrase) that the intermediary engineered. The fluent user is then navigated rather than navigating.
- *Consequence.* The top of the developmental scale cannot simply be "fluent automaticity." It must be fluency fitted to the decision spaces themselves (see the outline, §5.5), combined with the critical capacity of Watson & Callingham's top levels.
- *Relation.* Complicates thread 8. **This is the sharpest difference from literacy, and the strongest theoretical contribution.**

**G. Recursion.**
- *Contrast.* The user's behaviour retrains the intermediary, which then changes what the user meets. Print does not learn from its readers.
- *Consequence.* Competence is partly a property of the user–system history, which pushes Gal's "dynamic structure" further: the affordances themselves move.
- *Relation.* Extends Gal's account of competence as dynamic (thread 4).

**H. Stratified media.**
- *Contrast.* The same printed text reaches every reader. An intermediary constitutes users differently according to their distance from its training norm (your "stratified subject").
- *Consequence.* Equal algorithmacy can produce unequal outcomes, so outcome-based assessment confounds competence with treatment.
- *Relation.* Extends thread 11; complicates thread 9.

**I. An interested, persuasive medium.**
- *Contrast.* Printed texts can persuade, but the medium does not tune its persuasion to the reader in real time.
- *Evidence.* Streaming display raised perceived persuasiveness (F = 76.89) and competence without raising perceived effectiveness (Bhat, 2025 [F]).
- *Relation.* New relative to literacy; partly anticipated by critical numeracy's attention to advertising and disinformation (Gal et al., 2020, §4.3.1).

**J. What counts as a performance.**
- *Contrast.* Oracy struggles to isolate one person's performance inside an interaction (Mercer et al., §1.3). Algorithmacy has the same problem with a non-human party whose state the assessor may not control. PISA's computer-agent workaround for collaboration (Mercer et al., §1.3) is an ironic precedent.
- *Relation.* Extends thread 9.

## 5. A working structure for algorithmacy built from these threads

Offered for you to accept, cut or rewrite. Each part is tied to the thread it comes from.

1. **Definition** (thread 1). "Algorithmacy is the competency of navigating triadic coordination" (yours).
2. **Medium** (thread 2, issue A). Output of an opaque, optimizing, recursive intermediary.
3. **Core product** (thread 3, issues B and D).
   - **Navigation = perception of decision spaces × a model of the intermediary.**
   - Neither factor is sufficient. A user with a good model of the system who cannot see *where* judgment is required fails, and so does a user who sees the gaps without a model of what the system is doing.
   - The HCI paper covers the first factor.
4. **Components** (thread 4). Perceptual, interpretive (modeling the intermediary), dispositional (vigilance, trust calibration) and social (coordinating with the human on the other side of the triad).
5. **Functional and critical** (thread 5, issue C). Intertwined, as in Gal; critical without full technical understanding.
6. **Events and practices** (thread 6). An algorithmacy event is an encounter at a decision space; an algorithmacy practice is the culturally shaped way people navigate them.
7. **Domains** (thread 7). Gig and work, search and information, generative AI, civic and administrative, social and feed.
8. **Levels** (threads 8 and 12, issues E and F). Draft levels, modeled on Watson & Callingham:
   - (1) unaware / co-opted
   - (2) tacitly fitted (fluent but navigated)
   - (3) aware but inconsistent
   - (4) consistent but non-critical
   - (5) critical
   - (6) critical-systemic: can locate decision spaces and model the intermediary's interests
9. **Consequences** (thread 12). Claim specific consequences tied to specific practices, as Scribner & Cole and Dehaene do, not a great divide.
10. **Stakes** (thread 11, issue H). Vulnerability as a continuum; stratification as a confound.

## 6. What this means for the HCI paper

- The HCI paper's perceptual layer is the **first factor of the core product** (§5 item 3): perceiving decision spaces, the algorithmacy analogue of decoding in the simple view of reading. One sentence in the paper's §5.0 can say this and cite Hoover & Gough (1990) [A].
- **Issue F** (fluency can be capture) is the theoretical basis for the fluency resolution already placed in outline §5.5. Cite it as the algorithmacy-specific departure from literacy.
- **Issue D** (unmarked gaps) is the paper's own contribution to algorithmacy theory: no older competence model has a component for locating hidden judgment points.
- **Threads 6 and 12** should be named in one paragraph so that reviewers who know Street or Scribner & Cole see that the autonomous-model trap has been anticipated.
