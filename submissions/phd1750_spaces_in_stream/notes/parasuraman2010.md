# parasuraman2010: Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration
Full citation: Parasuraman, R., & Manzey, D. H. (2010). Human Factors, 52(3), 381–410. | DOI: 10.1177/0018720810376055 | Read: abstract, introduction, and the integrated-model section in full (pp. 381, 397–403; TU Berlin repository PDF); the review sections pp. 382–397 read in part only.

Question: How do automation complacency and automation bias relate, and what cognitive processes underlie them? (p. 381, Objective)
Claim: They are "different manifestations of overlapping automation-induced phenomena, with attention playing a central role" (p. 381, Conclusion).
Key points with locators:
- Complacency "occurs under conditions of multiple-task load, when manual tasks compete with the automated task for the operator's attention" (p. 381, Results).
- Automation bias yields omission and commission errors; not prevented by training or instructions (p. 381).
- Bahner, Huper et al. (2008): 21% committed commission errors despite easily recognizable automation error (p. 401).
- Manzey, Reichenbach et al. (2008): ~43% missed a wrong diagnosis the first time; half of these had sampled all needed information (p. 402).
- "looking-but-not-seeing effect, analogous to ... 'inattentional blindness'" (p. 403).
- Three sources of commission errors: overt redirection of visual attention, less attentive processing of looked-at information, active discounting (p. 403).
- Moray & Inagaki (2000) critique: complacency should be judged against an optimal sampling rate; eye-movement studies cited as exceptions (pp. 399–400).
