# zacks2009: Segmentation in Reading and Film Comprehension
Full citation: Zacks, J. M., Speer, N. K., & Reynolds, J. R. (2009). Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 138(2), 307–327. | DOI: 10.1037/a0015305 | Read: introduction, Exp. 1 method, Exps. 3–4, and General Discussion in full (PMC author manuscript PMC8710938); Exp. 1 results and Exp. 2 (film) read in part.

Question: Do changes in situational features (characters, goals, causes, objects, locations) predict where readers and viewers perceive event boundaries, and does segmentation relate to the control of reading? (Abstract; Overview)
Claim: Situation changes predict event boundaries in text and film; boundary clauses are read more slowly; less predictable clauses are more often boundaries (Abstract; GD).
Method (Exp. 1): N = 91; narratives from One Boy's Day in three presentation modes — auditory (not reviewable), visual-continuous (self-paced paper, reviewable), and visual-sequential (one clause at a time on screen, software-paced, previous clauses NOT reviewable; mean 3.35 words/s) (Exp. 1 Method). Coarse and fine segmentation by button press or pencil line.
Exp. 3: N = 32, self-paced clause-by-clause reading. Exp. 4: N = 32 predictability ratings.
Key results:
- Coarse and fine boundaries aligned above chance in all three groups (e.g., visual-sequential smallest t(26) = 2.97, p = .006) (Exp. 1 Results).
- Each additional situation change added 92.8 ms reading time, t(31) = 22.53, p < .001 (Exp. 3).
- Each 10% increase in segmentation probability → ~108 ms slower reading; effect fell from 10.8 to 7.0 ms per unit when situation changes were added — partial mediation (Exp. 3).
- Predictability correlated with reading time, mean r = −.276 (Exp. 4, Table 7).
Limitations: situation changes correlated with clause length (Exp. 4 Discussion); predictability self-rated.
My concerns (mine): narrative text, not expository/AI output.

Cited by me for: (1) EST in reading, not just film — readers slow at boundaries, i.e., boundaries regulate processing; (2) the visual-sequential condition is a direct precedent for "software-paced, non-reviewable" text, i.e., a stream.
Quote: "processing situational changes during comprehension is an important determinant of how one segments ongoing activity into events" (Abstract)
