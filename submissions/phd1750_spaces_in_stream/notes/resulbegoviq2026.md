# resulbegoviq2026: Eye Tracking and AI-Generated Content: A Systematic Literature Review of Visual Attention, Cognitive Processing, and User Engagement
Full citation: Resulbegoviq, H., Hlebec, V., Pejić Bach, M., & Stamatović, I. (2026). Journal of Eye Movement Research, 19(5), Article 97. | DOI: 10.3390/jemr19050097 | Read: §3 (methods), §4.2–4.3 (included content types and designs), §5.1–5.2 (discussion) in full (PMC13600100 via browser); §4.4–4.9 and §5.3+ read in part; supplementary tables not read.

Question: What does comparative eye-tracking evidence show about attention to, processing of, and engagement with AI-generated content? (§1.2)
Method: PRISMA 2020; Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed searched 2 July 2026; 896 records → 23 studies, 778 participants (Abstract; §3.2, §3.4). ELIGIBILITY REQUIRED A COMPARATOR (human-generated, authentic, or no-AI condition) (§3.3).
Key results:
- "no modality-independent gaze pattern emerged" (Abstract; §5.1).
- Longer viewing more often tied to difficulty, uncertainty, or checking than to preference (Abstract; §5.2.1).
- Eleven studies used AI text; none of those described is a streamed-vs-static presentation comparison (§4.2.1, §4.2.4).
- Interactive studies: gaze "additionally shaped by interface layout, task sequence, user goals, timing of the generated response" (§4.2.4).
My concerns (mine): the provenance-comparator criterion would exclude a streamed-vs-static study with no human comparator — e.g., Park & Lee (2024) does not appear among the textual studies listed. So this review's silence on streaming is weak evidence of a gap, not proof.
Cited by me for: the most recent synthesis of eye tracking on AI output; supports Roger's need to pair gaze with task outcomes ("gaze as context-dependent process evidence", §5.2.1).
