# zhu2026: Justified or Just Convincing? Error Verifiability as a Dimension of LLM Quality
Full citation: Zhu, X., Le Truong, K., Fogliato, R., Swamy, G., Zhang, W., Yang, M., Ye, L., Liu, B., Liu, M., Ilyas, A., & Wu, Z. S. (2026). arXiv:2604.04418v2. PREPRINT. | Read: §1–§2, §6, §7.1 in full; §8 and appendices skimmed (Appendix A tables checked for the cited numbers).

Claim: v_bal (error verifiability) measures whether a justification helps a rater tell correct from incorrect answers; post-training and scaling raise accuracy without raising verifiability; surface rephrasing and calibrated confidence do not help; two methods that add external information (reflect-and-rephrase, oracle-rephrase) do (Abstract; §1).
Method: v_bal scored mainly by LLM-as-judge raters (GPT-4.1-mini, Claude-Haiku-4.5, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite) (§5). Human validation: 19 undergraduates, 40 MATH500 items; LLM–human κ ≈ 0.48–0.50 in chosen settings (§6, Table 1). Humans' accuracy fell slightly WITH justifications (0.836 → 0.809) (§6.1).
Key number: "the FP score of Tulu3.1-8B drops from 0.717 at SFT to 0.602 after DPO" on GSM8K — a per-cell score for one model on one benchmark, not overall v_bal (§7.1; Appendix A table).
Authors' interpretation (hedged, "This suggests"): preference optimization makes justifications for wrong answers look more convincing (§7.1).
Corrections to the packet: (1) first author is Zhu, not Aggarwal — Aggarwal et al. (2026) is a separate related work (§2); (2) 0.717→0.602 is the FP cell, not "balanced verifiability"; (3) the paper finds that changing presentation WITHOUT new information does NOT help (§1 bullets) — the packet says the opposite.
Cited by me for: fluent justifications do not make errors easier to spot; verification needs added information, not restyling.
