# booth2013: The function of regressions in reading: Backward eye movements allow rereading
Full citation: Booth, R. W., & Weger, U. W. (2013). Memory & Cognition, 41(1), 82–97. (Published online 11 Aug 2012.) | DOI: 10.3758/s13421-012-0244-y | Read: full text (Springer HTML, via browser).

Question: Do long-range regressions serve to reread words, or to cue memory via the words' locations ("deictic pointer" hypothesis)? (Intro)
Claim: Readers regress to reread, not to cue memory by location (Abstract; General discussion).
Method: EyeLink 1000. Exp. 1 N = 22: statement sentence turned illegible (letters substituted, layout kept) once gaze crossed to the question; 2×2×2 with WM load (Exp. 1 Method). Exp. 2 N = 32: auditory words with on-screen "XXXX" placeholders. Exp. 3 N = 19: target word swapped during a regression (gaze-contingent).
Key results:
- Background: regressions are 15%–25% of eye movements in normal reading (Intro, citing Rayner & Pollatsek 1989).
- Inhoff & Weger (2005), cited: regressions on 4% of trials when the sentence disappeared vs 43% when it stayed visible (Intro).
- Exp. 1: fewer regressions when illegible, F(1, 851) = 65.34, p < .001; regression accuracy predicted comprehension only when legible, r(389) = −.13 (Exp. 1 Results).
- Exp. 2: regressions on 25% of trials and not aimed at targets (Exp. 2 Results).
- Exp. 3: after regressing to a changed word, readers chose the changed meaning (M = .68 vs .20), t(18) = 8.16, d = 1.87 (Exp. 3 Results).
Limitations: artificial tasks in Exps. 1–2 (GD).
Quote (GD): "Perhaps future generations of readers, with more experience of reading scrollable text on small screens, may be more inclined to use deictic pointers, as in this situation rereading is often impossible"
Cited by me for: regressions require the text to remain *available*; when streaming auto-scrolls earlier text away, readers lose the rereading function. Complements Schotter et al. (2014) already in Roger's list.
