# bucinca2021: To Trust or to Think: Cognitive Forcing Functions Can Reduce Overreliance on AI in AI-assisted Decision-making
Full citation: Buçinca, Z., Malaya, M. B., & Gajos, K. Z. (2021). Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 5(CSCW1), Article 188. | DOI: 10.1145/3449287 | Read: §1–§4 in full (arXiv 2102.09692v1); §5 (NFC audit) and §6 discussion read in part.
Note: Consensus listed the second author as "Maja Malaya et al." — the paper's author order is Buçinca, Malaya, Gajos. Cite as Buçinca et al.

Question: Do cognitive forcing functions reduce overreliance on AI compared with simple explainable-AI designs? (§3)
Method: N = 199 MTurk; nutrition task (replace high-carb ingredient); simulated AI with 75% accuracy (§3.1, §3.3). Six conditions: no AI; explanation; uncertainty; and three CFFs — on demand, update (decide first), wait (30 s "AI is processing" delay) (§3.2).
Key results:
- On incorrect AI predictions, carb-source detection: correct 0.27 (CFF) vs 0.08 (SXAI), F = 24.11, p ≪ .0001, d = .66; overrelied 0.48 vs 0.64, F = 9.24, p = .003, d = .36 (Table 2).
- No-AI participants outperformed both AI categories when the AI was wrong (§4.1).
- Trade-off: trust and preference correlated negatively with performance on incorrect predictions (§4.3); CFF designs rated more complex (Table 3).
Cited by me for: a decision space can act as a cognitive forcing function — an interruption at decision time. The "wait" condition shows that a pause alone (not segmentation) can reduce overreliance, which is exactly the confound Roger's arbitrary-break control is designed to separate.
