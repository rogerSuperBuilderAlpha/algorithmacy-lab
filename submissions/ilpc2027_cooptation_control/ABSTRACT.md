**Title:** Co-optation as Control: The Labour Process Between Two Rated Parties

## Abstract
Labour process theory locates control in the employment relation. When customers enter the analysis, they enter as an instrument of management: the employer uses customer feedback to discipline workers. Platform work breaks this picture. A marketplace, booking site or service platform employs neither the provider nor the customer. It enrols both in a rating system, turns each side's judgement into a verdict on the other, and binds both to its rules. Stark and colleagues call this co-optation, which differs from command, contract and collaboration. Selznick's co-optation absorbed outsiders into the leadership of an organization. Platform co-optation does the reverse: it projects evaluative authority over people who never chose the platform and have no seat in it.

This paper asks where the frontier of control runs under co-optation, and what autonomy remains to the people on each side of it. **Theoretical orientation:** labour process theory's contested terrain, read through organizational theories of co-optation and of the third party. **Methodology:** a conceptual analysis built on published ethnographies and case studies of online marketplaces and hotel rating platforms. The paper reads each one for how evaluation passes between the two parties and the operator.

**Findings:** Three mechanisms recur. First, the operator fuses the counterpart's judgement and its own rule into one score, so a worker cannot tell which one acted. Second, every message to the counterpart is also a message to the evaluator, so the worker addresses two audiences at once. Third, the rule changes without notice, so the worker must infer change from results. Under these conditions, task simplification and relational demand rise together. The simpler the task, the more a worker's standing depends on reading the evaluator, making intent legible through it, and tracking its changes.

**Contribution:** The frontier of control now runs between the two co-opted parties as well as between worker and employer. Autonomy there takes two forms. One is individual competence in reading and addressing an evaluator. The other is collective voice, which matters because individual exit forfeits ratings that do not transfer between platforms. The paper argues that labour process research should study both forms, and that worker organising on platforms should treat the counterpart as a possible ally, not only a source of discipline.

**Keywords:** co-optation; algorithmic control; platforms; customer evaluation; voice; conceptual analysis
