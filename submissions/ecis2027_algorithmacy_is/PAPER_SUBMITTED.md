# ALGORITHMACY: A CONSTRUCT FOR COORDINATING

*Short Paper*

## Abstract

Although information systems research investigates human-algorithm interaction through constructs such as AI literacy, algorithmic competency, and algorithm sensemaking, prevailing models typically conceptualize the algorithm as a personal assistant, a passive transmission channel, or an automated supervisor. However, digital platforms increasingly position the algorithm directly between interdependent actors, evaluating both parties and enforcing binding outcomes. This paper introduces *algorithmacy*, defined as the capacity to coordinate with an interdependent counterpart through an evaluative platform. This competence entails deciphering ambiguous algorithmic feedback, structuring communicative intent so that a counterpart can interpret it through the system, and tracking unannounced shifts in platform governance. We contrast algorithmacy with adjacent constructs, present a research model linking its antecedents and coordination outcomes, and outline the methodological roadmap for scale development and empirical validation.

**Keywords:** Algorithmacy, Algorithmic Management, AI Literacy, Coordination, Construct Development.

## 1. Introduction

When an online seller’s search rank drops after a lukewarm customer review, diagnosing the root cause is often intractable. The platform may have penalized specific phrasing in the review, recalibrated its sentiment weights, updated its ranking model without notice, or factored in historical account metrics invisible to the buyer. As Curchod et al. (2020) observed among eBay merchants, reviews do not operate as private bilateral feedback between trading partners. Instead, an algorithmic engine processes them to determine market visibility, transforming a dyadic transaction into a triadic relationship among buyer, seller, and platform operator.

Current information systems (IS) research does not adequately capture the competence required in this environment because prevailing constructs frame algorithmic systems in fundamentally different roles. AI literacy examines how individuals operate computational tools; algorithm literacy considers how consumers interpret curated feeds; algorithmic competency focuses on how gig workers navigate top-down algorithmic management; and AI-mediated communication investigates how automated phrasing alters interpersonal perception. None of these constructs addresses the challenge of two interdependent parties coordinating through an opaque intermediary that simultaneously evaluates both sides and enforces binding outcomes.

This mismatch is theoretical rather than psychometric: it cannot be resolved simply by appending new items to existing instruments. Existing scales assume either bilateral or hierarchical relationships, whereas algorithmacy emerges specifically from the demands of this triadic position. Because the construct reflects what an evaluative platform removes from direct interpersonal coordination, its core dimensions stem from structural platform mechanics rather than generic digital skill taxonomies. This paper establishes algorithmacy's discriminant boundaries, presents a research model for empirical testing, and outlines priorities for scale development and validation.

## 2. Four Positions for the System

The primary distinction among constructs governing human-algorithm interaction lies in how they configure the structural relationship between the system and its human users. Table 1 summarizes these configurations across four relational positions.

*Table 1. Four Structural Positions of the System in Human-Algorithm Interaction*

| Position of the System | Associated Constructs | Relational Scope | Excluded Dynamics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Partner to one user | AI literacy; algorithm literacy | Single user and the system | An interdependent counterpart on the other side |
| Channel between two users | AI-mediated communication competence | Two people; system acts as a conduit | The system’s independent evaluative verdict |
| Manager over one worker | Algorithmic competency; algorithm sensemaking | Single worker and the system | The customer or counterpart |
| Evaluator between two people | Algorithmacy (this paper) | Two interdependent users and the system | None |

- **Partner to one user.** Under this framing, scholarship examines dyadic interactions between an isolated individual and a technology. For example, Long and Magerko’s (2020) conceptualization of AI literacy spans seventeen competencies detailing how an individual critically evaluates, collaborates with, and uses AI as a tool. While one competency acknowledges the professionals who build and tune these models, none accounts for an interdependent human counterpart with whom the user must coordinate. Algorithm literacy operates within the same boundary. Dogruel et al. (2022) define it in terms of user awareness, procedural knowledge, critical evaluation of system outputs, and individual coping strategies. As recent reviews emphasize (Gagrčin et al., 2026; Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2025), the literature remains fragmented across overlapping terms, yet almost uniformly centers on a lone user interpreting a curated feed.
- **Channel between two users.** This configuration brings interpersonal interaction into view, but sidelines the system’s independent evaluative authority. Hancock et al. (2020, p. 89) define AI-mediated communication as interpersonal exchange in which an intelligent agent modifies, augments, or generates messages on a communicator’s behalf to achieve communication goals. While this model accounts for two human actors, the algorithm operates primarily as a proxy or communicative aid. Research in this stream examines how automated linguistic adjustments shape mutual impressions, interpersonal trust, and relational dynamics; however, the system issues no binding administrative or economic judgments on either party.
- **Manager over one worker.** Scholarship on platform labor adopts a hierarchical perspective, positioning the system directly above the worker. Researchers examine how workers navigate computational control: developing practical tactics to decode automated assignments (Jarrahi & Sutherland, 2019), building platform literacy to buffer against structural precarity (Sutherland et al., 2020, p. 470), or engaging in algorithm sensemaking to decipher opaque supervisory practices (Möhlmann et al., 2023). Zhou et al. (2025) formalized this perspective by validating a four-dimensional scale of algorithmic competency—spanning understanding, embracing, leveraging, and remediating algorithmic management. Although these models take algorithmic authority seriously, their relational scope remains dyadic: an isolated worker negotiating with an automated supervisor. When counterparts appear, they are treated merely as external risk factors; Zhou et al.’s Item 8, for example, captures exploratory tactics to avoid negative customer feedback rather than strategies to coordinate with the customer.
- **Evaluator between two people.** The fourth position reflects the triadic reality of platform markets. As Stark and Pais (2020, p. 53) argue, platform organization is inherently three-sided, linking the platform owner, the service provider, and the customer. Rather than simply routing communication or exercising ordinary managerial oversight, the platform enlists one party to evaluate the other, synthesizes that input through proprietary algorithms, and enforces the outcome on both (Curchod et al., 2020). By simultaneously matching market participants and automatically controlling provider conduct (Möhlmann et al., 2021), the algorithm operates as an evaluative arbiter between interdependent individuals. No existing literacy or competency construct accounts for all three nodes of this triad.

## 3. Algorithmacy Defined

Algorithmacy is defined as *the competence to coordinate with another person through a system that evaluates both parties and enforces its decisions on each*. Rather than compiling an inventory of generic technical or cognitive skills, this definition begins from a structural position and identifies what that position strips away from direct interpersonal exchange.

In direct, unmediated interaction, two people can ask what happened, clarify what they meant, and mutually negotiate the ground rules of their engagement. An evaluative platform dismantles each of these baseline coordination mechanisms:

1. **Causal conflation:** The system aggregates counterpart input with internal scoring algorithms into a single score or decision, preventing both parties from isolating the true source of an outcome. For instance, Rahman (2021) found that freelance workers could not separate client feedback from automated platform adjustments when evaluating rating declines.
2. **Dual-audience communication:** Because every interaction passes through an automated monitor, messages must simultaneously address two distinct audiences: the human counterpart and the algorithmic scoring system recording their exchange.
3. **Unannounced rule volatility:** Platforms routinely adjust their evaluative and ranking logic without notice, forcing participants to infer institutional shifts from sudden variances in operational outcomes. As DeVito (2021) observed, users under volatile algorithmic regimes must continually revise their "folk theories" as the ground shifts beneath them.

Each constraint corresponds directly to a constitutive facet of algorithmacy:

- **Interpreting:** The capacity to parse ambiguous platform feedback and distinguish counterpart actions from system intervention, thereby restoring causal attribution.
- **Specifying intent:** The ability to formulate actions and communications so that a counterpart can understand them even while an automated evaluator monitors and scores the exchange, thereby preserving mutual understanding.
- **Tracking:** The discipline of detecting unannounced rule changes as distinct from shifts in counterpart behavior, thereby re-establishing predictability in exchange.

These three facets share an analytical focus absent from adjacent constructs: how an evaluative intermediary reconfigures the relationship between two individuals. *Interpreting* resolves which party produced an outcome; *specifying intent* ensures that one’s meaning reaches the counterpart despite algorithmic mediation; and *tracking* determines whether the governing rules between the parties have shifted.

Because algorithmacy centers on triadic coordination rather than computational knowledge, technical expertise is neither necessary nor sufficient. A software engineer well-versed in machine learning may struggle to coordinate effectively under these conditions, whereas a non-technical seller or gig worker may exhibit high algorithmacy honed through sustained platform practice.

## 4. Discriminant Logic

A construct achieves theoretical legitimacy by demonstrating distinct conceptual boundaries and incremental predictive utility relative to established alternatives. For algorithmacy, this boundary becomes evident when technical sophistication or platform-gaming tactics fail to produce effective interpersonal coordination.

- **Distinction from AI and algorithm literacy:** AI literacy and algorithm literacy assess an individual’s technical and conceptual grasp of computational systems. Algorithmacy diverges from both whenever technical understanding is high but relational attunement to the counterpart is absent. A merchant may understand neural networks, collaborative filtering, and ranking models, yet still misattribute a sudden loss of search visibility to a buyer’s review when the platform actually revised its internal weighting formula—or vice versa. Knowing how algorithms work in the abstract does not clarify which party generated a specific outcome. Algorithmacy accounts for this relational attribution; technical literacy does not.
- **Distinction from algorithmic competency:** Algorithmic competency measures how workers understand, leverage, and resist automated supervision. Algorithmacy diverges from this construct when optimizing for the system alienates the human counterpart. Bucher et al. (2021), for example, describe platform workers who placate supervisory algorithms by deliberately curtailing communication with clients to avoid platform flags. A worker who excels at manipulating platform metrics this way may score high on algorithmic competency while actively undermining the collaborative relationship the work depends on. Algorithmic competency treats these evasion tactics as mastery; algorithmacy identifies them as coordination failures.

These conceptual differences establish clear empirical boundaries. Where a system acts as an evaluative arbiter between two interdependent actors, algorithmacy should explain variance in coordination performance beyond what general literacy and supervisory competency scales capture. Conversely, where technology functions merely as a personal tool, a passive communication conduit, or a top-down supervisor, the three facets of algorithmacy should offer little incremental explanatory power.

## 5. Research Model and Hypotheses

FIGURE1

Because algorithmacy develops through situated practice rather than formal instruction, its emergence depends on an individual's immersion in triadic coordination. Two antecedents are primary:

- **Task interdependence:** When participants depend heavily on a counterpart to achieve their goals, every transaction compels them to determine whether friction stems from partner behavior or platform intervention.
- **Platform tenure:** Accumulated experience under an evaluative system provides the empirical feedback loops required to separate counterpart idiosyncrasies from systemic algorithmic patterns.

In turn, algorithmacy directly improves the quality of interpersonal coordination. Actors who accurately parse ambiguous feedback and signal intent across an automated monitor will experience fewer misattributed failures, encounter fewer communicative misunderstandings, and adapt more swiftly to unannounced system adjustments. By clarifying intent and decoupling partner conduct from platform interference, algorithmacy should also significantly reduce disputes stemming from misread feedback.

Two structural moderators bound these coordination benefits:

- **Evaluative authority:** The performance dividend of algorithmacy depends on the degree of control the platform exercises over the actors. When algorithmic verdicts carry binding consequences—such as search demotions, automated penalty fees, or account deactivations—misreading feedback is immediately and severely costly. Conversely, where ratings lack binding enforcement or remain advisory, coordination errors carry lower stakes.
- **Algorithmic volatility:** Tracking competencies become salient primarily in unstable operating environments. Frequent, unannounced rule changes heighten the risk of coordinating on obsolete assumptions. As Kellogg et al. (2020) observe, contemporary algorithmic control is encompassing, instantaneous, interactive, and opaque. Where platforms exhibit these characteristics most acutely, continuously tracking shifting algorithmic rules is essential to sustaining exchange.

Empirical testing will control for AI literacy and algorithmic competency. Establishing discriminant validity requires showing that algorithmacy explains significant incremental variance in coordination quality and dispute reduction beyond general technical knowledge and individual gaming tactics.

## 6. Work Remaining

While this paper formalizes the conceptual foundations and structural model of algorithmacy, the empirical validation program proceeds across four sequential phases:

1. **Item generation:** Draft indicators for each facet are being derived directly from the triadic framework. Rather than assessing generic technical tasks, every item anchors on the relational exchange, capturing how the system intervenes between the user and their counterpart.
2. **Content validation:** An expert panel will evaluate the initial item pool to establish content validity, confirm facet mapping, and verify that no indicator can be answered without accounting for an interdependent counterpart.
3. **Factor structure and discriminant testing:** A pilot study of online marketplace sellers and platform hosts will evaluate the proposed three-factor model via confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and test discriminant validity against established scales of AI literacy and Zhou et al.’s (2025) algorithmic competency instrument.
4. **Model testing:** A longitudinal panel study will test the hypothesized antecedents, coordination outcomes, and structural moderators specified in the research model.

By ECIS 2027, we expect to report the refined item pool, expert panel assessments, and preliminary factor structure from the pilot administration.

## 7. Conclusion

Most information systems constructs frame human interaction with algorithms through familiar relational paradigms: the system as an individual’s tool, a conduit between communicators, or an automated supervisor. Modern platforms, however, increasingly occupy a fourth position—standing directly between interdependent individuals as an evaluative arbiter that enforces binding outcomes on both. In these environments, technical literacy and metric gaming are insufficient; what matters is the capacity to coordinate with another human being through an automated monitor. Algorithmacy captures this competence, anchors its core dimensions in platforms' structural constraints, and provides a precise theoretical foundation for future empirical inquiry.
