# ALGORITHMACY: A CONSTRUCT FOR COORDINATING THROUGH AN EVALUATING SYSTEM

*Short Paper*

## Abstract

IS research has several constructs for how people work with algorithms: AI literacy, algorithm literacy, algorithmic competency and algorithm sensemaking. Each places the system in one position. It is a partner to one user, a channel between two users, or a manager over one worker. Many platforms hold a fourth position: they stand between two interdependent people, evaluate both, and bind both to their verdicts. This short paper defines algorithmacy as the competence to coordinate with another person through such a system. It has three facets. The first is interpreting an ambiguous result, the second is specifying intent so the counterpart can read it through the system, and the third is tracking rule changes that go unannounced. We set out a research model that separates algorithmacy from its rivals, and state the scale development and validation work that remains.

**Keywords:** Algorithmacy, Algorithmic Management, AI Literacy, Coordination, Construct Development.

## 1 Introduction

A marketplace seller sees her search ranking fall after a buyer leaves a lukewarm review. She cannot tell what moved the ranking. It may have been the buyer's words, the platform's weighting of them, a change in the weighting itself, or her own conduct at some earlier point that the platform recorded and the buyer never saw. Curchod et al. (2020) studied sellers in exactly this position on eBay. Customer reviews there do not pass between buyer and seller as private messages. They enter the platform's tally, which judges the seller and decides her visibility. The relation is no longer a dyad between buyer and seller but a triad among operator, buyer and seller.

What competence does the seller need? IS research offers several answers. Each answers a different question, because each assumes a different position for the system. AI literacy asks what a person should understand about a system they use. Algorithm literacy asks what a person knows about the algorithms that curate what they see. Algorithmic competency asks how a worker understands and works with the algorithms that assign and evaluate their work. Studies of AI-mediated communication ask how a system that modifies messages changes what two communicators think of each other. None asks what the seller needs: the competence to coordinate with another person through a system that judges them both.

This paper argues that the gap is structural, not a matter of missing items. The existing constructs cannot be extended to cover the seller by adding questions, because they place the system in a different position relative to the people involved. I define algorithmacy as the competence that the fourth position demands. The construct is defined by what the evaluating system takes away from the two parties, so its facets follow from the structure rather than from a list of desirable skills. The paper then sets out discriminant logic against the two nearest rivals and a research model for testing the construct, and closes with the work that remains.

## 2 Four Positions for the System

Constructs for working with algorithms differ less in their items than in where they place the system relative to the people who use it. Table 1 sets out four positions.

| Position of the system | Construct | Who is counted | What it leaves out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Partner to one user | AI literacy; algorithm literacy | One person and the system | A second person on the other side |
| Channel between two users | AI-mediated communication competence | Two people; the system carries messages | The system's own verdict |
| Manager over one worker | Algorithmic competency; algorithm sensemaking | One worker and the system | The client or counterpart |
| Evaluator between two people | Algorithmacy (this paper) | Two people and the system | — |

*Table 1. Four positions for the system.*

**Partner to one user.** Long and Magerko (2020) define AI literacy as a set of competencies that let an individual evaluate AI critically, communicate and collaborate with it, and use it as a tool. Their seventeen competencies are about what a person should understand of AI. One competency names other people, the humans who program and tune AI systems, but no competency names a counterpart with whom the user coordinates through the system. Algorithm literacy follows the same pattern. Dogruel et al. (2022) define it through awareness of algorithms, knowledge of how they work, critical evaluation of their decisions, and the skill to cope with or influence them. Two integrative reviews find a field with many overlapping constructs and no cohesive framework (Gagrčin et al., 2026; Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum, 2025). In both, the relation studied is between a user and the platform that curates their feed.

**Channel between two users.** Hancock et al. (2020, p. 89) define AI-mediated communication as "interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication goals." Here two people are counted. The system acts for one of them and changes what passes between them, and research asks how its presence changes what each thinks of the other. The system does not judge either of them.

**Manager over one worker.** Studies of platform work place the system above the worker. Jarrahi and Sutherland (2019) describe workers who develop algorithmic competencies, a practical literacy for understanding and working with the algorithms that assign and assess their work. Sutherland et al. (2020, p. 470) name a "platform literacy, which is the know-how required to leverage platform resources in order to minimize the precarity of independent work." Möhlmann et al. (2023) describe algorithm sensemaking, the process by which workers make sense of algorithmic management. Zhou et al. (2025) built and validated the first scale of algorithmic competency, with four dimensions: understanding, embracing, leveraging and remediating algorithmic management. These constructs take the system's power seriously. They count one worker and one system. Two of Zhou et al.'s twelve items mention customers, but as objects of the worker's strategy toward the system. Item 8 reads, "I proactively explore AM rules to minimize negative customer feedback". No item asks about coordinating with the customer.

**Evaluator between two people.** The fourth position is the seller's. Stark and Pais (2020, p. 53) describe the platform relation as "typically three-sided": the owner, the provider and the user-customer. The platform does not merely carry messages between provider and customer, and it does not merely manage the provider. It enrols the customer in evaluating the provider, fuses that evaluation with its own rule, and binds the provider to the result (Curchod et al., 2020). Möhlmann et al. (2021) show that on such platforms the algorithm matches the two sides and controls the provider at once. In this position the system is an evaluator between two people, and no existing construct counts all three.

## 3 Algorithmacy Defined

I define algorithmacy as the competence to coordinate with another person through a system that evaluates both and binds both to its verdict. The definition does not list skills. It names a structural position and asks what the position takes away from the two people in it.

Coordinating directly, two people can ask each other what happened, say what they meant, and agree on the rules of their exchange. The evaluating system removes each of these. First, it fuses the counterpart's input with its own rule into one result, so neither party can tell who caused an outcome. Rahman (2021) found that freelancers on one platform could not tell how much of their score came from the client and how much from the platform. Second, every message to the counterpart also reaches the evaluator, so a message must be written for two readers. Third, the system changes its rule without notice, so the parties must infer change from shifts in their results. DeVito (2021) found that users of changing platforms keep revising their theories of how the system works, because the system moves under them.

Each removal defines one facet of algorithmacy.

- **Interpreting** is reading an ambiguous result to tell the counterpart's contribution from the system's. It restores the ability to attribute responsibility.
- **Specifying intent** is writing so that the counterpart can read what an action meant even as the system scores it. It restores common understanding between the parties.
- **Tracking** is noticing when the system's rule has changed, as distinct from a change in the counterpart. It restores predictability.

The three facets share an object that no rival construct has: the relation between the two people, as the system shapes it. Interpreting asks who caused a result between us. Specifying intent asks what my action means to you. Tracking asks whether the rules between us have changed. A person can know a great deal about machine learning and still fail at all three, and a person with no technical knowledge can be good at all three through practice.

## 4 Discriminant Logic

A new construct earns its place only if it predicts something its rivals cannot. Algorithmacy should diverge from its two nearest rivals in identifiable cases.

**Against AI literacy and algorithm literacy.** These measure what a person knows about algorithms. Algorithmacy should diverge from them where knowledge is high but the counterpart is ignored. A technically sophisticated seller may understand ranking systems well and still misread a drop in visibility as the buyer's fault when the platform changed its weighting, or the reverse. Knowledge of the system does not tell her which of the two acted. Algorithmacy predicts better attribution; literacy does not.

**Against algorithmic competency.** Algorithmic competency measures a worker's strategy toward the system. Algorithmacy should diverge from it where a worker games the system but loses the counterpart. Bucher et al. (2021) describe workers who pacify the algorithm by curtailing their contact with clients to stay clear of its scrutiny. Such workers may score high on leveraging the system and still damage the relation the work depends on. Algorithmacy predicts that these workers coordinate worse with their counterparts; algorithmic competency predicts the opposite.

The two divergences give the construct a testable boundary. In settings where the system holds the fourth position, algorithmacy should explain variance in coordination with the counterpart beyond what literacy and algorithmic competency explain. In settings where the system is only a partner, a channel or a manager, the three facets should add little.

## 5 Research Model

Figure 1 sets out the model. Algorithmacy is the focal construct, measured as three correlated facets.

*Figure 1. Research model. Task interdependence → Algorithmacy (interpreting, specifying intent, tracking) → coordination quality with the counterpart; dispute rate. Moderators on the algorithmacy–outcome paths: perceived system authority; rule-change frequency. Controls: AI literacy; algorithmic competency.*

**Antecedents.** Task interdependence should raise algorithmacy, because people whose outcomes depend on a counterpart through the system get more practice in reading and addressing both. Tenure on the system should raise it for the same reason.

**Outcomes.** Algorithmacy should raise the quality of coordination with the counterpart: fewer misattributed failures, fewer misread messages and faster adjustment after rule changes. It should also lower the rate of disputes that arise from misattribution.

**Moderators.** The effect of algorithmacy on outcomes should be stronger where the system's verdicts carry more authority over the parties, because misreading a binding verdict costs more than misreading an advisory one. It should also be stronger where the system changes its rules often, because tracking matters most when the rules move. Kellogg et al. (2020) describe algorithmic control as more encompassing, instantaneous, interactive and opaque than earlier forms, which is the condition under which these moderators should be high.

**Controls.** AI literacy and algorithmic competency enter as controls. The discriminant claim of Section 4 is that algorithmacy predicts the outcomes over and above both.

## 6 Work Remaining

This short paper offers the construct and the model; the empirical work is ahead. Four steps remain before ECIS 2027 and after it. First, I am generating items for each facet from the structural definition, so that every item names the counterpart and the system together. Second, expert review will check that each item belongs to one facet and that no item could be answered without reference to a counterpart. Third, a pilot with platform sellers and hosts will test the three-facet structure by confirmatory factor analysis, with AI literacy and the Zhou et al. (2025) algorithmic-competency scale as discriminant measures. Fourth, a panel study will test the antecedent, outcome and moderator paths in Figure 1. By the conference I expect to report the item pool, the expert review and the pilot's factor structure.

## 7 Conclusion

The IS constructs for working with algorithms place the system as a partner, a channel or a manager. Many platforms place it between two people as an evaluator, and in that position the competence that matters is coordinating with the other person through the system. Algorithmacy names that competence. Its three facets follow from what the evaluator removes, and its discriminant predictions mark where it should and should not add to what IS research already measures.
