**Title:** Playing by Rules No One Wrote: Keeping the Counterpart in View Under Algorithmic Mediation

## Abstract
The sub-theme asks who sets the rules of play in organizations mediated by opaque algorithms. On platforms, and increasingly inside firms, the answer is that a system sets them. Neither of the people it connects wrote those rules, and the system changes them without notice. A hotel and its guests, a seller and a buyer, or a manager and an employee whose appraisal an AI tool may draft, all coordinate through a system that evaluates both sides and binds both to its verdict. This paper asks what happens to human connection in that arrangement and what keeps it alive.

The argument starts from Simmel's third party. A mediator helps two people settle their own affairs. An arbitrator takes the decision out of their hands. An opaque evaluating system is an arbitrator that neither party chose. Every message one person sends the other is also scored, and every result mixes the other person's judgement with the system's rule. People in this position lose each other in two ways. Some pacify the algorithm, curtailing contact with the counterpart to stay clear of scrutiny. Others treat the system as a neutral channel and read its scores as personal verdicts, missing the rule that judges them both.

Two practices keep the counterpart in view. Specifying intent means writing so that the other person can read what an action meant through the system's filter. Tracking means noticing when the rule has changed, as distinct from a change in the other person. Together these practices are part of what the author calls algorithmacy: the competence to coordinate through an evaluating intermediary. The paper concludes that humanness under algorithmic mediation is not only resistance to the machine. It is also the practical work of reaching another person through a machine that judges both of them.

**Keywords:** algorithmic mediation; humanness; third party; rules; coordination
