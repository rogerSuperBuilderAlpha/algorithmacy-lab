## What the talk may use it for
CP 1.363 holds Peirce's clearest argument for both clauses, and the talk's first section can be built on it. Negative clause: giving is a triple fact, and even if A enriches B, B receives C and A parts with C, it does not follow that A gave C to B unless the three dyads are "welded into one fact." Positive clause: "A sells C to B for the price D" is two triple facts that share a minted term E. The reason is valency. A dyadic relative is a form with two blanks and a triadic one a form with three, so joining two triads at one shared index X leaves four blanks, and joining two dyads the same way leaves two. The road image carries it: three-way forks give any number of termini, while straight roads laid end to end never give more than two. CP 1.356 is the terse definition of first, second and third.

**Slide-safe (checked against the printed page, CP vol. 1 pp. 187-188):** the "genuine three" sentence, the enrich/receive/part-with passage, the two blank-place sentences and the road sentence. **Not slide-safe as CP prints it:** the sale example. CP's letters contradict its own premise, and the critical edition reads them the other way (Notes).

## Loci
- CP 1.363 (vol. 1 p. 187) — "The reason is that while it is impossible to form a genuine three by any modification of the pair, without introducing something of a different nature from the unit and the pair, four, five, and every higher number can be formed by mere complications of threes." — both clauses. Page image.
- CP 1.363 (vol. 1 p. 188) — "A may enrich B, B may receive C, and A may part with C, and yet A need not necessarily give C to B. For that, it would be necessary that these three dual relations should not only coexist, but be welded into one fact. Thus we see that a triad cannot be analyzed into dyads." — negative clause, by example. Page image. (W6 prints "Thus, we see".)
- CP 1.363 (vol. 1 p. 188) — "Take the quadruple fact that A sells C to B for the price D. This is a compound of two facts: first, that A makes with C a certain transaction, which we may name E; and second, that this transaction E is a sale of B for the price D." — positive clause (the tetrad). Page image. CP's reading; W6 swaps B and C (Notes).
- CP 1.363 (vol. 1 p. 188) — "we can take two of these triple relatives and fill up one blank place in each with the same letter, X, … and then the two taken together will form a whole having four blank places" — two triads joined at one blank: 3+3−2 = 4. Page image.
- CP 1.363 (vol. 1 p. 188) — "But when we attempt to imitate this proceeding with dual relatives, and combine two of them by means of an X, we find we only have two blank places in the combination, just as we had in either of the relatives taken by itself." — two dyads joined at one blank: 2+2−2 = 2, so dyads compose only into dyads. Page image.
- CP 1.363 (vol. 1 p. 188) — "A road with only three-way forkings may have any number of termini, but no number of straight roads put end on end will give more than two termini." — the valency image. Page image; the same words in W6 (FTS snippet).
- CP 1.356 — "The first is that whose being is simply in itself, not referring to anything … The second is that which is what it is by force of something to which it is second. The third is that which is what it is owing to things between which it mediates and which it brings into relation to each other." — the three categories in one place. Scan OCR and e-text.
- CP 1.357 — "The first must therefore be present and immediate" — Firstness as immediacy. Scan OCR and e-text.
- CP 1.358 — "The genuine second suffers and yet resists, like dead matter, whose existence consists in its inertia." — Secondness as resistance. Scan OCR and e-text.

## Notes
- **The sale example differs between CP and the critical edition.** CP 1.363 (page image, vol. 1 p. 188) prints "A makes with C a certain transaction … this transaction E is a sale of B for the price D." That contradicts the premise "A sells C to B", in which C is the goods and B the buyer. The Peirce Edition Project text in W6 (Open Library full-text-search snippets of the scan) reads "1st, that A makes with B a certain transaction, which we may name E; and 2nd, that this transaction E is a sale of C for the price D." The W6 reading is the coherent one, so CP appears to have swapped the two letters. Works that reprint or quote CP carry the swap. The same snippets show it in Hoopes, *Peirce on Signs*, and in the English of Apel's *From Pragmatism to Pragmaticism*. Koshkin's paraphrase (arXiv 2406.14058, p. 3) carries it, and so does the lab's `thinkers/peirce/paper.md` l. 105-106. For a slide, either quote W6 and name it, or quote CP with "[sic]" and give the W6 letters. Before print, confirm the W6 reading on the page. The snippets are scan OCR, and I did not see the W6 page number.
- **Paraphrase inside quotation marks in the lab papers.** `thinkers/peirce/paper.md` l. 105-106 quotes "A makes with C a certain transaction E" and "E is a sale of B for the price D" as CP 1.363. Those strings are Koshkin's paraphrase, not Peirce's text. Peirce wrote "A makes with C a certain transaction, which we may name E" and "this transaction E is a sale of B for the price D" (CP), or the W6 reading above. `exegesis.md` C5 marks them "paraphrased in Koshkin", but `paper.md` drops that flag.
- **Date.** The CP editors date the work c. 1890 (vol. 1 p. 181 note). W6 dates it 1887-88. W6's scan OCR, seen in snippets, reads "Selections 22-28. A Guess at the Riddle /1887-1888/ Typescript, 67 sheets, Houghton" and "22-28. A Guess at the Riddle, 1887-88 (General Headnote)". Another volume of the edition (archive.org item writingsofcharle0000peir) also gives "(1887-88)". The talk should say "1887-88 (W6)" and can add "c. 1890 in CP".
- The CP scan is the Belknap offset reprint (1960) of the 1931 first edition, so its text and pagination are the 1931 ones. The archive.org electronic transcription agrees word for word on every locus above.
