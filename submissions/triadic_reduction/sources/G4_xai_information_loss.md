#### The Ontological Transition to Algorithmacy

The evolution of human coordination and shared cognitive work has historically been defined by discontinuous ontological transitions in the mediums through which that work is accomplished. Historically, the capacity for coordinated action was strictly bound to oracy, a competency defined as the communicative facility a face-to-face speaker exercised before a listener present in the exact same temporal and spatial environment[1]. The advent of writing precipitated the first major structural transition in human coordination, introducing literacy. This transition forced humans to occupy a third structural position—that of an author addressing an absent reader through a static document that conveyed meaning without physically acting[1]. Literacy did not merely enhance speech; it demanded a fundamentally new cognitive architecture capable of reading, writing, citing, and negotiating complex institutions built entirely on asynchronous text[1].

Contemporary society is undergoing a second structural rupture of equal magnitude: the transition to algorithmacy[1]. Algorithmacy is defined as the specific communication competency through which a human worker coordinates with another human party not through a neutral, passive channel, but through an algorithmic third party[1]. The platform labor and gig economy literature has increasingly documented a phenomenon known as the "variance puzzle"—a persistent observation wherein equivalently positioned participants interacting with identical algorithmic systems achieve wildly divergent coordination and economic outcomes[1]. This variance cannot be explained by traditional computer-mediated communication (CMC) or human-machine communication (HMC) paradigms, which conceptually trap the worker on one side of a passive medium or model the interaction as a simple sender-receiver dyad[1].

An algorithmic interface is not a more sophisticated conduit for digital writing; it is an active, goal-oriented agent with its own objectives that transforms every input as part of its operation[1]. True algorithmacy, therefore, requires a cognitive recognition of irreducible triadic relations—the human user, the technological artifact, and the embedded intent, values, and constraints of the system's designer. Resolving the variance puzzle reveals that differential algorithmacy fundamentally equates to differential accuracy of human beliefs regarding this triadic structure[4]. Users who understand the algorithm as an active mediator develop robust mental models of the system's operational topology.

Empirical observations of algorithmic platforms confirm that this competency is often acquired implicitly through exposure to mediated coordination, reflective practice, and the interpretation of ambiguous, probabilistic feedback[1]. When transposed to algorithmic systems, this Peircean triad is operationalized through the theoretical framework of Semiotic Engineering. Contrastive justification requires a deep, structural understanding of the counterfactual boundaries and the complex, synergistic dependencies between variables.

To operationalize true algorithmacy, computational systems must physically and mathematically encode Peircean Thirdness. #### Mathematical Formalization: Quantifying Synergy Loss via Partial Information Decomposition (PID)

The cognitive superiority of inherently interpretable topologies over post-hoc XAI attribution maps is not merely a qualitative psychological or ethnographic observation; it can be rigorously mathematically formalized. We can quantify the exact loss of synergistic information ($Syn$) that occurs when a user is forced to rely on a post-hoc XAI map rather than engaging directly with the interpretable architecture by applying the Partial Information Decomposition (PID) framework.

##### The Foundations of PID

Introduced in a seminal 2010 work by Williams and Beer, PID was developed to address a fundamental limitation in classical Shannon information theory: the inability of standard mutual information metrics to separate dependence into distinct, non-overlapping atoms of redundant, unique, and synergistic contributions[26]. Given a set of source variables $S = \{X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\}$ and a target variable $Y$, the classical mutual information $I(S; Y)$ quantifies the total dependence but obfuscates how that information is distributed across the sources[46].

For a simple bivariate source system $S = \{X_1, X_2\}$, PID formally decomposes the joint mutual information as follows[26]:

$$I(X_1, X_2; Y) = Red(X_1, X_2 \to Y) + Unq(X_1 \to Y) + Unq(X_2 \to Y) + Syn(X_1, X_2 \to Y)$$

Where these specific information atoms denote:

- **$Red(X_1, X_2 \to Y)$:** Redundant (or shared) information about the target $Y$ that is independently present in *both* $X_1$ and $X_2$. Observing either variable yields this information[26].

- **$Unq(X_i \to Y)$:** Unique information about $Y$ that is available *only* from observing $X_i$, and cannot be extracted from any other variable[26].

- **$Syn(X_1, X_2 \to Y)$:** Synergistic (or complementary) information about $Y$ that emerges *only* when $X_1$ and $X_2$ are observed jointly. A classic example is an XOR logic gate, where neither input alone provides information about the output, but together they determine it entirely[26].

The full PID framework organizes all possible subsets of sources onto a redundancy lattice of antichains, strictly ordered by set inclusion, which represents a transition from redundancy-dominated interactions at the bottom of the lattice to synergy-dominated interactions at the top[26]. By defining an intersection measure of redundancy $I_{\cap}$, PID uses a recursive Möbius inversion to extract the specific, non-overlapping partial information atoms $\partial I(\alpha)$ for each node $\alpha$ in the lattice[26]:

$$I(A; Y) = \sum_{\alpha \preceq \{A\}} \partial I(\alpha) \quad \text{and} \quad \partial I(\alpha) = I_{\cap}(\alpha) - \sum_{\beta \prec \alpha} \partial I(\beta)$$

Since Williams and Beer's initial $I_{min}$ formulation was critiqued for failing certain desired properties, modern implementations utilize alternative redundancy measures (such as $I_{BROJA}$) for discrete variables[46]. However, to analyze the continuous variables typical of deep learning feature spaces, researchers utilize the Gaussian PID (GPID), which expresses redundant, unique, and synergistic information as closed-form log-determinant expressions over the covariance blocks of the system[55].
