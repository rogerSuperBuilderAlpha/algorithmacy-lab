# Review 4 — Platform studies / algorithmic management lens

**Manuscript:** "When is a combination a configuration? Integrated information and the constitution of
organizational wholes" — submitted to *Organization Theory*, Special Themed Section "Theorizing the
Configurational Nature of Organizational Phenomena" (Catalysing & Crystallizing).

**Reviewer lens:** platform studies and algorithmic management (Kellogg/Valentine/Christin, Rahman,
Cutolo & Kenney, Rochet & Tirole, Vallas & Schor).

---

## VERDICT

**Major revisions.** The within-case criterion and the necessary/contingent distinction are genuinely
useful to platform scholarship, but the manuscript misuses two-sided-market economics (conflating
multihoming with disintermediation, and treating price theory as causal-structure theory), builds its
motivating gap on a partial strawman of platform research, and lets its formalization of power stop one
mechanism short of the literature it cites.

*Register note (per review protocol):* the piece is a dense theory essay in the OT
catalysing-and-crystallizing register — impersonal, claim-first, computed illustrations as microscope
slides. I hold it to that bar. I do not ask for field data; a theory essay in this format owes none. I do
ask that every literature it recruits be used the way its own specialists use it, because a synthesis
essay lives or dies on exactly that.

## Summary of the contribution (in my own words)

The essay imports the partition machinery of integrated information theory — cause-effect structure,
partitions, Φ, the complex — stripped of its consciousness claims, and offers it as a within-case
criterion for when an arrangement of interacting parts is one configuration rather than an aggregate.
Three deliverables follow: a constitution criterion (irreducibility over every partition), a membership
account (the complex, with pivotality grading cores and peripheries), and a dissolution theory (three
operators — substitution, bypass, constraint-lifting — none of which is formation reversed). The third
operator yields the paper's most original move for my field: the distinction between necessary
irreducibility (an intermediary that computes a joint condition the parties cannot reproduce, e.g.,
escrow) and contingent irreducibility (a conduit held in the core only by a maintained constraint, e.g.,
a franchise-law dealer), separable by a single counterfactual: restore the forbidden direct tie,
recompute, see whether the intermediary stays in the core. The paper positions this as complementary to
QCA and as a formal seat for power in configurational theory, with the platform economy as the flagship
application terrain.

Platform scholars should want a version of this paper to exist. The question "is the platform doing
integrating work, or holding a gate?" is our question, and we have answered it with metaphors
(bottleneck, cage, dependence) rather than a test. But the version under review handles our literatures
too loosely to be citable by them yet.

## Major issues

**1. Two-sided-market economics is price theory, and the manuscript uses it as causal-structure theory —
and misdefines multihoming in the process.** Section 6's table assigns platform economics (Rochet &
Tirole, 2003; Armstrong, 2006) the native test "disintermediation / single-homing," and Section 5 claims
"the platform literature's multihoming... ask[s] whether the parties can reach each other around a
controlling intermediary." Neither is right. Rochet and Tirole's contribution is the non-neutrality of
the price *structure* under cross-side externalities; Armstrong's "competitive bottleneck" arises when
one side single-homes and platforms compete on the multihoming side. Multihoming, in that literature,
means a user patronizes *several platforms at once* — it disciplines each platform's prices; it says
nothing about parties reaching each other *directly*. The question the manuscript actually wants is
**disintermediation / leakage**, which has its own literature with results the paper should confront: Gu
and Zhu (2021, *Management Science*) show leakage occurs precisely when accumulated trust makes the
platform's ongoing contribution reproducible — which is the contingent-irreducibility verdict, derived
and *empirically tested* before this paper. Hagiu and Wright (2015, *Management Science*) formalize the
marketplace-versus-reseller choice, which is close kin to the strict/partial mediation contrast in
Section 7. These are the nearest rivals to the paper's flagship distinction, and they are absent.
*Paste-ready fix for Section 5 (replace the multihoming sentence):* "The essential-facilities doctrine
and the platform literature's leakage problem both ask whether the parties can reach each other around a
controlling intermediary — whether matched parties keep transacting through the platform or take the
relationship off-platform once the match is made (Gu & Zhu, 2021; Cutolo & Kenney, 2021). Multihoming
asks a different question, whether a side patronizes several platforms at once and thereby disciplines
each one's prices (Rochet & Tirole, 2003; Armstrong, 2006); it constrains what the gatekeeper can
charge, not whether the gate is needed." Amend the table row accordingly (bypassable route →
"intermediary the matched parties can route around; leakage / disintermediation"), and add Gu & Zhu and
Hagiu & Wright to Section 5's sibling paragraph — with a sentence saying what the Φ counterfactual adds
over the leakage literature's own test (my reading: a unified formalism in which the same operator also
governs membership and dissolution, and a way to distinguish two intermediaries the leakage regression
treats identically).

**2. The "pairwise account" framing of platform scholarship is a partial strawman.** Section 1 claims
platform research explains the composite arrangement "pair by pair" — each clause with a bilateral
rationale — leaving the joint apparatus untheorized. Two objections. First, the definitional core of
two-sided-market economics since Rochet and Tirole (2003) is that the two sides *cannot* be analyzed
bilaterally: cross-side externalities mean the platform's problem never factors into side-by-side
dyads. The founding insight of the field the paper wants to correct is already an irreducibility claim,
in price space. Second, algorithmic-management scholarship treats the control apparatus as a system,
not a stack: Kellogg, Valentine and Christin's (2020) six mechanisms are presented as an architecture
of control; Rahman's (2021) invisible cage is a regime, not a clause; Vallas and Schor (2020) analyze
the platform as a distinctive governance form. What these literatures genuinely lack is a *formal
within-case test* for when the apparatus is one causal whole — which is the paper's actual
contribution, and a sufficient one. Claim that gap; do not manufacture a bigger one. *Paste-ready fix
(Section 1, replacing "and the economics explains them pair by pair... stacked bilateral contracts"):*
"Two-sided-market economics begins from a joint determination — Rochet and Tirole (2003) built the
field on the observation that a platform's two sides cannot be priced separately — but what it fixes
jointly is the price structure, not the causal constitution of the arrangement. Algorithmic-management
scholarship describes the control apparatus as a system (Kellogg, Valentine & Christin, 2020; Rahman,
2021), yet neither literature has a test for when the apparatus is one causal whole rather than a stack
of instruments with a shared owner. That test is what a configurational theory should supply."

**3. The application-layer readability claim gets Kellogg et al. backwards and assumes away the opacity
that defines the terrain.** Section 2: "In platform and algorithmic arrangements the rules are often
readable from the application layer alone... (Kellogg et al., 2020)." The headline finding of that
literature is the opposite: the rules are opaque, dynamic, and experimentally manipulated. Rahman
(2021) — whom the manuscript cites *elsewhere* for exactly this point — shows workers cannot read the
evaluation algorithm and are systematically wrong when they reverse-engineer it; Rosenblat and Stark
(2016) document Uber changing dispatch and pricing logic without notice. Two consequences the paper
must own: (a) the "fixed update rule" modeling commitment is weakest precisely on the terrain claimed
as the liveliest application; a platform that A/B-tests its dispatch is not a fixed transition system.
(b) If determinations are observable but rules are inferred, the encoding sensitivity the paper
concedes (four of ten cases flipped under re-encoding) is not a robustness footnote for the platform
agenda — it is the central obstacle to it. *Paste-ready fix:* "In platform arrangements the committed
determinations are visible at the application layer — the dispatch, the price, the rank — while the
generating rules are opaque, often deliberately so (Rahman, 2021; Kellogg et al., 2020). The model runs
on the committed determinations and the parties' responses to them; where the platform varies its rules
faster than an encoding can be validated, the verdict inherits that uncertainty, and the sensitivity
discipline of section 7 becomes the price of admission rather than an afterthought."

**4. Trivial positivity: every real matching platform reads both sides, so what does the dispatch
verdict discriminate?** The dispatch result turns on one dependency — whether the mediator's commit
reads the unseen third party. But reading both sides is what matching *is*; no deployed dispatch reads
only the driver. If Φ > 0 for essentially the entire class of dispatch platforms, the criterion's
discriminating power on this terrain lives entirely in the analyst's encoding choices, and the design
variance platform researchers actually study — surge pricing, acceptance-rate hostages, rating gates,
assignment versus offer models — is invisible to the binary verdict. The strict/partial mediation
contrast in Section 7 helps (dispatcher-in-core versus matchmaker-outside is a real and recognizable
cut: it is roughly Uber versus Craigslist), but between those poles sits everything interesting. *Fix:*
add a paragraph naming at least one within-class design difference that changes the verdict or relocates
the complex — the assignment-versus-offer distinction is a natural candidate, since an offer model
restores a party's contestability, which the paper's own fifth design move covers — or concede
explicitly that the criterion sorts mediation *forms*, not mediation *designs*, and that the finer
variance is future work.

**5. Power gets one mechanism; the literature has several, and the constraint operator cannot express
some of them.** Contingent irreducibility formalizes power that works by *forbidding the direct tie* —
franchise law, walled gardens, exclusives. That is real and the formalization is valuable. But platform
power in the manuscript's own citations also runs through information asymmetry (Rahman, 2021),
control of visibility and ranking (Curchod et al., 2020), and dependence built from non-portable,
platform-specific reputational capital (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021). Several of these do not forbid the
direct tie at all; they make it *worthless* — a worker's five years of ratings do not travel to the
direct relationship. What is the "constraint" whose lifting the counterfactual computes when the
barrier is data non-portability rather than a forbidden channel? The operator is undefined there, and
the paper should say so. Relatedly, the distinction courts a normative slide: "the necessary
integrators were never threatened" reads as necessary = safe = legitimate, but necessity does not
launder power — an escrow can be irreplaceable and extractive at once. The paper's own best resource
against this slide is buried in Section 4: the owner who sits *outside* the complex while the triad
inside stays irreducible. Constitution and control come apart, and that is a finding platform scholars
would cite — stated, it also disarms the objection that a partial-mediation marketplace "outside the
core" (Section 7) is thereby powerless, when Airbnb-style platforms govern search ranking, pricing
tools, reviews, and dispute resolution from exactly that spectator position. *Paste-ready fix (Section
5 or 7):* "The complex draws the boundary of constitution, not of control. A platform written out of
the core — the partial-mediation marketplace that matches and steps back — can still set the rules,
order the queue, and hold the data in which the parties' reputations live (Curchod et al., 2020; Cutolo
& Kenney, 2021). The constraint operator captures power that works by forbidding the direct tie; power
that works by making the direct tie worthless — non-portable ratings, platform-specific reputational
capital — needs a counterfactual this essay does not supply, and the agenda of section 7 should include
it."

**6. The fifty-one-intermediary catalog and its retrodiction claim overreach for a theory essay.** One
hand deals every card: the author assembles the catalog, encodes the cases, classifies them, and scores
the retrodiction. No coding procedure, no second coder, no case list. And the clean sort has
counterexamples on both sides: travel agents did integrating work (search, advice, ticketing, recourse)
and died anyway; securities exchanges — necessary integrators on the paper's reading — were bypassed at
the margin by dark pools and internalization. "The necessary integrators were never threatened" is a
falsifiable sentence, and false. *Paste-ready fix:* "...the contingent conduits survived as long as
their constraints did; and the necessary integrators proved most durable, because a cheap direct tie
cannot reproduce a joint condition — though durability came in degrees, and exchange-style integrators
met their own bypass at the margin." Also either move the catalog to an appendix with the case list, or
label it explicitly as an illustrative sort with no evidential weight.

## Minor issues

1. **The "transmit / transform / commit ladder" is the authors' construction, not the cited
literatures' native test.** Hancock, Naaman and Levy (2020) define AI-mediated communication and vary
agency over message modification; Kellogg et al. (2020) taxonomize control mechanisms. Neither draws
this ladder. Own it as an adaptation ("adapting Hancock et al....") rather than attributing it.
2. **Missing references, given the terrain claimed:** Vallas and Schor (2020, *Annual Review of
Sociology*) on what platforms are as a governance form; Rosenblat and Stark (2016, *IJoC*) on
information asymmetries in ride-hail; Gu and Zhu (2021) and Hagiu and Wright (2015) per Major issue 1;
Jacobides, Cennamo and Gawer (2018, *SMJ*) — the ecosystem-bottleneck concept is the strategy field's
nearest rival to "necessary irreducibility" and should be cited and distinguished.
3. **"Rating hostage"** is a nice Williamsonian coinage but arrives unglossed in Section 1; one clause
("a rating the worker forfeits on exit, held hostage against defection") would make it land for readers
outside contract economics.
4. **Dependence is heterogeneous within a position.** Schor and colleagues show workers on the same
platform differ sharply in dependence (supplemental versus primary earners); the binary core-membership
verdict flattens this. One sentence acknowledging that the model's "worker" is a role, and that
occupants of the role differ in their outside options, would pre-empt the objection.
5. **Style, register-aware:** the prose is distinctive and mostly earns its density; this is not slop.
But the antithesis construction recurs to the point of metronome — "Connection is not constitution,"
"a partnership, not a takeover," "siblings, not one idea stated twice," "marks traffic; they do not
say," "not of headcount or wiring density" — several per section by Section 6. Keep the two or three
that carry argument (the quorum verdict earns its line) and state the rest positively. Em-dash use is
heavy but mostly load-bearing; a few paired-dash asides in Sections 4–5 would read better as sentences.
The closing epigram ("told from the heaps") earns its place; the section-final epigram habit elsewhere
(nearly every section exits on one) could lose one or two.
6. **Section 7's ride-hail example** would benefit from one named empirical anchor (e.g., Uber's
assignment dispatch versus a job-board's open listing) so platform readers can check the encoding
against a case they know, consistent with the paper's own reproducibility discipline.

## Bottom line

This is the rare formalism import that platform and algorithmic-management scholars could actually use:
the necessary/contingent distinction names something our field has circled for a decade with
"bottleneck," "cage," and "dependence," and the owner-outside-the-complex result — constitution is not
control — is a real conceptual gift if the authors surface it. But the manuscript currently recruits
platform economics for a job it does not do (Major 1), motivates itself against a version of platform
research that stopped in 2002 (Major 2), waves off the opacity that defines algorithmic management
(Major 3), and formalizes only one of platform power's several mechanisms while flirting with the
inference that necessity is legitimacy (Major 5). Every one of these is fixable without touching the
formal core, which I did not find fault with as mathematics. Fix the economics, engage the leakage
literature as the nearest rival, own the limits of the constraint operator, and this becomes a paper I
would assign. As submitted, a platform-economics referee will stop at the multihoming sentence and not
recover.

**Recommendation: major revisions.**
