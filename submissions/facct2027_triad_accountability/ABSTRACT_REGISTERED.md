# Abstract as registered on OpenReview, 26 Oct 2026 (author's wording)

**Title:** Accountability in the Triad: Who Answers When an Algorithm Judges Two People at Once

**TL;DR:** When an algorithm evaluates two interdependent people at once, two-place accountability fails, so platforms need a triadic forum that hears both parties together and separates each one's influence from the algorithm's.

Algorithmic accountability research typically models a two-place relationship, with a decision system on one side and an affected individual on the other. However, many socio-technical systems don't fit this dyadic structure. For example, marketplace rankings, ride-hailing matches, and booking platform review scores mediate interactions between two interdependent parties, evaluating both simultaneously and binding each to the outcome. This arrangement is called a triad, an organizational structure in which conventional accountability models struggle to assign responsibility. According to Bovens' conceptual framework, accountability is a relational mechanism in which an actor must explain and justify conduct to a forum authorized to judge it. In a triad, each party's outcome depends on both the counterpart's behavior and the platform's proprietary rules. The evaluative algorithm often obscures how much each party contributes to the outcome, allowing the platform operator to shift responsibility onto either user. As a result, any accountability forum that considers only one party receives incomplete evidence. By synthesizing empirical findings from online seller ratings, hotel ranking systems, and home-sharing host evaluations, this work specifies the requirements for a triadic forum. Such a forum must adjudicate both parties simultaneously and support three essential competencies for contesting responsibility: interpreting ambiguous algorithmic feedback, specifying communicative intent through the system, and tracking unannounced rule changes. The paper concludes with design and auditing implications, emphasizing that audits should assess whether systems enable joint contestation and whether explanations effectively separate counterpart influence from algorithmic intervention.
