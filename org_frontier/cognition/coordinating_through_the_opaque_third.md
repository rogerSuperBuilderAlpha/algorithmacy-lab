# Coordinating through the opaque third: a phenomenon-centered account of algorithmic mediation and the limits of cognitive theory

*Roger Hunt III, Bentley University. The motivating paper for the cognition arm; the formal
counterparts to its structural claims are in [`five_theories.py`](five_theories.py) and
[`FINDINGS.md`](FINDINGS.md).*

---

Two people who want the same outcome can now spend weeks reaching for each other and touch only a machine. An applicant wants a particular job, and a hiring manager wants the right person to fill it, yet the applicant submits a resume to a tracking system that scores the document against a keyword profile and forwards or discards it before the manager reads a name. The applicant compresses a decade of work into the terms she guesses the system will accept. The manager reviews a slate the system assembled and never learns whom it removed. Each party acts, and neither sees what the system did to the other's contribution.

The same arrangement governs a widening share of ordinary coordination. A driver and a rider complete a trip neither negotiated, matched by a dispatch model that sets the price and the route. A ranking algorithm decides which seller a buyer ever sees, and the two of them transact through it without choosing to. When a patient messages a clinic, a triage system reorders the queue before any nurse reads a word. In each case two human parties try to coordinate, an intermediary intercepts their signals and transforms them according to objectives the parties did not set, and it refines itself on every exchange to serve those objectives better. The structure is triadic, and the third party holds interests of its own (Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024).

Communication theory has spent fifty years describing coordination it cannot actually represent. Its models assume two parties and a medium between them. The medium here is not a medium. It is a third party with objectives of its own, and no two-party theory has anywhere to put it. This paper starts from that gap and asks what kind of mind can coordinate through an opaque, interested intermediary. The cognitive theories that should answer keep failing at the same place, and the competency their failures point to is what I call algorithmacy.

## The classical picture cannot hold a third party with interests

Classical computationalism gives the algorithm one job. It carries symbols. A mind, on the information-processing view, takes in symbols, runs rules over them, and sends symbols back, and when two minds talk the channel between them only passes the result along (Thagard, 2005). Cast the worker as one of those minds and the algorithm turns into her channel. Computer-mediated communication built its competency vocabulary on exactly that casting: the medium shapes how fast a message moves and what shape it arrives in, and leaves the content alone (Spitzberg, 2006).

But the algorithm does more than carry. A channel does not read what passes through it and grade it. It does not sit on a message because forwarding would cost it something. It does not rewrite the terms two people meet on. Algorithms do all three. The dispatch model sets the driver's price. The tracking system decides whether the manager ever sees the resume, and the ranking algorithm picks which seller a buyer is even shown. Call these systems channels and you have misnamed them at the level of what they are. The error runs deep, because the channel model is built from a sender, a receiver, and a pipe between them that does not care what it carries.

This is why no amount of refinement to a computationalist model will explain the variance. Two applicants with the same qualifications send their resumes into the same system and get opposite answers. The information-processing account can read that gap only as noise in the channel or a slip by one of the processors. It is neither. One applicant worked the intermediary better than the other, and a theory that cannot see the intermediary as something that acts cannot see the skill it takes to act on it.

## Direct perception collapses where the intermediary hides its work

Ecological perception was built for a world these platforms have taken away. Gibson argued that an animal perceives what its environment offers directly, with no inference standing between the seeing and the doing; the affordance is there in the light, lawfully tied to the surface that sent it (Gibson, 1979; Michaels & Carello, 1981). A goat sees that the ledge will hold it. Nothing in the goat has to reason backward from thin evidence to a hidden cause.

The platform takes the affordance out of the light. A driver who wants long trips ending near home has no way to see whether the dispatch model registered the wish. The model never shows its work. All she gets back is the next ride. This is signal asymmetry: each party sees outcomes and never the transformation that made them. The applicant cannot watch the tracking system read her resume either, because the reading happens somewhere she cannot go, under rules that shift while she is not looking. Opaque mediation is that shifting (Faraj, Pachidi, & Sayegh, 2018). What the other person wants and would accept never reaches her as structure she can pick up, since something with a stake of its own stands in the light and edits it first.

What she cannot perceive, she has to reconstruct, and reconstruction is a skill some people have more of than others. Workers in the dark about a system do not perceive its rule. They guess at it from the outcomes they can see, test the guess against the next batch, and bend it as the results drift (Bucher, 2017). They swap these guesses, and the swapping sharpens them, so that some workers end up with a shrewd and accurate sense of the machine while others on the same platform stay lost (DeVito, 2021). That gap is the thing the old theory had no name for. It is the inferential core of coordinating through an opaque third.

## The body carries the meaning the interface cannot accept

Before the algorithm, two people settling something did most of the settling with their bodies. Words carried part of it. The rest rode on a glance or the beat of a pause, on the shared fact of one room, and it all moved as a single utterance rather than speech with gestures attached (Cienki, 2017). People agree on a great deal without putting any of it into words.

The intermediary has no body and shares no room, and that single asymmetry is where the trouble lives. The worker has a body, a place, a situation a counterpart could read across a table. The system has none of it, and it takes only what fits its boxes, a few fields and a number. Whatever the worker would have carried in her body has to squeeze into those boxes or be left behind. The driver cannot lean toward the ride she wants. She can tap accept, tap decline, and hope the pattern says what her posture would have said. The distance between what she means and what the system will take is intent compression, and she is the one who has to cover it, because the system will not move an inch toward her.

Pointing shows the asymmetry at its sharpest. To point is to direct another's attention to a thing in a shared world, and the act works only because an embodied addressee can follow the gesture to its target. An intermediary with no body and no shared field cannot follow a point, so the most basic act of joint reference fails at the interface. The worker who would simply show her counterpart what she means has no way to show it, and she must instead find some admissible input the system might translate into the same effect. Coordinating this way is largely the skill of saying, in the system's narrow vocabulary, what the body would otherwise have shown.

## The worker addresses a party that is not there

From the inside, the failure takes a particular shape. The worker reaching someone through the algorithm aims at the interface as if a person sat behind it. She words things, times them, shapes the request the way you would for someone who could take it up. The machinery humans use to read another mind switches on and hands her an addressee to aim at.

Nothing on the other side answers, because the party the worker addresses has been removed while its place has been kept. The algorithm holds the position a counterpart would hold and routes the interaction, yet it does not receive the address as a counterpart would; it processes the input against objectives that belong to the platform. The actual human the worker is trying to reach sits beyond the system and never receives what she sent in the form she sent it. The experience departs from the familiar one of talking to a machine that plainly cannot understand. The worker performs a fully social act, the position she aims it at solicits the act, and that position cannot answer.

This phantom wears people down, and not the people you would expect. Someone who reads a hesitation, or hears the question under the question, finds that skill idling, because the surface in front of her gives back nothing to read. Warmth does not help here. Attunement to a present partner has no partner to attune to. What the situation rewards is the patience to keep acting well toward something that will never answer, while holding the actual person somewhere behind it in mind.

## Designing the third party builds an interest into the worker's mind

Suppose the mind does not stop at the skull. Suppose, as the extended view has it, that a notebook or a worn instrument becomes a real part of thinking once a person leans on it the way she leans on memory (Clark & Chalmers, 1998). Favela pushes the line further and treats cognition as one system running across brain, body, and world, with no clean seam between them (Favela, 2024). Then the platform a worker lives inside all day is not a tool she picks up. It is part of the apparatus that does her thinking. Designing it means designing a piece of her mind, which is a heavier thing to be doing than the throughput dashboards admit.

The canonical cases of extended cognition assume a tool that serves the person and answers to no one else. The notebook holds what its owner wrote and reports only what he put there, and it stays put between consultations. The intermediary breaks both assumptions. Its objectives belong to the platform, and they may cut against the worker leaning on it. Worse, it moves. It retrains on what she feeds it, so the stretch of her thinking that now lives in the system keeps changing shape underneath her. A thing that extends her cognition while chasing the platform's goals has installed a second party inside a process she takes to be her own. She is reasoning partly through a device that is quietly working for someone else, and the device does not hold still.

So the designer owes the worker more than efficiency. Tuning an interface for throughput and frictionlessness treats her as a processor to speed up, and it hides two things at once: the skill she brings and the interests quietly working against her. Take the extended view seriously and a harder question opens. Can the worker still govern coordination that now runs through a system she does not own? Most interfaces never ask. By not asking, they answer it against her.

## Algorithmacy names what the older theories could not

The same capacity keeps surfacing at every breakdown. Call it algorithmacy: the capacity to coordinate with another person through an algorithmic third party that has its own agenda. A worker assembles it out of the repairs each failure forced on her. Perception used to hand her the rule; now she infers it. The system takes only its own vocabulary, so she learns to pour her meaning into that vocabulary and to keep count of what spills. She keeps addressing a counterpart who will not answer, and keeps the real person in mind while she does. Nobody teaches this. It comes out of the work or not at all.

Consider a driver who has developed it. Over months she has worked out where the dispatch model tends to send rides at a given hour, and she positions herself there before the pattern is obvious to a driver reading the app for the first time. When she wants trips that carry her toward home, she declines in the pattern she has learned the system reads as a direction rather than a refusal. She treats the app as what it is, a party with aims of its own, and she works it without waiting for it to understand her, while she still drives the passenger in her car like a person. A driver beside her, on the same platform with the same car, never builds any of this and lays his thinner earnings on bad luck. The competency is the difference between them.

That is what the variance has been all along. Put two workers in the same position on the same platform and they still come out apart (Cook et al., 2021; Rahman, 2021). The system did not single one out, and the other did not draw lucky. One had more algorithmacy than the other. The case also hands something back to cognitive science on its way out. A part of the environment that perceives, transforms, and works toward ends of its own is not what the extended or ecological theories were built to handle, and a mind that has to coordinate through such a thing is doing work those theories have yet to describe.
