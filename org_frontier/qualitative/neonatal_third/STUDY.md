# The Third in the Room: How Neonatal Nurses Coordinate Care Between Parents and an Infant Who Cannot

*Proposal draft — front end through Method, with draft interview guide.*

---

**Abstract (99 words)**

Coordination is increasingly accomplished through a third party that stands between two others, and the case that most demands explanation, coordination through an algorithmic intermediary, resists direct study because the intermediary is opaque. This study turns to a naturally occurring triad whose third party is fully observable: the neonatal nurse who coordinates an infant's care between the infant and its parents. The infant cannot participate in organizing its own care, which sets this triad apart from the ones where the parties could coordinate without the third. Through interviews and observation, the study explores how nurses accomplish this work.

---

**Problem statement and research question**

Coordination between two parties is often routed through a third, and how that third shapes what the two can accomplish together is an old question in social theory and a pressing one wherever an algorithm now occupies the middle position. A worker and a customer coordinate through a platform; an applicant and a hiring manager coordinate through a screening system; two colleagues coordinate through a recommendation engine. In each case a third interprets, ranks, or commits something the two parties must then act on. The difficulty for anyone who wants to understand what such a third does is that its most consequential version, the algorithmic intermediary, cannot be watched at work. Its operations are proprietary, its reasoning hidden, and the people coordinating through it can report only what they infer from its outputs. The structure that most needs explaining is the one least available to direct observation.

Researchers have met this difficulty mainly by finding ways to study the algorithm itself despite its opacity, enrolling it in fieldwork through comparison across systems and triangulation against the traces it leaves (Christin, 2020). This study takes a different route. Rather than pursue the opaque third directly, it looks for a triad in which the third party is a person whose work can be observed from the inside, and asks what that person does in the position a machine would otherwise occupy. The wager is that a transparent third and an opaque one can share a structural position closely enough that the first illuminates the second, a wager the study advances cautiously and does not assume.

Most candidate triads will not serve, because the third in them turns out to be removable. A husband and wife can organize the husband's care between themselves, drawing on what each knows of the other's wishes, even when illness temporarily incapacitates one of them, because there is a coordinating relationship to fall back on. The nurse or case manager who helps them is useful but not necessary to the coordination itself; remove the third and a workable pair remains. The same holds for the platform cases. The applicant and the hiring manager will eventually meet and coordinate directly, and the screening system that stands between them for now is an obstruction to be gotten past, not a permanent condition of their contact. In each of these the third is provisional, and the relationship it stands within has an existence apart from it.

The neonatal intensive care unit may present a case where this is not so. An infant in intensive care cannot participate in organizing its own care under any circumstances. It cannot report a symptom, weigh an option, consent to a procedure, or direct anyone to act on its behalf, and a parent cannot supply the infant's wishes the way a spouse can act on a partner's known preferences, because the infant has formed none. The parent therefore has no way to coordinate the infant's care with the infant. Whatever coordination of that care occurs runs through someone who can render the infant's condition into terms the parent can act on, and the nurse at the bedside is ordinarily that someone. Whether this makes the nurse a third that cannot be removed, rather than a helpful third who could in principle be bypassed, is an open question, and it is the question this study is built to explore.

This is a question about practices, and the study pursues it as one. It does not set out to prove that the neonatal triad is irreducible or that the nurse constitutes anything. It sets out to observe and describe what nurses do when they occupy this position, and to generate material from which the larger question, what a third contributes to coordination when the parties cannot coordinate without it, can be more sharply posed.

**Research question.** How do neonatal nurses coordinate the care of an infant between the infant and its parents, given that the infant cannot participate in organizing its own care? Two lines of inquiry follow. First, what practices make up this work: how does the nurse render the infant's condition into something the parent can act on, and how does the nurse move among the infant, the parents, and the rest of the clinical team? Second, how do nurses understand their own position in this arrangement: do they experience themselves as relaying between a parent and infant who would otherwise connect, or as the standing condition through which a parent reaches an infant at all, and what in their account distinguishes the two?

---

**Expected contributions**

The study is exploratory and is designed to produce grounded description rather than to settle a theoretical claim, so its expected contributions are framed accordingly.

To theory, the study offers a worked empirical case for an old and newly urgent question: what a third party does for coordination when the two parties it stands between cannot coordinate without it. Social theory has a rich vocabulary for the third who profits from the division of two others, the third who reconciles them, and the third who brokers a connection they could not make alone, but each of these presumes two parties capable of relating directly, with the third as an addition to a relationship that could exist without it. The neonatal case may not fit that template, because one of the two parties cannot relate in the relevant sense at all. By describing in detail how a nurse works this position, the study aims to give later theorizing a concrete instance to reason from, and to clarify whether the distinction between a third that adds to a workable pair and a third without which there is no pair marks a difference worth building theory around. The study expects to sharpen the question, not to answer it.

The contribution connects to a broader interest in coordination through algorithmic intermediaries. If the practices by which an observable human third holds together a coordination the parties cannot accomplish alone can be described closely, they offer a point of comparison for the opaque case, where the third's work cannot be watched directly. The study does not claim that the nurse and the algorithm are the same kind of third, and establishing whether the comparison holds is itself a further question the data might inform. The aim here is the more modest one of producing a careful account of one observable triad, so that the general structure has at least one case examined from the inside.

To practice, a close description of how nurses do this coordination has value for neonatal care independent of the theoretical interest. The work by which a nurse renders an infant's condition into terms a frightened parent can act on is largely undescribed as a skilled practice, and naming it could inform how the work is taught, supported, and recognized. The findings may also bear on family-centered care, which asks nurses to involve parents in coordination without having articulated what that coordination requires of the nurse when the patient is an infant who cannot take part in it.

**Literature review**

Four conversations bear on this study, and reading them together locates what it can add. Work on studying algorithmic systems establishes that the opaque third resists direct observation and has developed ways to study it anyway, but has not tried substituting an observable human third for it. Social theory on the third party establishes that a third changes a relationship but assumes two parties able to relate. The neonatal nursing literature has described the nurse's work of linking parents and infants in detail, but has framed it as emotional bonding rather than as coordination. Research on coordination and articulation work has analyzed how parties accomplish joint work through intermediaries and artifacts, but has not taken up the case where one party cannot participate at all. None has examined the nurse as a third party in the coordination of a patient who cannot coordinate, which is the opening this study works in.

*Studying the opaque third, and the case for a transparent one.* The interpretation, ranking, and commitment that algorithmic systems perform have become a central concern for organization theory, which has mapped how such systems direct and evaluate the people who work through them while keeping their operations out of view (Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020). The methodological problem this poses, that the algorithm cannot be observed deciding, has drawn careful responses, most fully the argument that ethnographers can still study algorithms by comparing systems against one another and triangulating the traces they leave in the practices of the people around them (Christin, 2020). These responses share a commitment to studying the opaque third itself, working around its opacity rather than going elsewhere. The present study departs from that commitment. It treats the opacity as a reason to study a different instance of the same structural position, one occupied by a person whose work can be observed directly, on the wager that what the observable third does can inform an account of what the opaque one does. That a human intermediary and an algorithmic one can occupy comparable structural positions has been argued in work treating the algorithm as a co-worker that fills a role once held by a person (Tarafdar, Page, & Marabelli, 2023) and in accounts of the human labor that sits where automation is presumed to be (Faraj, Pachidi, & Sayegh, 2018). What has not been done is to turn that equivalence into a research strategy, choosing a transparent third precisely because its opaque counterpart cannot be watched. The study advances this move carefully, since it rests on a structural likeness between the two thirds that the comparison itself would have to bear out.

*The third party in social theory.* The claim that a relationship among three differs in kind from a relationship between two is Simmel's (1950), who argued that adding a third to a pair produces a structure that cannot be reduced to its component pairs, and that the third can take distinct positions within it, profiting from the other two's division, dividing them deliberately, or mediating between them. Later work on brokerage kept the structural insight while turning to the third who connects parties rather than dividing them, the broker who joins those who would otherwise stay apart (Obstfeld, 2005). The sociology of service work carried the triadic frame into the workplace, analyzing the worker positioned among an employer and a customer whose demands pull against each other (Korczynski, 2009; Bélanger & Edwards, 2013), and recent work has made the Simmelian basis explicit, reading the service triangle as a Simmelian triad in which the customer is positioned as a scapegoat while the employer benefits without acting (Laemmli, 2023). Across these accounts the third is an addition to a relationship between two parties who can relate to each other directly, and the analytic interest lies in how the third tilts, exploits, or repairs a relationship that has an existence apart from it. The deeper resource for a third of another kind is relational sociology, which takes relations rather than pre-given entities as primary and holds that parties acquire their standing from the relations into which they are bound (Emirbayer, 1997). That move makes it possible to ask whether a third might constitute a relationship rather than merely mediate one, but the case in which one of the two parties cannot hold up its end of any relationship, so that there is no pair for the third to add to, is not one this tradition has taken up. It is the case the neonatal unit may present.

*The neonatal nurse as a link between parent and infant.* A substantial qualitative literature describes the neonatal nurse's work of connecting parents to their hospitalized infants, and it has documented that work closely. Nurses describe themselves as bridging separated mothers and infants (You & Kim, 2020), and the nurse's role is analyzed in terms of promoting the parent's closeness to or separation from the infant (Feeley, Genest, Niela-Vilén, Charbonneau, & Axelin, 2016), a framing parents themselves echo when they account for their own experience of closeness and distance in the unit (Treherne, Feeley, Charbonneau, & Axelin, 2017). This research establishes that the nurse's linking work is real, demanding, and consequential for families, and it supplies the present study with a detailed picture of what nurses do at the bedside. It frames that work, however, as emotional and relational, the fostering of a bond, and analyzes it through the closeness of parent to infant rather than the organization of the infant's care. The distinction matters here, because attachment and coordination are not the same relationship. An infant and a parent can form an emotional bond through gaze, touch, and proximity, and this literature establishes that they do; but forming a bond is not organizing the infant's care, and an infant who returns a parent's gaze is not thereby a party to decisions about its treatment. The existing literature's image of the nurse as a bridge belongs to the domain of bonding, where there is an emotional relationship for the nurse to support. Whether the same image holds in the domain of care coordination, where the infant cannot participate at all, is a different question, and it is the one this study takes up. The bonding literature has examined the nurse as a bridge to a relationship; this study examines the nurse as a possible condition of one.

*Coordination and articulation work.* How parties accomplish joint work through intermediaries, artifacts, and one another's awareness has been studied closely in the sociology of work and in computer-supported cooperative work. Strauss (1988) named the articulation work by which interdependent tasks are meshed into a functioning whole, the often invisible labor of arranging, aligning, and working things out that makes visible production possible, and later work developed the analysis of such labor as routinely unrecognized (Star & Strauss, 1999). The computer-supported cooperative work tradition extended this to coordination through shared information spaces, showing that interdependent actors can coordinate by way of a common artifact without direct communication between them (Schmidt & Bannon, 1992), and the broader theory of coordination framed the task as the management of dependencies among activities (Malone & Crowston, 1994). Within healthcare specifically, the patient has been theorized as moving between a thing coordinated around, represented in records and handed between clinicians, and an active participant in their own coordination, a distinction drawn as the difference between a boundary object and a boundary subject (Bishop & Waring, 2019). This body of work supplies the present study with its central concept, coordination as accomplished practice rather than given fact, and with the recognition that much of the work of coordinating is invisible and goes unnamed. What it has studied, though, is coordination among parties who can each take part, or a patient who can move from object to subject as their condition allows. The neonate is the case that can only ever be a boundary object, never a boundary subject, because it cannot participate in its own coordination under any condition, and rendering such a party so that others can coordinate around it is part of what the neonatal nurse appears to do.

*The opening.* The four conversations leave a specific gap. Research on the opaque algorithmic third works around its opacity rather than substituting a transparent third for it. Social theory on the third assumes two relatable parties. The neonatal nursing literature describes the nurse's linking work but frames it as bonding rather than coordination. The coordination literature analyzes joint work among parties who can each contribute, or a patient who can become a participant, but not the case where one party never can. What none has examined is the nurse as a third party in the coordination of a patient who cannot coordinate, the practices by which a nurse renders a non-participating infant's condition into terms a parent can act on and holds the three-cornered arrangement together. This study enters that opening not to settle whether the neonatal triad is irreducible, but to describe how the nurse does this work and to generate, from a transparent and observable case, material for thinking about what a third party contributes to coordination when the parties cannot coordinate without it, a question that matters well beyond the nursery and most of all where the third is a machine no one can watch.

**Method**

This is a qualitative, interpretive study designed to build theory from what neonatal nurses do and say about coordinating an infant's care between the infant and its parents. Because the study brings a prior distinction to the field, between a third party that adds to a relationship the two parties could sustain without it and a third without which there is no such relationship, it follows an abductive logic rather than a purely inductive one. It does not test that distinction as a hypothesis, and it does not treat it as a category the data must confirm. The distinction works as a sensitizing concept (Blumer, 1954), a lens that orients attention toward how the nurse stands between parent and infant without dictating what the analysis will find there. The substantive account of the nurse's coordination practices is left to emerge from the nurses' own accounts and observed work. The analytic procedures follow the conventions of the Gioia methodology (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013), which is suited to a phenomenon that is processual, meaning-laden, and not yet captured by an existing construct.

*Site selection.* The study will be conducted in the neonatal intensive care units of one or two hospitals in a single metropolitan area. The setting is chosen because it presents the structural condition the study is built to examine in a form no other accessible site offers. The patient cannot participate in coordinating its own care under any circumstances, so the question of how a third party holds together a coordination the two parties cannot accomplish alone is forced into the open rather than absorbed by direct contact between them. The neonatal unit also makes the work observable. The nurse's coordination happens at the bedside and in the unit, in conversation with parents, in handoffs, and at the monitor, where it can be watched and asked about, which is precisely what the opaque algorithmic version of this structural position does not allow. Confining the study to one or two units in a single metropolitan area keeps the regulatory environment, the technology, and the staffing model comparable across participants, so that variation in how nurses do the work is not confounded with variation in the institutions they work in. Where two units are used, the contrast between them, for instance between a unit caring for the most premature infants and one caring for infants closer to term, offers a way to observe whether the nurse's position shifts as the infant's capacity to register a parent's presence changes, without making that contrast the spine of the design.

*Sources of data.* The study draws on three sources, with interviews as the primary source and observation and documents providing triangulation. The first is semi-structured interviews with 20 to 25 neonatal nurses, recruited through unit nurse managers and professional networks and sampled for variation in experience, shift, and the acuity of the infants they typically care for. Interviews will run 60 to 90 minutes, be audio-recorded with consent, and be transcribed verbatim. Consistent with an abductive design, the interview guide will evolve as early analysis surfaces new lines of inquiry, and sampling will continue until additional interviews stop yielding new second-order themes. A draft guide appears in the Exhibit. The second source is observation, in the form of bedside and unit observation during shifts, recording in field notes how nurses read the infant's condition, render it for parents, move between parents and the rest of the team, and respond to changes. The observer will take brief jottings during observation and expand them into full descriptive and reflective notes immediately afterward, and will occupy an observer role that does not involve participating in care. Observation makes it possible to see practices nurses may not articulate and to check what they describe against what they do. The third source is documentary material, including the unit's standard charting and handoff tools and parent-facing materials, used to corroborate how the infant's condition is rendered into the official, shared form that parents and the team act on. Because the setting involves a vulnerable patient population and families under stress, the study will proceed only under institutional review board approval, will obtain informed consent from participating nurses, and will obtain consent from any parents present during observation or exclude them from the field of observation, with a protocol for suspending observation at the bedside whenever it would intrude on care or on a family in distress.

*Procedures.* Analysis will proceed abductively, cycling between the data and the sensitizing distinction, and will be organized as a Gioia-style data structure (Gioia et al., 2013). Open coding will build first-order categories in the nurses' own terms, staying close to the language they use for what they do, for instance how they describe explaining a reading to a parent or deciding what a parent needs to know. Constant comparison will group these into second-order themes that name the practices analytically, and the themes will then be examined for the aggregate dimensions they compose. The dimensions will be allowed to take whatever shape the data support; the sensitizing distinction orients where to look, at the nurse's position between parent and infant, but does not supply the dimensions in advance, and a finding that the nurse functions as a relay between a parent and infant who coordinate in some fashion of their own would be as analyzable as a finding that the nurse constitutes the only link between them. Where nurses describe the same situation differently, one treating the parent and infant as a pair she supports, another treating the infant's state as something she alone renders, the disagreement will be retained and analyzed as data about how nurses understand their position, rather than resolved into a single account. The product will be a data structure showing the path from the nurses' language to the analytic themes, supported by a table of representative quotations for each theme so that a reader can audit the move from evidence to claim (Pratt, 2009).

*Trustworthiness.* The study will pursue the four criteria of trustworthiness (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). For credibility, it will triangulate interviews against observation and documents, return emerging themes to a subset of participating nurses for correction through member checks, and engage the units over a sustained period. For transferability, it will provide thick description of the units, the work, and the metropolitan context, so that readers can judge where the findings travel. For dependability, it will commit the interview guide and the initial coding scheme to a dated record before fieldwork begins and maintain an audit trail of analytic decisions, so that the path from question to claim is inspectable and the claims are visibly not retrofitted to the data. For confirmability, the data structure and the quotation table will tie each claim to evidence rather than to prior expectation. Throughout, the researcher will keep a reflexive memo on a particular risk specific to this study, that the sensitizing distinction could lead the analysis to see a constitutive third where the nurses themselves experience something more ordinary, and the memo will track instances that cut against the distinction as well as instances that fit it, so that the lens is held open rather than confirmed.

**Exhibit A — Draft interview guide**

*Opening (rapport, context)*

1. Walk me through a typical shift, from when you arrive to when you hand off. What does your day actually involve?
2. How did you come to work in the NICU? How did you learn to do this work well?

*The infant's condition and how it is known*

3. When you start a shift with a baby you do not know yet, how do you figure out how that baby is doing? What do you look at, and what tells you the most?
4. Tell me about a recent time when a baby's condition changed during your shift. How did you notice, and what did you do next?
5. Some of what you know about a baby comes from the monitors and the chart, and some comes from other places. Walk me through where it comes from on a given shift.

*Working with parents*

6. Tell me about a time you explained a baby's condition to a parent. What did you say, and how did you decide what to tell them and how to tell it?
7. When a parent looks at their baby and the monitors, what do they tend to understand on their own, and what do they tend to need you for?
8. Tell me about a time a parent saw the same thing you saw, a number, an alarm, the baby's color, and made something different of it than you did. What happened?
9. What can a parent do for their baby's care on their own, and what can they only do through you or the team?

*The nurse's position*

10. When a decision gets made about a baby's care, walk me through how it actually happens. Who is involved, and where do you sit in that?
11. Some people might say a nurse passes information between the parents and the baby. Others might say it is more than that. From your own experience, how would you describe what you do between a parent and their baby?
12. Tell me about a time you felt you were the only one who could tell what a baby needed. And a time you felt the parent knew something about their baby that you did not.

*Contrast and closing*

13. How is coordinating care for a baby who cannot tell you anything different from caring for an older patient who can? What changes for you?
14. What do you wish people understood about the skill this work takes?
15. Is there anything I should have asked but did not?
