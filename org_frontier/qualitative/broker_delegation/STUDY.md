# Whose Reach Is It: When a Worker Goes to Market Through an Agent, Who Becomes the Central Party

*Proposal draft — front end through Method, with draft interview guide.*

---

**Abstract (96 words)**

A worker who reaches a market through an agent hands the work of dealing to a party who stands between the worker and the platform. A formal prior says the agent then becomes the central, bound party the platform and counterpart deal with, and the worker moves to the edge. This study takes that prior to the field. It studies actors and their agents, authors and their literary agents, sellers and their brokers, creators and their managers, asking who the far side actually deals with and whether the worker remains pivotal or becomes a party the system can route around its representative.

---

**Problem statement and research question**

A worker who wants to reach a market often reaches it through someone else. An actor books work through an agent. A novelist sells a manuscript through a literary agent. A homeowner lists through a real-estate broker. A creator signs deals through a manager. In each case a fourth structure stands behind the obvious three: a worker, an agent who acts for the worker, a platform or market the agent works through, and a counterpart on the far side, with each adjacent pair reading and responding to one another. The worker reaches the platform only through the agent. The question this raises is plain to ask and hard to answer: once the agent is in place, who is the central party the platform and the counterpart deal with, and what becomes of the worker's standing in the arrangement.

A formal model in this project's catalog gives the question a sharp prior. The delegation thread builds a four-party chain, worker to agent to platform to counterpart, assigns each party a rule, and measures how often each sits in the irreducible core of the coordination. The agent sits in that core nearly twice as often as the worker it acts for. The worker, having handed its dealings to the agent, is the least central of the four. Delegation moves standing to the party that stands between the worker and the platform (delegation thread, this project). The expectation that follows is direct. The agent becomes the central party the platform and the counterpart deal with. The worker's reach now runs through the agent, and the worker holds a smaller place in the arrangement than the agent does.

The prior carries an intuition many people in these markets would recognize. The agent has the relationships, fields the calls, and is the name the buyer knows. A casting director deals with agents all day and meets the actor at the audition. An acquiring editor negotiates with the literary agent and may never speak to the author until the deal is done. If the prior holds, the worker is a name the agent carries to market, and the agent is the party the market engages.

The prior could also fail, and how it fails is the contribution. Two departures are worth naming in advance. In the first, the worker stays pivotal and the agent is a conduit the worker can route around. The agent carries messages and opens doors, but the platform and the counterpart understand themselves to be dealing with the worker, the worker can and sometimes does reach the platform directly, and the worker sustains the agent's position rather than ceding standing to it. A bestselling novelist whose name sells the book, who could place the next manuscript through any agent or none, fits this shape. In the second, the platform deals with the worker directly and the agent is a spectator, wired into the arrangement but idle in the coordination that matters, present at the edges of a deal the principals make between themselves. A studio that wants one specific star and negotiates around an agent who only processes the paperwork fits this shape. Each departure is a different answer to who is bound into the core, and the field is where the answer is settled.

This is a question about practice, and the study pursues it as one. It sets out to observe who the parties on each side actually deal with, how the worker's reach is held or routed, and where the people in these markets locate the central party, and to report whether what they show departs from the prior and in which direction.

**Research question.** When a worker reaches a market through an agent, is the worker or the agent the central party the platform and the counterpart deal with, and what holds the worker's standing in place or moves it to the agent? Two lines follow. First, whose dealings are which: who initiates, who decides, who the far side names as its counterpart, and whether the worker can reach the platform around the agent. Second, how the parties understand the position: does the agent experience itself as the party the market engages or as a conduit for a worker who remains the principal, and what in the worker's account marks an agent the worker routes through from one the worker has become subordinate to.

---

**Expected contributions**

The study is exploratory and built to produce grounded description, and its contributions are framed to a pre-registered prior that the fieldwork can confirm or overturn.

To theory, the study tests a formal prediction about delegation against observed practice. The prior says that going to market through an agent cedes the worker's centrality to the agent. Confirming it in the field would give a structural claim an empirical case and would specify the practices through which the cession happens: how the platform comes to deal with the agent, how the worker's direct reach atrophies, how the agent's relationships become the channel the worker depends on. Overturning it would be the larger result. A worker who stays pivotal while routing through an agent, or a platform that deals with the worker while the agent watches, would mark a boundary on the delegation prior and would say where the formal model's assumption breaks. The model assumes the worker reaches the platform only through the agent. Markets where the worker can route around the agent violate that assumption, and finding where they do, and what makes the difference, is the contribution the prior invites.

To the brokerage conversation in social theory, the study brings a distinction the network literature has tended to hold apart from the question of standing. Brokerage describes a party positioned between others who would otherwise be unconnected. Whether occupying that position makes the broker the central party the two sides deal with, or leaves the broker a conduit the connected parties sustain, is a question about who is bound into the coordination, and it is the question the worker-agent market poses in a clean form. The study offers a set of cases where the broker's position and the broker's standing can be observed coming apart or staying joined.

To practice, a close account of who deals with whom in these markets bears on how workers and agents structure their relationships. Workers in agent-mediated markets manage a real risk, that the representative who extends their reach also becomes the party the market knows, leaving the worker dependent on an agent the worker can no longer do without. Describing when that happens and when it does not could inform how workers choose, hold, and leave agents, and how agents understand the standing they take on when they act for someone.

**Literature review**

Four conversations bear on this study. Network theory on brokerage describes the party between others but leaves open whether the broker captures the standing of the parties it connects. Work on delegation and agency models the worker who hires a representative but frames the problem as incentive alignment, not as a question of who becomes central. The literature on cultural intermediaries and talent agents has watched agents do this work in detail and has read it mostly as taste-making and gatekeeping. Classical social theory on the third party supplies the oldest version of the question, who holds the standing when coordination runs through a third, and the sharpest terms for asking it. Reading them together locates what the study adds: a field test of whether the agent's position becomes the worker's place.

*Brokerage and the standing of the broker.* The structural account of brokerage begins with the claim that a party who spans a gap between otherwise disconnected others holds an advantaged position. Burt argues that structural holes, the gaps between parties who do not deal with one another directly, are a source of advantage for the party that bridges them, who controls the flow across the gap and profits from it (Burt, 1992), and his later work sets brokerage against closure, the bridging of gaps against the building of dense, redundant ties, as alternative sources of value (Burt, 2005). Obstfeld distinguishes the broker who keeps two parties apart and profits from the separation, the *tertius gaudens*, from the broker who joins them, the *tertius iungens*, oriented to bring disconnected others together (Obstfeld, 2005). The formal groundwork sorts kinds of brokerage by the group memberships of the parties a broker connects, naming the coordinator, the gatekeeper, the representative, and others as distinct positions (Marsden, 1982; Gould & Fernandez, 1989). The recent synthesis marks a tension the field has carried throughout: brokerage is at once a source of advantage and a position of strain, valuable to occupy and hard to hold, and the broker's standing depends on keeping the bridged parties from connecting directly (Stovel & Shaw, 2012). This conversation supplies the study with its central object and a vocabulary for the positions a broker can occupy. It leaves open the question this study presses. The literature establishes that the broker's position is advantageous; it is less settled on whether occupying that position makes the broker the party the bridged others deal with, so that the worker loses standing to the agent that brokers its reach, or whether the worker remains the principal the broker serves. The agent-mediated market lets the two outcomes be told apart by watching who the far side names as its counterpart and whether the worker can reach across the broker.

*Delegation and agency.* Reaching a market through a representative is an act of delegation, and a large literature models what follows. Principal-agent theory frames the relationship as one in which a principal hires an agent to act on its behalf and must contend with the agent's divergent incentives and private information (Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Eisenhardt, 1989). Relational-contracts theory shifts attention from formal incentives to the self-enforcing agreements that govern ongoing relationships, the understandings parties sustain because the relationship's future value makes betrayal costly (Baker, Gibbons, & Murphy, 2002), and it speaks to how worker and agent hold a relationship together over time. The agency frame asks whether the agent acts in the principal's interest. The question here is upstream of that one. Before asking whether the agent serves the worker well, the study asks who the market deals with once the agent is in place, and whether the worker remains the principal in any sense the platform and the counterpart recognize. An agent can be a faithful representative and still become the party the market engages, so that the worker's faithful agent is also the worker's replacement at the center of the arrangement. Agency theory holds the principal's centrality fixed and studies the agent's loyalty. This study treats the principal's centrality as the thing in question.

*Cultural intermediaries and talent agents.* A close empirical literature has watched agents do exactly the work this study examines, in the industries where agents are most visible. Bielby and Bielby's study of Hollywood talent agencies shows the agent managing uncertainty in a market where quality is hard to judge in advance, packaging clients and brokering their access to work, with the agency's own standing shaping which clients reach which projects (Bielby & Bielby, 1999). Zafirau's ethnographic account of Hollywood agents and managers describes the reputation work agents do, building the perceived value of the people they represent and turning their own relationships into the channel through which a client's career moves (Zafirau, 2008). The broader cultural-intermediaries literature, descending from Bourdieu, frames such figures as taste-makers who shape what reaches a market and how it is valued (Negus, 2002). A growing literature on the creator economy extends the analysis to platform-native work, describing the managers, multi-channel networks, and representatives who have grown up around creators, and how representation reshapes a creator's relationship to the platform (Cunningham & Craig, 2019). This conversation supplies rich description of what agents do and a clear sense that agents shape access, value, and reach. It has mostly read that work through gatekeeping and taste, asking what gets through and who decides. The structural question, whether the agent's gatekeeping makes the agent the central party in a deal or leaves the agent a conduit the principal works through, is the angle the present study takes up. The talent-agency literature shows the agent making markets; this study asks whether, in making the market for a worker, the agent takes the worker's place in it.

*The third party in classical social theory.* The oldest version of the question is Simmel's. A relationship among three differs in kind from a relationship between two, and Simmel named the positions a third can occupy within a triad, among them the *tertius gaudens*, who profits from the division of the other two, and the mediator, who stands between two parties and reconciles them (Simmel, 1950). The *tertius gaudens* is the direct ancestor of the brokerage literature's profiting broker, the mediator of its connecting one. Simmel's deeper point is that the third's standing depends on the relationship between the other two: the third who profits does so by keeping the two apart, and a third who brought them into direct, settled contact would dissolve its own position. The agent-mediated market puts this dynamic in motion. An agent whose two sides, the worker and the platform, come to deal directly has brokered itself out of the central position, while an agent who keeps them apart, who remains the channel through which each reaches the other, holds the position the worker hired it to occupy and may hold the standing the worker meant to keep. Simmel gives the study its sharpest terms for the departure it watches for: whether the worker and the platform deal directly across the agent, making the agent a mediator the principals sustain, or whether the agent keeps itself between them, making the agent the third whose position is its standing.

*The opening.* The four conversations leave a specific gap. The brokerage literature establishes that the broker's position is advantageous but is unsettled on whether the position becomes the broker's standing at the principal's expense. Agency theory holds the principal central and studies the agent's loyalty. The talent-agency literature watches agents make markets but reads the work as gatekeeping. Classical theory supplies the terms for asking whose standing it is but not a contemporary field case. What none has done is take a formal prediction, that delegation moves the worker's centrality to the agent, to a set of agent-mediated markets and ask who the parties on each side actually deal with. This study enters that opening with a pre-registered prior and a willingness to be wrong about it, to describe how the worker's reach is held or routed and to report whether the central party in these arrangements is the worker or the agent who stands between the worker and the market.

**Method**

This is a qualitative, interpretive study designed to test a pre-registered prior against what the parties in agent-mediated markets do and say, and to characterize any departure from it. Because the study carries a sharp expectation to the field, that the agent becomes the central party and the worker moves to the edge, it follows an abductive logic. The prior works as a sensitizing concept (Blumer, 1954), orienting attention toward who deals with whom and toward the worker's capacity to reach the platform around the agent, without dictating the finding. The prior is held open. The design commits in advance to what would count as departing from it, a worker who stays pivotal while the agent serves as a conduit, or a platform that deals with the worker while the agent spectates, and treats either departure as the result of interest rather than as noise. The analytic procedures follow the Gioia methodology (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013), suited to a phenomenon that is processual, relational, and not captured by an existing construct.

*Site selection.* The study will examine three or four agent-mediated markets chosen to vary the worker's outside option while holding the four-party structure constant. Candidate markets are literary publishing, where authors reach editors through literary agents; residential real estate, where sellers reach buyers through listing brokers; talent representation in screen or music work, where performers reach producers through agents; and creator-economy management, where creators reach brand and platform deals through managers. Each market has the structure the prior models: a worker, an agent who acts for the worker, a platform or market on the far side, and a counterpart. The markets differ in the feature the prior's assumption turns on, whether the worker can reach the platform around the agent. A first-time novelist may have no path to an editor except through an agent, while a bestselling author can place a book through any agent or directly. A creator with a large following can be approached by a brand without a manager, while an unknown creator depends on the manager's relationships. Sampling across this range lets the study observe whether the worker's standing tracks its outside option, which is where the prior's assumption that the worker reaches the platform only through the agent is strong and where it is weak. Confining each market to a single national context keeps the legal and commercial environment comparable within it.

*Sources of data.* The study draws on three sources, with interviews primary and observation and documents providing triangulation. The first is semi-structured interviews with parties in each market: workers, agents, and where access allows, parties on the platform or counterpart side, the editors, producers, buyers, and brand representatives who deal with the agents. Interviewing both sides of the same kind of deal is central, because the prior is a claim about who the far side deals with, and the far side's own account is the test. The study targets 30 to 40 interviews across the markets, sampled for variation in the worker's standing, from newcomers to established names, since the prior may hold for one and break for the other. Interviews will run 60 to 90 minutes, be recorded with consent, and be transcribed verbatim. The guide will evolve as early analysis surfaces new lines, and sampling will continue until new interviews stop yielding new second-order themes. A draft guide appears in the Exhibit. The second source is observation, where access permits: an agent's calls and meetings, a negotiation, an open house, a deal as it moves, recorded in field notes that track who initiates, who the parties address, and whether the worker appears in the dealing or stands behind the agent. The third source is documentary material: representation agreements, deal memos, correspondence chains, and the platform-facing materials that show whose name a deal is made in. Correspondence is especially direct evidence for the prior, since the addressee of a deal's emails is a record of who the far side deals with. The study will proceed under institutional review board approval, obtain informed consent, and protect commercially sensitive material under terms agreed with participants.

*Procedures.* Analysis will proceed abductively, cycling between the data and the prior, organized as a Gioia-style data structure (Gioia et al., 2013). Open coding will build first-order categories in the parties' own terms for what they do, staying close to how an editor describes whom she negotiates with or how an author describes what his agent does for him. Constant comparison will group these into second-order themes that name the practices analytically, who initiates a deal, who the counterpart addresses, whether and how the worker reaches the platform directly, and the themes will be examined for the aggregate dimensions they compose. One analytic move is fixed in advance because the prior demands it. For each kind of deal where both sides are interviewed, the study will compare the worker's account, the agent's account, and the counterpart's account of who the deal was made with, and will treat divergence among them as data about whose centrality is real and whose is assumed. A counterpart who names the agent as the party it dealt with, while the worker describes itself as the principal, is evidence on the prior, and the direction of the divergence is the finding (the project's qualitative methods treat such disagreement as data; see METHODS.md, this directory). The product is a data structure showing the path from the parties' language to the analytic themes, with a quotation table so a reader can audit the move from evidence to claim (Pratt, 2009).

*Trustworthiness.* The study pursues the four criteria of trustworthiness (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). For credibility, it triangulates the worker's, the agent's, and the counterpart's accounts of the same kind of deal against observation and documents, returns emerging themes to a subset of participants for correction through member checks, and engages each market over a sustained period. For transferability, it provides thick description of each market, the deal, and the legal and commercial context, so readers can judge where findings travel and which market a given finding came from. For dependability, it commits the interview guide, the initial coding scheme, and the pre-registered prior with its two named departures to a dated record before fieldwork, so the path from question to claim is inspectable and the departure, if found, is visibly not retrofitted. For confirmability, the data structure and quotation table tie each claim to evidence. A reflexive memo will track a risk specific to this study, that the prior could lead the analysis to read the agent as central wherever the agent is busy, missing a worker who is pivotal behind a visibly active agent, and the memo will record instances that cut against the prior as carefully as instances that fit it, so the prior is tested rather than confirmed.

**Exhibit A — Draft interview guide**

*Opening (rapport, context)*

1. Walk me through how a typical deal comes together in your work, from first contact to a signed agreement. Who is involved at each step?
2. How did you come into this relationship? How did the worker and the agent first start working together, and why?

*Whose dealings are which*

3. When a deal gets made, who makes the first move, and to whom? Walk me through a recent one.
4. When the people on the other side, the editor, the producer, the buyer, the brand, want to discuss a deal, who do they call? Has that always been who they call?
5. Tell me about a recent negotiation. Who was in the room or on the thread, who did the talking, and where was the worker in it?

*The worker's reach*

6. Can the worker reach the other side directly, without going through the agent? Has the worker ever done that? What happened?
7. If the worker wanted to make a deal tomorrow without this agent, could they? What would that take, and what would they lose?
8. Tell me about a time the worker and the other side dealt with each other directly. How did the agent figure in, if at all?

*Who the far side deals with (for counterparts)*

9. When you make a deal like this, who do you think of yourself as dealing with, the worker or the agent? Walk me through a recent case.
10. Has there been a deal where you wanted to go straight to the worker and the agent was in the way, or one where you would not deal with the worker except through the agent? Tell me about it.

*The position itself*

11. Some people might say the agent becomes the party the market deals with, and the worker stands behind them. Others might say the agent just opens doors and the worker is still the principal. From your own experience, which is closer, and what makes the difference?
12. Tell me about a time you felt the worker was the one the deal really turned on. And a time you felt it was the agent.
13. What happens to a worker who leaves their agent? What do they keep, and what do they lose?

*Contrast and closing*

14. How is this different for a well-known worker than for a newcomer? Does the agent's place in it change?
15. What do you wish people understood about who actually does what in these deals? Is there anything I should have asked but did not?
