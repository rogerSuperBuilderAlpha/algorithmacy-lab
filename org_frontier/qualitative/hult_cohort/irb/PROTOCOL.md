## 1. Purpose

The study asks how participants in an intensive developer programme learn to read a review process
they cannot fully see, and what happens when they disagree with what it decides.

Participants build software and submit each project through a gate: peers file a written review and
then cast a private vote, and the gate decides what advances. They coordinate through
project-management, internal-communications and showcase platforms that the cohort itself builds
and then runs on, so the systems change under them as the programme proceeds.

A companion survey study (protocol 260511078) measures three self-reported competences across three
waves. It can show whether scores move. It cannot show how anyone learns, what evidence they revise
their theories on, or what a participant does when a gate outcome strikes them as wrong. Those are
the questions here.

## 2. Research questions

1. How do participants build and revise working theories of what the gate rewards — from which
   incidents, on what evidence, and with what revisions?
2. What does a participant do when a gate outcome seems wrong: whom do they approach, what forum do
   they try to construct, and what follows?
3. Do reading a reviewer's wants and making one's own work legible appear as distinct practices
   with distinct occasions and distinct failures, or as one undifferentiated skill?
4. When platform or process rules change without announcement, who notices, how, and how does
   noticing travel through the cohort?
5. Where participants' accounts of who decided diverge from the documented record, what governs the
   difference?

Question 2 carries a pre-registered falsification condition. The theoretical claim under test is
that accountability — a forum with standing to demand justification — cannot be supplied by
individual competence however skilled. **The claim is refuted by a participant who reliably obtains
answerability through individual skill alone, without any forum existing.** The investigator will
look for that case and report it if found.

## 3. Design and procedures

### 3.1 Participants

Adults, 18 or older, enrolled in the Fall 2026 cohort. The whole cohort is invited; nobody is
excluded on any characteristic. Expected enrolment is in the tens.

An interview subsample of 8–15 is selected for maximum variation on three criteria fixed in
advance: outcome at the first gate (passed clean, revised, or failed), prior experience of platform
or gig work, and position in the coordination record. Selection is by the investigator from
consented volunteers only.

### 3.2 What participants are asked to do

| Component | What it involves | When |
|---|---|---|
| Interview, wave 1 | 45–60 minutes, semi-structured, audio recorded with permission | Weeks 6–10, after at least one complete build-review-vote cycle |
| Interview, wave 2 | 45–60 minutes, same form | Weeks 15–17 |
| Observation | The investigator takes field notes during regular cohort working and review sessions | Throughout |
| Coordination record | Existing pull requests, written reviews, issue threads and timing data from the programme's own repositories | Retrospective, at analysis |

Participation in any component is separable. A participant may consent to the record and decline
interviews, or the reverse.

### 3.3 What is never collected

- **Individual votes.** The gate's votes are private by design. Vote outcomes are used only in
  aggregate. An instructor-researcher reading individual ballots would change the thing being
  studied and is not proposed.
- **Assessment records, grades, or personnel files.**
- **Any data about a participant who declined**, beyond the fact of non-participation, which is not
  recorded against a name.

### 3.4 Written reviews are dyadic

A written review has an author and a recipient, and consenting to one's own data does not consent
for the other party. **A written review enters analysis only where both the reviewer and the
reviewed have consented.** Where only one has, the review is excluded.

## 4. The dual role, and what constrains it

The investigator teaches the programme he proposes to study. This is the protocol's central risk
and each constraint below is structural.

**What the instructor does and does not decide.** Advancement is decided by the peer gate — a
written review followed by a private vote among peers. The investigator does not cast a gate vote,
does not break ties, and does not assign a grade that determines advancement. His role is to run
the programme, teach its sessions, and maintain its platforms. This is stated in the consent form
in the same words.

**Recruitment does not run through the instructor.** The study is announced by a written notice on
the cohort platform and administered by a named third party who is not the investigator and has no
role in the programme. Consent is recorded on the platform. The investigator does not ask anyone in
person, in a session, or in a one-to-one channel.

**The investigator does not learn who declined.** Consent records are held by the third party and
released to the investigator only as a list of consented participants, after the first gate cycle
has completed. Nobody's decision reaches him while it could matter.

**No interview touches a live outcome.** A participant is never interviewed about a project whose
gate outcome is still open. Interviews cover completed cycles only.

**Withdrawal is total and late-available.** A participant may withdraw at any point before
publication; their interview recording, transcript, and coordination-record extract are deleted on
request, and the request goes to the third party, not to the investigator.

**Timing of the second interview.** Wave 2 falls at weeks 15–17 and may precede final assessment.
Where a participant's final assessment is still open, the second interview is deferred until after
it closes.

### 4.1 Declared fallback design

If the board finds interviews conducted by the instructor unacceptable during the programme, the
study proceeds as follows without further negotiation: observation and the coordination record run
during the cohort, and **all interviews move to after final assessments close**, from January 2027.
This loses the temporal contrast between mid-cohort and exit accounts, which is a real cost to
question 1, and it preserves questions 2 through 5 intact.

The fallback is stated here so the board can approve it directly rather than through a revision
round.

## 5. Risks, benefits, and confidentiality

**Risks are minimal and social.** The realistic risks are two: that a
participant feels obliged to take part because the person asking teaches them, and that something
said about a peer's review becomes identifiable within a small cohort. Section 4 addresses the
first. The second is addressed by pseudonymisation at transcription, by removing project names and
distinguishing detail from any quoted material, and by reporting cohort roles rather than
identities. In a cohort of tens, no quotation is used where the pairing of role and incident could
identify the speaker to another participant.

**No direct benefit** is offered. Nothing about participation affects standing, assessment, project
outcomes, or the relationship with the programme, with Hult, or with Bentley.

**Handling.** Recordings are transcribed and the audio deleted once the transcript is checked.
Transcripts carry a pseudonym and no name. The coordination-record extract is pseudonymised on the
same one-way coded identifier the survey study uses, so the two studies can be discussed in the
same paper without either holding a name. Identifiers are stored separately from content and are
accessible to the investigator alone. Only de-identified material is quoted, shared, or deposited.

## 6. Analysis

Abductive, following Timmermans and Tavory, with a Gioia-style data structure built per research
question. The three competences from the companion study are used as **sensitizing concepts and not
as a coding frame**: a finding that lived practice divides differently from the theoretical account
is a result the study is designed to be able to report, and the coding scheme is committed to the
repository before the first interview so that it can be checked against what was actually done.

**Single-coder declaration.** The methods guide sets an inter-rater bar of α ≥ .80. The
investigator is the only coder. Rather than claim a reliability statistic that a solo design cannot
produce, the study declares a single-coder design and substitutes two checks: an audit trail from
transcript through code to claim, and member checks in which participants are shown the account of
their own case and asked whether it is recognisable. If a second coder is recruited, a reliability
subsample of transcripts is coded and the statistic reported.
