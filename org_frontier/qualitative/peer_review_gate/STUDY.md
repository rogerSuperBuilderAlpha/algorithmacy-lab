# Who Decides the Manuscript: How Authors, Editors, and Reviewers Locate the Editorial Verdict

*Proposal draft — front end through Method, with draft interview guide.*

---

**Abstract (96 words)**

An editorial decision on a manuscript passes through three parties and a software system: an author who submits, reviewers who advise, and an editor who decides, coordinated through a manuscript management platform. The standard picture puts the editor at the pivot, a party in every coalition that can move the manuscript forward, holding a gate that binds. This study examines where the decision is actually located. Through interviews with authors, handling editors, and reviewers, and analysis of decision correspondence, it asks whether the editor commits the verdict or relays one settled elsewhere, by the reviewers or by the system itself.

---

**Problem statement and research question**

A manuscript enters a journal and a decision comes back. Between the two sits an arrangement of three parties and a machine. The author submits and waits. Reviewers read and recommend. A handling editor, sitting above the reviewers and below the editor-in-chief, weighs the recommendations and issues a verdict: reject, revise, accept. The whole exchange runs through a submission system, Editorial Manager or ScholarOne or one of their kin, which routes the files, matches reviewers, sets deadlines, and formats the letter that reaches the author. The structure repeats across every journal in every field, millions of times a year, and a scholarly career is built on its outcomes.

The question of who decides looks settled. The editor decides. The masthead says so, the decision letter is signed by the editor, and the formal authority to accept or reject a manuscript rests with no one else. Cooperative game theory gives this intuition a sharper form. An editor who sits in every coalition that can advance a manuscript is a veto player, a party every productive coalition must include, and a veto player carries the pivotal weight in the arrangement (see [`veto_player/THREAD.md`](../../threads/veto_player/THREAD.md)). On that reading the editor commits the determination. The reviewers advise and the system routes, but the verdict is the editor's act, a gate that binds because nothing passes the editor without the editor's say.

Editorial practice gives reasons to doubt that the formal picture describes the lived one. A handling editor facing two reviews, one positive and one negative, on a manuscript outside the editor's own expertise, may have little to add beyond tallying what the reviewers said. The decision letter then conveys a verdict the reviewers reached, with the editor as the channel through which it travels. Editors themselves report deciding largely on the reviews when the reviews agree, and intervening only when they conflict (Hirschauer, 2010). If that is the common case, the reviewers are the de facto deciders and the editor relays. The veto-player reading would then misplace the decision, naming as the committer a party who often only transmits.

The submission system complicates the picture further. Desk-rejection rules screen manuscripts before any human editor reads them, on criteria encoded in the workflow. Reviewer-matching algorithms determine which experts ever see a manuscript, and the choice of reviewer is widely understood to shape the outcome, since the recommendation a manuscript receives depends heavily on who is asked (Teplitskiy, Acuna, Elamrani-Raoult, Körding, & Evans, 2018). A system that selects the reviewers may select the verdict. Where the decision sits, then, is an open question even though the formal answer is fixed, and the parties may not agree among themselves about the answer.

This study takes the question as one about how the parties locate the decision in their own accounts and in the traces the work leaves. It does not set out to prove that the editor is or is not the real decider. It sets out to describe how authors, editors, and reviewers experience and explain the editorial verdict, and to find where each of them places the act that settles the manuscript's fate.

**Research question.** Where do the parties to an editorial decision locate the act that determines a manuscript's outcome, and how do their locations of it differ? Two lines follow. First, what does the handling editor do between the reviews and the letter: weigh and commit a verdict, or aggregate and transmit one the reviews already imply, and what distinguishes the two in practice? Second, what does the submission system decide before and around the editor, through desk-reject rules and reviewer matching, and do the parties experience those as the editor's choices, their own, or the system's?

---

**Expected contributions**

The study is exploratory and aims to produce grounded description, so its contributions are framed as openings.

To theory, the study offers an empirical test of the veto-player reading against a setting built for it. The editorial triad is the cleanest available instance of a third party holding formal decision authority over two others, with the authority written into the masthead and the signature on the letter. If even here the party who formally commits the verdict turns out to relay one settled by the reviewers or pre-empted by the system, the case for distinguishing structural pivotality from the act of deciding is made on the arrangement most favorable to conflating them. The contribution is that distinction, drawn on a setting where the formal and the lived locations of the decision can be compared directly.

The study connects to a broader interest in coordination through an intermediary whose deciding cannot be watched. Algorithmic intermediaries are opaque, and the people coordinating through them can report only what they infer from outputs. The editorial system is partly such an intermediary, since its desk-reject and reviewer-matching rules act without explanation, but it sits beside human parties who can be interviewed. A close account of how authors and editors attribute outcomes between a human editor and a software workflow offers a point of comparison for settings where the deciding party is wholly machine.

To practice, a description of where the decision actually sits has value for the governance of peer review. Reform proposals address the editor, the reviewers, and the platform as if each were a stable locus of accountability, but if the verdict is jointly produced and the parties disagree about who authored it, then accountability is harder to place than the reform literature assumes. Naming where the decision is felt to sit, and where the accounts diverge, would inform how editorial responsibility is assigned and contested.

**Literature review**

Four conversations bear on this study, and reading them together locates what it can add. Work on peer review as a social process has shown that the editorial decision is constructed through interaction rather than read off the manuscript, but has treated the editor's authorship of the verdict as given. Work on gatekeeping established the gatekeeper as a structural position controlling access, while leaving open whether the gatekeeper commits the decision or executes one made elsewhere. Work on coordination and articulation has analyzed how interdependent parties mesh distributed work, without taking up the case where one party holds nominal authority over the others' contributions. Work on the third party and decision authority in organizations supplies the vocabulary of the pivotal third, but has not been brought to the editorial triad. None has asked where the parties themselves locate the act that decides, which is the opening this study works in.

*Peer review as a social process.* Peer review has been studied as an accomplished social practice. The broad synthesis establishes that reviewer recommendations on the same manuscript agree only weakly, that the outcome depends substantially on who reviews, and that the decision is produced through a chain of judgments rather than determined by the manuscript's quality alone (Bornmann, 2011). The bias literature sharpens this, documenting that characteristics of authors, reviewers, and their relationship shape evaluations in ways the formal process does not acknowledge (Lee, Sugimoto, Zhang, & Cronin, 2013). The closest study to the present one examines how editors decide. Editors describe weighing reviews against one another, discounting recommendations they distrust, and reaching a verdict through interpretive work that the decision letter then presents as the orderly outcome of review (Hirschauer, 2010). Quantitative work on reviewer influence shows that a manuscript's fate tracks the disposition of the reviewers it happens to draw, so that reviewer assignment carries much of the decision (Teplitskiy et al., 2018). This conversation establishes that the editorial verdict is constructed and contingent, and it supplies the present study with a detailed picture of the judgments that go into it. It has framed the editor as the one who decides, examining how the editor decides while taking for granted that the editor does. Whether the parties experience the editor as the author of the verdict, or as the party who signs a verdict the reviewers or the system produced, is a question this conversation has not posed, and it is the one this study takes up.

*Gatekeeping in scholarly publishing.* The image of the gate organizes a long line of work on who controls access to publication. The editor was named the gatekeeper of science early, the party deciding which work enters the literature and which is turned away (Crane, 1967), within a broader account of science as a system of evaluation and reward in which recognition is allocated through institutional gates (Merton, 1973). Recent work has carried the gatekeeping frame into the mechanics of editorial decision-making, analyzing the conflicts an editor manages among reviewers, authors, and the journal's standards, and the discretion the editor exercises in resolving them (Gould, 2012). This conversation establishes the gatekeeper as a structural position controlling a channel, and it supplies the present study with the recognition that the editorial role regulates access to the literature. It has tended to treat holding the gate and deciding what passes it as one thing, so that the party at the gate is assumed to author the decision the gate enforces. The veto-player thread separates the two: a party can occupy the indispensable structural position, be in every coalition that moves the manuscript, and still relay a determination settled elsewhere instead of committing one (see [`veto_player/THREAD.md`](../../threads/veto_player/THREAD.md)). The gatekeeping literature has examined the editor as the holder of the gate. This study examines whether holding the gate is the same as deciding what passes it.

*Coordination and articulation work.* How interdependent parties mesh distributed contributions into a single outcome has been analyzed closely in the sociology of work. Strauss (1988) named articulation work, the often invisible labor of arranging and aligning tasks so that the visible product comes together, and later work developed the analysis of such labor as routinely unrecognized (Star & Strauss, 1999). An editorial decision is articulation work of a particular kind. Several reviews, written independently by people who never meet, are aligned by the editor and the system into a single letter that speaks with one voice, and the work of producing that voice from discordant inputs is largely hidden behind the finished verdict. This conversation supplies the present study with the concept of the decision as assembled, and with attention to the hidden labor of assembly. What it has analyzed is coordination among parties of roughly equal standing, or coordination distributed across artifacts and awareness. The editorial case adds a feature the articulation literature has not centered: one party, the editor, holds formal authority over the contributions being meshed, and the system encodes rules that constrain the meshing before any party acts. How articulation work proceeds when one of the coordinating parties is also the nominal decider, and when a software workflow pre-structures what there is to coordinate, is the question this study brings to the conversation.

*The third party and decision authority.* Social theory of the triad supplies the vocabulary for a third standing between two others. Simmel (1950) argued that a third party occupies distinct positions, among them the tertius gaudens, the third who derives advantage from a configuration of the other two, profiting from their division or their dependence on the third's position between them. Later work specified the active third who joins parties or holds them apart, theorizing the orientation toward connecting others who would otherwise remain unconnected (Obstfeld, 2005). The cooperative-game treatment adds the structural fact that a third who sits in every productive coalition carries the pivotal weight, the veto player whose inclusion every coalition requires (see [`veto_player/THREAD.md`](../../threads/veto_player/THREAD.md)). This conversation supplies the present study with its prior: the editor as the pivotal third, the party between author and reviewers whose position every decision must pass through. It has established what structural pivotality is and why the third in the middle position holds it. It has not asked whether the pivotal third commits the determination or conveys one, which is the distinction the editorial case is built to examine. The third who is in every coalition may decide, or may stand at the point everything passes through while the deciding happens upstream among the reviewers or downstream in the system's rules.

*The opening.* The four conversations leave a specific gap. The peer-review literature shows the verdict is constructed but assumes the editor authors it. The gatekeeping literature establishes the editor as the holder of the gate but conflates holding it with deciding what passes. The articulation literature analyzes assembled outcomes but not the case where one assembler holds authority and a system pre-structures the inputs. The triad literature supplies the pivotal third but does not ask whether the pivot decides or relays. What none has examined is where the parties to an editorial decision locate the act that settles the manuscript, and whether the editor who formally commits the verdict is experienced as its author, its transmitter, or the executor of a determination the reviewers or the submission system reached first. This study enters that opening to describe how authors, editors, and reviewers place the decision, on a setting where the formal location is fixed and the lived one is open, so that the distinction between holding a pivotal position and committing a determination can be examined where it most matters.

**Method**

This is a qualitative, interpretive study designed to build theory from how the parties to an editorial decision account for where the decision sits. The study brings a prior to the field, the veto-player reading on which the editor commits the verdict, and it follows an abductive logic. It does not test that reading as a hypothesis, and it does not treat the editor's authorship as a category the data must confirm. The prior works as a sensitizing concept (Blumer, 1954), orienting attention toward the location of the deciding act without fixing what the analysis will find there. A finding that the editor relays the reviewers' verdict, or that the system commits through its rules, is as analyzable as a finding that the editor decides. The analytic procedures follow the Gioia methodology (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013), suited to a phenomenon that is processual, contested among the parties, and not captured by the formal account of who decides.

*Site selection.* The study will focus on three to four journals in the social sciences, selected for variation in field, prestige tier, and submission platform, with at least one running Editorial Manager and one running ScholarOne. Journals are the unit because the editorial decision repeats there in a stable form, with the same three party roles and the same system mediating each instance. The social sciences are chosen because their reviews are discursive and their decisions turn on interpretation, with no checklist a referee can mechanically apply, which keeps the editor's interpretive work in play and makes the question of who decides live. Variation in prestige tier matters because desk-rejection is heavier at selective journals, so the system's role in the decision should differ across the tiers and the contrast can be observed. Holding the field roughly constant across the journals keeps variation in how the decision is located from being confounded with variation in disciplinary review norms.

*Sources of data.* The study draws on three sources, with interviews primary and decision correspondence and platform documentation providing triangulation. The first is semi-structured interviews with 24 to 30 participants spanning the three roles: handling editors, frequent reviewers, and recently decided authors, recruited through journal mastheads, professional networks, and snowball referral, and sampled so that for several specific manuscripts an editor, a reviewer, and the author can each be interviewed about the same decision. Interviews will run 60 to 90 minutes, be recorded with consent, and be transcribed verbatim. The guide will evolve as early analysis surfaces new lines, and sampling will continue until additional interviews stop yielding new second-order themes. A draft guide appears in the Exhibit. The second source is editorial correspondence: decision letters, reviewer reports, and where editors will share them, the editor's notes or comments to the editor-in-chief, used to compare what the letter presents as the basis of the decision against what the parties say went into it. The third source is platform documentation and the editor's view of the workflow, including desk-reject criteria, reviewer-matching settings, and the decision-type menus the system offers, used to establish what the system commits or constrains before the editor acts. Because the data touch confidential review materials and identifiable decisions, the study will proceed only under institutional review board approval, will obtain informed consent from all participants, will anonymize manuscripts and parties, and will handle reviewer identities under the confidentiality the review process assumes, excluding any material a participant declines to share.

*Procedures.* Analysis will proceed abductively, cycling between the data and the sensitizing prior, organized as a Gioia-style data structure (Gioia et al., 2013). Open coding will build first-order categories in the parties' own terms, staying close to how they describe the decision, for instance an editor saying the reviews "made the call for me" or an author saying the system "killed it before anyone read it." Constant comparison will group these into second-order themes naming the practices analytically, such as deferring to concordant reviews, overriding a recommendation, or attributing an outcome to the workflow. The themes will be examined for the aggregate dimensions they compose, with the location of the deciding act allowed to take whatever shape the data support. Where the three parties to the same decision locate it differently, the editor claiming to have weighed and decided while the author experiences a relayed reviewer verdict and the reviewer assumes the editor decided, the divergence will be retained and analyzed as data about how the decision is distributed and misattributed, rather than resolved into one account. The matched cases, where editor, reviewer, and author speak to the same manuscript, will anchor this analysis, since they let the same decision be seen from three positions. The product will be a data structure tracing the path from the parties' language to the analytic themes, with a table of representative quotations for each theme so a reader can audit the move from evidence to claim (Pratt, 2009).

*Trustworthiness.* The study will pursue the four criteria of trustworthiness (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). For credibility, it will triangulate interviews against decision correspondence and platform documentation, use the matched cases to cross-check accounts of the same decision, and return emerging themes to a subset of participants for correction through member checks. For transferability, it will provide thick description of the journals, the platforms, and the fields, so readers can judge where the findings travel, for instance to the natural sciences where reviews are less discursive. For dependability, it will commit the interview guide and the initial coding scheme to a dated record before fieldwork and maintain an audit trail of analytic decisions. For confirmability, the data structure and the quotation table will tie each claim to evidence. Throughout, the researcher will keep a reflexive memo on a risk specific to this study, that the veto-player prior could lead the analysis to read the editor as the decider because the masthead says so, and the memo will track instances where the decision sits with the reviewers or the system as carefully as instances where it sits with the editor, so the prior is held open.

**Exhibit A — Draft interview guide**

The guide is asked in role-adapted versions. Editors are asked the full set; reviewers and authors are asked the items that bear on their position, rephrased to their vantage.

*Opening (rapport, context)*

1. Walk me through what happens to a manuscript from the moment it arrives to the moment the author hears back. What are the actual steps, and who touches it at each one?
2. How did you come into this role, editor, reviewer, or author at this journal, and how did you learn how decisions here get made?

*The reviews and the verdict (editors)*

3. Take a recent decision where the reviews agreed. Walk me through what you did between reading them and writing the letter. What did you add to what the reviewers said?
4. Now take one where the reviews conflicted. What did you do then, and how did you decide which way to go?
5. When you write a decision letter, how much of it is the reviewers' verdict and how much is yours? Is there a difference, in your experience, and when does it matter?
6. Tell me about a time you went against what the reviewers recommended. What made you do it, and how did it feel to overrule them?

*The system (all roles)*

7. Before a manuscript reaches a human, what does the system do to it? Walk me through how a desk reject happens and who, or what, decides it.
8. How are reviewers chosen for a manuscript? How much of that is your choice, and how much does the system propose or constrain?
9. Have you seen a decision turn on who got assigned to review? Tell me about it.

*Locating the decision (all roles)*

10. When the author gets the letter, whose decision are they getting? If you had to say who actually decided, who would it be?
11. Some people would say the editor decides and the reviewers advise. Others would say the reviewers decide and the editor passes it along. From where you sit, which is closer to the truth, and when?
12. As an author, when a decision came back, where did you feel it came from, the editor, the reviewers, or the system? What made you feel that?
13. Tell me about a decision where you were not sure who had really made it.

*Contrast and closing*

14. How is deciding a manuscript different from a decision where one person is plainly in charge? What about this one is shared, and with whom?
15. What do you wish people understood about who actually decides what gets published?
16. Is there anything I should have asked but did not?
