# The Record as a Third Party: How Clinicians Coordinate Care Across the Shift Boundary Through a Handoff Tool

Care continuity at shift change runs through a record. An outgoing clinician writes the patient into a handoff tool, an incoming clinician reads it, and the care carries on under someone new. The interdependence prior expects a true handoff to bind only when the parties couple both ways: the incoming clinician queries back and the outgoing one adjusts. A one-way dump conveys a note and binds nothing. This study examines the shift boundary through interviews, observation, and the handoff documents themselves, asking when the record holds the two clinicians together and when it merely sits between them.

## Problem statement and research question

Hospital care does not stop when a clinician goes home. A patient admitted under one physician is, twelve hours later, the responsibility of another who has never met them, and that transfer happens hundreds of times a day across a single hospital. The handoff is the moment care continuity is preserved or broken, and it is among the most studied hazards in patient safety, because a fact known to the outgoing clinician and lost to the incoming one is a fact that can kill (Patterson & Wears, 2010). The remedy hospitals have settled on is to route the handoff through a structured record. A clinician fills in an I-PASS sheet or an electronic handoff module, the next clinician reads it, and the standardized form is supposed to carry across the boundary what a hurried conversation would drop (Starmer et al., 2014).

The structured record raises a question the safety literature has mostly stepped around. A handoff is meant to be coordination between two clinicians accountable for the same patient at different times, working out together what the incoming one needs to know. A record can hold the two clinicians together, or it can stand between them as a substitute for their contact. When the outgoing clinician writes a note and leaves, and the incoming clinician reads it cold the next morning with no one to ask, the record has delivered a document from one person to another who reads it alone. Which of these a given handoff is depends on the practice, not the form: two units running the same I-PASS template can produce a live exchange in one and a one-way drop in the other.

The record introduces a further possibility. The handoff tool can commit a determination of its own. An electronic module that flags a patient as high-acuity, surfaces an unacknowledged critical lab, or carries forward a problem list and a code status presents the incoming clinician with a standing fact that neither clinician authored in that moment and both must heed (Berg, 1999; Cabitza, Rasoini, & Gensini, 2017). When the record commits this way, it becomes a third party in the coordination, holding a determination the outgoing and incoming clinicians both answer to. The handoff would then be a three-cornered arrangement, and whether it binds would turn on the record's couplings as much as the clinicians'.

**Research question.** How do clinicians coordinate a patient's care across the shift boundary through a handoff record, and when does that record hold the two clinicians together as opposed to conveying a note from one to the other? Two lines follow. First, what makes a handoff reciprocal in practice: under what conditions does the incoming clinician query back, the outgoing clinician revise, and the two settle the patient between them, and under what conditions does the incoming clinician read alone and proceed? Second, when does the record itself commit a determination both clinicians must heed, and how do they experience a handoff in which the tool has fixed something neither chose in that exchange?

This is a question about practice, and the study pursues it as one. It observes and describes what clinicians do at the boundary, and generates material from which the structural question, when coordination through a record binds and when it conveys, can be sharply posed.

## Expected contributions

The study is exploratory and builds grounded description, so its contributions are openings for later work.

To theory, the study offers a worked case of coordination through a record at a sharp temporal boundary, where the two parties are the same role at two times and never overlap for long. The handoff is an unusually clean instance of artifact-mediated coordination: bounded, repeated, and accomplished between two people who may never speak. The interdependence prior predicts such a transfer binds only when the coupling runs both ways. By describing when the incoming clinician's queries reach an outgoing clinician who can still revise, and when the handoff collapses into a one-way deposit, the study aims to give the prior a lived shape, the conditions under which a record coordinates two people and the conditions under which it carries a document past a boundary with no one to answer.

A second contribution concerns the record as a possible third party. The safety literature treats the handoff tool as a channel whose job is fidelity. The study takes seriously that the tool can commit determinations of its own, an acuity flag, a forced code-status field, a carried-forward problem list, that both clinicians then answer to. A record that commits this way makes the handoff a three-party arrangement in which the tool holds a standing fact. Describing how clinicians experience such a record, whether they treat its determinations as the outgoing clinician's voice, as their own to override, or as a third thing that binds them both, would clarify when a documentation tool crosses from medium to party. The distinction bears on every setting where a system now commits something the people coordinating around it must heed.

To practice, a close account of when handoffs bind and when they merely convey has value for patient safety on its own. Hospitals have invested heavily in structured tools on the premise that the form carries the safety. The same form supports a live exchange or a dead drop depending on conditions the form leaves unspecified, and naming those conditions could inform how handoffs are scheduled, staffed, and supported, so that the structure does not certify a coordination that never happened.

## The prior

The nearest prior in the catalog is the interdependence thread ([THREAD.md](../../threads/interdependence/THREAD.md)), which reads Thompson's typology of pooled, sequential, and reciprocal interdependence through exact Φ and finds that only reciprocal coupling binds. Parties that pass work one way down a line produce a sequential arrangement, and a feedforward line carries no integration at all: the handoff that goes one direction leaves no loop, and without a loop there is nothing irreducible to find. Only when the parties read one another both ways does the coordination commit.

The prior sets a clear expectation for the shift boundary. A handoff that binds the two clinicians into an irreducible whole should show reciprocal coupling. The incoming clinician queries back, asking the outgoing one what a vague note meant or whether a plan still holds; the outgoing clinician, still reachable, revises in light of the question. That loop makes the handoff a coordination between two people. A handoff with no return path, where the outgoing clinician writes and is gone and the incoming clinician reads alone, is sequential and conveys a note. By the prior, it carries no integration.

The prior is held open at two places where the real setting may depart from it. The first departure is the record committing a determination. When the handoff tool flags an acuity, forces a field, or carries a problem list forward, a fact stands between the two clinicians that neither authored in that exchange and both must heed. The arrangement gains a third node, and whether it binds turns on whether that node reads the clinicians and is read by them, both ways, beyond emitting a flag. The second departure is the genuinely one-way handoff. The incoming clinician may be reading a document the outgoing one left, with no one to query and no one revising, coordinating with no one. The prior would call this no integration. The field may show it is the ordinary case, and that the structured handoff hospitals trust is, for long stretches, a clinician alone with a record. Either departure is where the contribution is built.

## Literature review

Four conversations bear on this study, and reading them together locates the opening. Handoff safety research establishes that the transfer is hazardous and has worked to standardize it, while leaving open whether a standardized form secures the coordination it is meant to carry. Coordination and articulation research establishes that joint work is accomplished through artifacts and invisible alignment, without dwelling on the temporal boundary where the two parties never overlap. Research on the medical record as an actor establishes that records do work of their own, without settling when that work makes the record a party. Distributed cognition and common ground establish what it takes for two minds to share a state, against which the one-way read can be measured. None has asked when a handoff through a record binds the two clinicians and when it conveys a note.

*Handoff safety, and what standardization leaves open.* The handoff is among the most scrutinized hazards in hospital care, and a large literature documents how badly it can fail (Cohen & Hilligoss, 2010). Patterson and Wears (2010) reviewed the effort to measure handoff quality and found the field unable to settle on reliable tools, because the handoff is a complex interaction whose success resists capture by a checklist. The dominant remedy is structured standardization, exemplified by I-PASS, whose multi-site implementation cut medical errors substantially by giving clinicians a common format (Starmer et al., 2014). The premise running through this work is that fidelity is the problem: get the outgoing clinician's knowledge across intact and the patient is safe. The question examined less is whether a completed form means a coordination occurred. A clinician can fill every field and leave, the next clinician can read every field alone, and the standardized handoff will record a transfer that bound no one.

*Coordination and articulation work.* How interdependent actors accomplish joint work through artifacts and one another's awareness has been studied closely in the sociology of work and in computer-supported cooperative work. Strauss (1988) named the articulation work by which interdependent tasks are meshed into a functioning whole, the often invisible labor of arranging and aligning, later developed as a category of routinely unrecognized work (Star & Strauss, 1999). The CSCW tradition extended this to coordination through shared information spaces, showing that interdependent actors can coordinate by way of a common artifact (Schmidt & Bannon, 1992). Within healthcare, the patient has been theorized as moving between a thing coordinated around and an active participant in their own coordination, drawn as the difference between a boundary object and a boundary subject (Bishop & Waring, 2019). This work supplies the study's central concept, coordination as accomplished practice. The handoff sharpens it at a point the tradition treats in passing: the two parties are the same role at two times, they may never overlap, and whatever binds them runs through a record across a boundary in time.

*The record as an actor.* Berg (1999) showed that the medical record does work of its own, accumulating and coordinating the activity of dispersed clinicians, so that the record shapes the work it documents. Suchman (2007) established that the interaction between people and machines is built up in situ, with the machine's contribution depending on how people read and answer it. Work on clinical decision tools warns that systems which surface flags and recommendations introduce determinations clinicians must contend with, carrying consequences neither the makers nor the users fully control (Cabitza, Rasoini, & Gensini, 2017). Together these establish that a handoff record can commit something of its own, an acuity flag, a carried-forward problem list, a forced field. The question they leave open is the one this study presses: when the record commits a determination both clinicians heed, has it become a third party, or is it still a medium they read through. The answer turns on whether clinicians treat the record's determinations as a standing fact that binds them or as content they author and override at will.

*Distributed cognition and common ground.* Hutchins (1995) showed that cognition in a working system is distributed across people and artifacts, so that a state held by no single person is carried by the arrangement as a whole, the condition a handoff would have to achieve for two clinicians to share a patient. Clark and Brennan (1991) specified what sharing a state requires: grounding, the back-and-forth by which two parties confirm they have understood the same thing, accumulated through queries and acknowledgments. Grounding is reciprocal by construction, and a one-way read affords none of it. This conversation gives the study its measure of the departure. A handoff in which the incoming clinician queries back and the outgoing one confirms is a grounding exchange; a handoff read alone is a clinician taking a document on trust, the common ground assumed.

## Setting and boundary

The recurring act is the shift-change handover on a hospital inpatient unit. The boundary is the transfer of accountability for a panel of patients from the clinician going off shift to the clinician coming on, accomplished through a structured handoff record. What repeats is the per-patient handoff: for each patient on the list, a summary is written or carried forward, read, and acted on, and the cycle runs every shift, every day.

The unit is kept small. Three parties hold the recurring act: the outgoing clinician, the incoming clinician, and the handoff record. The patient is present in the record as the thing coordinated around and does not author the handoff. The wider team enters where it bears on whether the two clinicians couple back to each other. The study confines itself to the moment of transfer and the practices immediately around it, the writing, the reading, the query back if there is one, and the first hours of the incoming shift when a misread handoff surfaces.

---

## Parties

The **outgoing clinician** holds the current state of each patient, what was done this shift, what is pending, what to watch, and updates the record to carry it across. The candidate question is whether this party is reachable after writing. A clinician who has left the building and does not answer a page is, for the next shift, a written note with no author behind it.

The **incoming clinician** takes the record as a starting state and acts on it. The candidate question is whether this party queries back, treating the handoff as the opening of a coordination, or reads and proceeds, treating it as a finished document.

The **handoff record** carries the outgoing clinician's account and, in its electronic form, may commit determinations of its own. It is a candidate for spectator where it only stores and displays what one clinician wrote for another to read; it watches the handoff without being read into it. It is a candidate for a third party where it flags, forces, or carries forward a fact both clinicians must heed. Which of these the record is in a given unit is one of the things the study is built to observe.

## Methods

This is a qualitative, interpretive study designed to build theory from what clinicians do and say at the shift boundary. It brings a prior to the field, the interdependence distinction between a transfer that couples both ways and one that conveys one way, and so follows an abductive logic. The distinction works as a sensitizing concept (Blumer, 1954), orienting attention toward whether the handoff binds without dictating what the analysis finds. The analytic procedures follow the Gioia methodology (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013).

*Site selection.* The study will be conducted on the inpatient units of one or two hospitals in a single metropolitan area, chosen because the shift boundary there is sharp, recurring, and routed through a structured handoff tool that can be observed and read. Confining the study to a small number of units in one area keeps the regulatory environment, the technology, and the staffing model comparable, so that variation in how clinicians do the handoff is not confounded with variation in the institutions. Where two units are used, a contrast between a unit whose handoff tool commits little and one whose electronic module flags and forces fields offers a way to observe whether the record's couplings shift the work.

*Sources of data.* The study draws on the methods in [METHODS.md](../METHODS.md): semi-structured interviews as the primary source, with observation and document and log analysis for triangulation. Interviews will be conducted with 20 to 25 clinicians (attending physicians, residents, and nurses, depending on which roles own the handoff on the chosen units), recruited through unit leadership and sampled for variation in role, shift, seniority, and patient acuity. Interviews run 60 to 90 minutes, recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim, and the guide evolves as early analysis surfaces new lines until interviews stop yielding new themes. A draft guide appears in Exhibit A. Observation covers handovers across shifts, recording in field notes who writes, who reads, whether a query back occurs, whether the outgoing clinician revises, and what the record displays and commits at the moment of transfer, which makes it possible to see whether a handoff clinicians call a conversation is in practice a one-way read. Document and log analysis covers the records for the observed patients: the structured fields, the carried-forward content, and where available the system logs of who opened a record, when, and whether the tool flagged or forced anything. The study proceeds only under institutional review board approval, with informed consent and a protocol for protecting patient information, and observation will be suspended whenever it would intrude on care.

*Procedures.* Analysis cycles abductively between the data and the sensitizing distinction, organized as a Gioia-style data structure (Gioia et al., 2013). Open coding builds first-order categories in the clinicians' own terms, close to how they describe the handoff: "I just read what they left," "I paged her to ask what she meant," "the flag was already on it." Constant comparison groups these into second-order themes that name the practices analytically, the query-back loop, the dead drop, the carried-forward determination, the override of a flag. The themes are examined for the aggregate dimensions the finding turns on, expected to bear on whether the handoff couples both ways and whether the record commits, though the dimensions take whatever shape the data support. Two coders work against a committed scheme, and inter-rater reliability is reported for the second-order themes. A table of representative quotations accompanies each theme so a reader can audit the move from the clinicians' language to the analytic claim (Pratt, 2009).

*Findings.* The findings will give a thick description of the shift boundary as clinicians live it: how the outgoing clinician decides what to write and what to leave out, how the incoming clinician reads a list of patients they have never met, whether and how a query back happens, what the record commits on its own, and where authority over the plan is felt to sit across the transfer. Where clinicians describe the same handoff differently, one as a live exchange, another as a note read alone, both accounts are retained as data about how the boundary is understood. The settling observation for that disagreement is whether a query back actually reaches an outgoing clinician who revises, which the handover and the system logs can establish where interview accounts alone cannot.

## Trustworthiness

The study pursues the four criteria of trustworthiness (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). For credibility, it triangulates interviews against observed handovers and the records, returns emerging themes to a subset of participating clinicians through member checks, and engages the units over a sustained period. For transferability, it provides thick description of the units, the handoff tool, the staffing model, and the institutional context. For dependability, it commits the interview guide and the initial coding scheme to a dated record before fieldwork and maintains an audit trail of analytic decisions. For confirmability, the data structure and the quotation table tie each claim to evidence. A reflexive memo tracks a risk specific to this study: the prior could lead the analysis to read a query back as binding coupling where clinicians experience something routine, so the memo records instances that cut against the prior alongside those that fit it.

## Contribution, limits, and what would falsify this

The study aims to refine the interdependence prior, specifying the conditions under which coordination through a record binds two parties at a temporal boundary and the conditions under which it conveys a document to someone reading alone, and the conditions under which the record stops being a medium and becomes a third party holding a determination both clinicians heed. Both refinements are stated so another study, in a handoff setting or any record-mediated transfer, can build on them.

The finding would be overturned if observation showed that handoffs clinicians describe as live exchanges are, in practice, one-way reads with no query back and no revision, so that the reciprocal coupling the prior requires is rarely present and the structured handoff is mostly a clinician alone with a record. It would be complicated if the record's committed determinations turn out to do the binding the clinicians' couplings do not, making the tool the load-bearing party. The study is confined to inpatient shift change in one or two units, and how far the account travels, to sign-out across services, transfer between facilities, or the algorithmic case where the record commits without a human author, is a question the data can inform but not settle.

## Exhibit A — Draft interview guide

*Opening (rapport, context)*

1. Walk me through a shift change, from when you start writing the handoff, or start reading it, to when you take over the patients. What does it actually involve?
2. How did you learn to give and take a handoff well? What did you get wrong early on?

*Writing the handoff (outgoing)*

3. When you write a handoff for a patient, how do you decide what goes in and what you leave out? What do you assume the next person will already know?
4. Tell me about a recent handoff you wrote for a patient you were worried about. What did you want to be sure the next clinician understood?
5. After you have written the handoff and gone off shift, does anyone ever reach you about a patient? Walk me through the last time that happened.

*Reading the handoff (incoming)*

6. When you come on and read the handoff for patients you have never met, how do you figure out what is really going on? What do you trust, and what do you double-check?
7. Tell me about a time the handoff did not tell you something you needed, and you went looking. Who or what did you go to?
8. When something in a handoff is unclear, do you contact the clinician who wrote it? Walk me through the last time you did, and a time you decided not to.

*The record itself*

9. Some of what is in a handoff was typed by the last clinician, and some is carried forward or put there by the system, a flag, a problem list, a code status. How do you tell which is which, and does it matter to you?
10. Tell me about a time the system flagged or surfaced something on a patient, and you and the previous clinician both had to deal with it. What did you make of the flag?
11. Have you ever disagreed with something the record carried forward, and changed it, or left it? What happened?

*The boundary*

12. When you take over a patient, walk me through where the decisions sit. Are you continuing the last clinician's plan, the record's plan, or making it your own? How does that feel different across those?
13. Some people might say a handoff is just passing a note from one shift to the next. Others might say it is two clinicians working a patient out together. From your experience, which is it, and does it change from patient to patient?
14. How is taking over a patient from a colleague you can still reach different from taking over one whose previous clinician is unreachable? What changes for you?

*Closing*

15. What do you wish people understood about what it takes to hand off a patient safely, or to pick one up?
16. Is there anything I should have asked but did not?
