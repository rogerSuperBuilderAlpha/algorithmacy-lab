# Watching the Queue: Whether a Triage Supervisor Is a Member of Service Coordination or Only Its Spectator

*Proposal draft — front end through Method, with draft interview guide.*

---

**Abstract (96 words)**

Customer-service triage routes a contact through an automated classifier to a human agent, with a supervisor watching the queue from a dashboard. A prior from in-silico work says the watcher is never in the coordination's core: a party read by no one makes no difference to what the others do next. This study examines that prior in a live contact center. It asks whether the supervisor stays a spectator or gets pulled into the work because agents adjust to being watched, and whether the customer co-determines the routing or is only its object. Departures from the prior are the contribution.

---

**Problem statement and research question**

A customer with a problem reaches a company and is sorted before a person ever speaks to them. An interactive voice menu or a chatbot classifier reads what the customer says, assigns a category, and routes the contact to a queue, and an agent picks it up. Above the agent sits a supervisor watching a dashboard: how long the queue is, how long each contact is taking, which agents are behind, which contacts are about to breach a service-level deadline. The supervisor reads the agent's every move and reads the classifier's output, and the agent and the classifier do not read the supervisor back in any moment-to-moment way. The arrangement has four parties and a clear asymmetry of who reads whom.

The question this raises is who actually does the coordination. Four bodies are present, but presence is cheap. A party can sit inside the boundary of a coordinated act, take in everything the others do, and still make no difference to what any of them does next. Such a party watches a coordination it is not part of. The supervisor looks like the obvious candidate for this role, and so, for a different reason, does the customer, whose problem is what gets sorted but who may have no hand in the sorting. Naming which of the four parties are members and which are spectators is the problem, and it is a problem about practice, because the answer turns on what each party actually does to the others in the running of a contact.

A prior from this lab's in-silico work gives a sharp expectation to bring to the field. The observer thread (`../../threads/observer/THREAD.md`) places a fourth party around a coordinating triad and varies one thing: whether the party is read by the others or only reads them. A party that reads the others but is read by none enters the irreducible core in none of the runs, 0 of 164 forms. A party with inputs and no outputs has no causal effect on the others' next state, so it cannot belong to the structure however closely it watches. The same party, once it is read by even one other, becomes a candidate member, in the core in about a quarter of forms. What moves a party from outside to inside is whether it is read.

Carried to the contact center, the prior predicts two things. The supervisor monitoring the queue is a spectator on the whole-system coordination, present but not a member, because the agent and the classifier do not adjust their next action to the supervisor in the moment. And the customer may be the object of the coordination rather than a party to it, sorted and routed without co-determining where it goes. Both predictions are stated here so the field can break them.

The prior is held open, and the conditions of departure are named in advance. The supervisor would depart from the prior by turning out to be read: if agents adjust their handling to the fact of being watched, route a hard contact differently because the supervisor is on the floor, or pull a contact a particular way knowing it will be reviewed, then the supervisor's watching feeds back into the agent's action and the supervisor is read after all. The customer would depart by co-determining the routing: if how the customer answers the classifier, repeats themselves, escalates, or games the menu actually shifts which queue and which agent the contact reaches, then the customer is a party to its own routing and not only its object. Either departure is the finding. The prior's clean prediction is a baseline the field is invited to overturn, and the overturning is what the study is built to catch.

**Research question.** In customer-service triage, which of the four parties — customer, automated classifier, agent, supervisor — are members of the coordination that handles a contact, and which only watch it? Two lines follow. First, is the supervisor read: do agents and the routing adjust to the supervisor's watching in the running of a contact, and if so, through what practices? Second, is the customer read: does the customer co-determine the routing of its own contact, or is it sorted as an object by the classifier and the agent?

---

**Expected contributions**

The study is exploratory and produces grounded description, so its contributions are framed as sharpened questions and worked cases.

To theory, the study offers an empirical test of a structural prediction about membership in coordination. The prior holds that a party read by no one cannot be a member, and the contact center is a site where that prediction has teeth, because it contains a party — the supervisor — who reads everything and is read by little, and a second party — the customer — whose status as member or object is genuinely unsettled. Describing what the supervisor and the customer actually do in the running of a contact gives the membership question a concrete instance. A finding that the supervisor is read, that surveillance pulls the watcher into the work it watches, would mark a specific way the prior fails, and naming that mechanism is worth more than confirming the prior again.

To the study of algorithmic management and surveillance, the work brings a structural lens to a setting usually read through control and resistance. The literature establishes that watching service workers shapes how they work. This study asks what that shaping does to the watcher's own position in the coordination, which is a question the control framing does not pose. If being watched changes the agent's action, the surveillance relation runs in both directions in a sense the field has not named, and the supervisor's standing depends on it.

To practice, a close account of who coordinates a contact and who watches it informs how contact centers are staffed and how supervision is designed. Supervision is built on the assumption that watching the queue improves the work. Whether and how the watching enters the work is exactly what the study observes, and the answer bears on what supervisors should do and on whether the dashboard makes them members or leaves them outside.

**Literature review**

Four conversations bear on this study, and reading them together locates the opening. Work on algorithmic management establishes that automated systems direct and surveil service workers, but treats the supervisor and the system as agents of control without asking whether they are members of the coordination they manage. Work on surveillance and the watcher establishes that being watched changes the watched, which is the mechanism by which a spectator might become a member, but has not framed it as a question about coordinative membership. Work on coordination and articulation work supplies the concept of membership as accomplished through mutual adjustment, but has not taken up the four-cornered triage case with a dedicated watcher. Work on the customer as co-producer establishes that the served party sometimes participates in the service, which is the second open question here. None has asked which parties in algorithmic triage are members of the coordination and which only watch, which is where this study works.

*Algorithmic management and the surveillance of service work.* Automated systems now direct, evaluate, and discipline the people who work through them, and the synthesis of this terrain maps six mechanisms by which algorithms control work — restricting, recommending, recording, rating, replacing, and rewarding — across the employment relationship (Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020). The contact center is among the oldest sites of this control. The electronic panopticon of the call center was described early as a regime in which every call, pause, and idle second is logged and made available to a supervisor, so that the worker is watched continuously and knows it (Fernie & Metcalf, 1998; Bain & Taylor, 2000). Surveillance capitalism extends the analysis to the rendering of behavior into data that others act on, the watched party turned into a source of prediction for parties it cannot see (Zuboff, 2019). This conversation establishes the asymmetry the present study starts from: the agent and the classifier are watched and recorded, the supervisor watches and records. What it has not asked is whether the watching makes the supervisor a member of the work or leaves the supervisor outside it. The control framing positions the supervisor as an agent acting on the worker, which presumes the supervisor's standing the study sets out to examine. The membership question is orthogonal to control, and the contact center is where it can be posed against a literature that has mapped the same site for other purposes.

*The watcher, the watched, and being-watched.* That the watched party adjusts to being watched is the core insight of the surveillance tradition, and it is the mechanism by which a spectator could turn out to be a member. Foucault's reading of the panopticon holds that the watched internalizes the gaze and governs themselves under the assumption of being seen, so the watcher need not act to have an effect (Foucault, 1977). Brought into the workplace, this becomes the claim that electronic surveillance and peer scrutiny together produce a self-disciplining worker, with the team itself enlisted as a watcher (Sewell, 1998; Sewell & Wilkinson, 1992; Barker, 1993). Goffman's account of the gaze supplies the interactional version: a party who knows itself observed manages its conduct for the observer, performing for an audience whose watching shapes the performance (Goffman, 1959, 1963). Read against the prior, this conversation supplies exactly the departure condition. The prior says a party read by no one makes no difference. The surveillance literature says the watched adjust to the watcher, which means the watcher is, after all, read — the agent's action is a function of the supervisor's gaze. Whether that adjustment happens in the moment-to-moment running of a contact, in a way that would pull the supervisor into the coordinative core, is an empirical question this conversation frames but the contact-center studies have not asked in these terms. The study takes the surveillance literature's central finding as the hypothesis that would break the prior, and goes to the field to see whether it holds at the grain that membership requires.

*Coordination and articulation work.* What makes a party a member of joint work, as against an onlooker, is the question the sociology of work has studied under the heading of articulation. Strauss (1988) named the articulation work by which interdependent tasks are meshed into a functioning whole, the arranging and aligning that makes production run, and later work developed the analysis of such labor as routinely invisible and unrecognized (Star & Strauss, 1999). The point this conversation contributes is that coordination is accomplished through mutual adjustment among parties who act on one another, and a party belongs to it by acting in it, not by being near it. Suchman's analysis of situated action carries the same lesson into the moment: a plan does not execute a coordination, the parties improvise it in real time against the particulars in front of them, and what each does is shaped by what the others just did (Suchman, 1987, 2007). This gives the study its criterion of membership. A party is a member of the coordination of a contact if its action shifts what another party does next in the handling of that contact, and a spectator if it takes everything in and changes nothing downstream. The criterion is what makes the supervisor's status a real empirical question, because the dashboard makes the supervisor central to the queue's management while leaving open whether the supervisor's watching enters any single contact's handling. The articulation literature has studied coordination among parties who act on one another and has named the invisible work that holds it together. It has not taken up the case of a party wired in to watch the whole and positioned to act on none of it, which is the supervisor's case here.

*The customer as co-producer, or as the object of service.* The served party's place in service work is the study's second open question, and the service literature is split on it in a way that maps the question precisely. One line treats the customer as a co-producer who supplies labor, information, and judgment that the service cannot proceed without, a participant in the production of the outcome (Bishop & Waring, 2019; Orlikowski & Scott, 2015). Bishop and Waring (2019) draw the distinction that bears most directly here, between the patient as a boundary object that clinicians coordinate around and the patient as a boundary subject who takes part in coordinating their own care, and they show the same person can move between the two. Orlikowski and Scott (2015) trace how the rating systems that intermediate service reconfigure who counts and what the served party contributes. Against this, the older image of the service triangle positions the customer as the party whose demands are managed, sometimes as the one absorbed or blamed within the triad rather than empowered by it (Korczynski, 2009). The split is the study's question stated as a literature. In triage, the customer either co-determines the routing — answering the classifier, escalating, repeating, gaming the menu, in ways that move the contact — or is sorted as an object, read by the classifier and the agent and routing nowhere of its own doing. Which one holds is observable in how a contact actually moves through the system, and the prior's prediction that the customer is the object of the coordination is one this conversation gives the study both the concepts and the counter-case to test.

*The opening.* The four conversations leave a specific gap. Algorithmic-management research maps surveillance in the contact center but presumes the supervisor's standing and leaves the question of whether the supervisor is a member of the work unasked. The surveillance literature establishes that the watched adjust to the watcher, which is the mechanism that could make a spectator a member, but has not framed it as a question of coordinative membership. The articulation literature defines membership as accomplished through mutual action but has not taken up a party positioned only to watch. The service literature splits on whether the customer participates or is processed, which is the study's second question, but has not joined it to the membership of the watcher. What none has asked is which parties in algorithmic triage are members of the coordination and which only watch, with a stated prior — the watcher is never in the core, the customer may be the object — and stated conditions under which the field would break it. That is the opening this study enters, to describe who coordinates a contact and who watches it, and to report where the field departs from what the prior predicts.

**Method**

This is a qualitative, interpretive study designed to build theory from what the parties to customer-service triage do and say about how a contact is handled. The study carries a prior into the field, that a party read by no one is a spectator and not a member, with the supervisor as the expected spectator and the customer as the expected object. It follows an abductive logic. It does not test the prior as a hypothesis to confirm, and it treats the prior as a sensitizing concept (Blumer, 1954) that orients attention toward who reads whom in the running of a contact, while leaving the substantive account of membership to emerge from observed practice and the parties' own accounts. The named departure conditions — the supervisor read, the customer co-determining the routing — are the cases the analysis watches for most closely. The analytic procedures follow the Gioia methodology (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013), suited to a phenomenon that is processual, meaning-laden, and not captured by an existing construct.

*Site selection.* The study will be conducted in the contact-center operations of one or two firms in a single industry and region, chosen because they run the four-party arrangement the study examines in a form that can be observed. An automated classifier sorts the contact, an agent handles it, a supervisor watches the queue from a dashboard, and the customer is on the other end. The setting makes the coordination observable in a way the algorithmic third elsewhere does not allow: the agent's work happens at a desk and on calls that can be watched and asked about, the supervisor's watching happens on a floor and a screen that can be sat beside, and the classifier's output is recorded in the routing system. Confining the study to one or two centers in a single industry keeps the technology, the regulatory environment, and the staffing model comparable across participants, so that variation in who coordinates a contact is not confounded with variation in the firms. Where two centers are used, a contrast between a center with tight, automated routing and one where agents and supervisors retain discretion offers a way to observe whether the supervisor's and customer's positions shift with how much the system commits, without making that contrast the spine of the design.

*Sources of data.* The study draws on four sources, with interviews and observation as the primary sources and routing logs and documents providing triangulation. The first is semi-structured interviews with 25 to 30 participants across the four roles — agents, supervisors, the people who build and tune the classifier, and, where access permits, customers who have recently been routed — sampled for variation in tenure, queue type, and the acuity of the contacts handled. Interviews will run 60 to 90 minutes, be recorded with consent, and be transcribed verbatim. The guide will evolve as early analysis surfaces new lines, and sampling will continue until interviews stop yielding new second-order themes. A draft guide appears in the Exhibit. The second source is observation: sitting beside agents as they handle contacts, beside supervisors as they watch the queue, and in the room during handoffs and escalations, recording in field notes how the contact moves, how the agent reads the classifier's assignment, whether and how the agent adjusts to the supervisor's presence, and what the customer does that shifts the routing. The observer will take jottings during observation and expand them into full notes immediately after, in a non-participant role. The third source is routing and monitoring logs, which record the classifier's assignment, the queue, the transfers, and what the supervisor's dashboard displayed, used to corroborate what observation and interviews report about how a contact moved and what each party could see. The fourth source is documentary material: the routing rules and category definitions the classifier applies, the supervisor's monitoring protocols and quality-review forms, and the scripts and escalation policies agents work under, used to establish each party's stated rule. The study will proceed under institutional review board approval, obtain informed consent from participating staff, and obtain consent from or exclude customers whose contacts are observed, with a protocol for suspending observation whenever it would intrude on a contact in distress or expose protected information.

*Procedures.* Analysis will proceed abductively, cycling between the data and the prior, and will be organized as a Gioia-style data structure (Gioia et al., 2013). Open coding will build first-order categories in the parties' own terms, staying close to how they describe what they do, for instance how an agent describes handling a contact differently when the supervisor is on the floor, or how a customer describes getting routed where they wanted. Constant comparison will group these into second-order themes that name the practices analytically — adjusting to being watched, gaming the menu, overriding the classifier — and the themes will be examined for the aggregate dimensions they compose. The central analytic move is to determine, for each party, whether its action shifts what another party does next in the handling of a contact, the criterion of membership the articulation literature supplies (Strauss, 1988; Suchman, 2007). Where the parties describe the same arrangement differently — a supervisor who says watching changes nothing, an agent who says it changes everything — the disagreement will be retained and analyzed as data about whose account of the coordination governs, following the lab's treatment of disagreement as a finding. The product will be a data structure tracing the path from the parties' language to the analytic themes, with a table of representative quotations for each theme so a reader can audit the move from evidence to claim (Pratt, 2009).

*Trustworthiness.* The study will pursue the four criteria of trustworthiness (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). For credibility, it will triangulate interviews against observation and routing logs, return emerging themes to participants for correction through member checks, and engage the centers over a sustained period. For transferability, it will provide thick description of the centers, the routing technology, and the industry context, so readers can judge where the findings travel. For dependability, it will commit the interview guide, the initial coding scheme, and the named departure conditions to a dated record before fieldwork begins and keep an audit trail of analytic decisions, so the path from question to claim is inspectable and visibly not retrofitted. For confirmability, the data structure and the quotation table will tie each claim to evidence. The researcher will keep a reflexive memo on a risk specific to this study, that the prior could lead the analysis to see a spectator where an agent's adjustment to the supervisor is real but quiet, and the memo will track instances that cut against the prior — the supervisor read, the customer co-determining — as closely as instances that fit it, so the prior is held open rather than confirmed.

**Exhibit A — Draft interview guide**

*Opening (rapport, context)*

1. Walk me through a typical shift, from when you start to when you hand off. What does it actually involve?
2. How did you come to this work, and how did you learn to do it well?

*How a contact gets sorted*

3. When a contact comes to you, how do you know what it is and where it belongs? What does the system tell you, and what do you figure out yourself?
4. Tell me about a recent contact where the system put it in the wrong place. How did you notice, and what did you do?
5. Walk me through who decides where a contact goes. The classifier, you, the customer, someone else — where does the decision actually get made?

*The customer's part*

6. When a customer is being routed, what can they do to change where they end up? Do they ever steer it, work around the menu, or push to get somewhere?
7. Tell me about a time a customer got themselves routed somewhere by how they answered or what they said. What happened?
8. Is the customer mostly being sorted, or are they part of sorting themselves? Walk me through a case of each.

*Being watched, and watching*

9. (Agents) When the supervisor is watching the queue, does it change how you handle a contact? Tell me about a time it did.
10. (Agents) Is there a contact you would handle one way if no one were watching and another way knowing it will be reviewed? Walk me through it.
11. (Supervisors) When you watch the queue, what are you doing — and does the watching change how the agents work, or do they work the same whether you are there or not?
12. (Supervisors) Tell me about a time your watching changed how a contact got handled. And a time you watched something go wrong and could only watch.

*Position and membership*

13. When a contact gets handled from start to finish, who is actually part of making it happen, and who is mostly looking on? Where do you sit in that?
14. Some people might say the supervisor passes nothing into a contact, just keeps score. Others might say the supervisor is in every contact through how agents work under the watching. From your experience, which is closer?

*Closing*

15. What do you wish people understood about who really runs a contact through this system? Is there anything I should have asked but did not?
