# Bowen — Stage 1 exegesis: the claims in his own words

Source: Murray Bowen, *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice* (New York: Jason Aronson, 1978), cited by page.
The triangle passages come from three papers collected there: "The Use of Family Theory in Clinical
Practice" (1966; pp. 147–181), "Theory in the Practice of Psychotherapy" (1976; pp. 337–387), and "On the
Differentiation of Self" (1972; pp. 467–528). Kerr and Bowen, *Family Evaluation* (1988), is cited for the
systematized restatement.

## C1 — The triangle is the smallest stable relationship system

> "The triangle, a three-person emotional configuration, is the molecule or the basic building block of any
> emotional system, whether it is in the family or any other group. The triangle is the smallest stable
> relationship system. A two-person system may be stable as long as it is calm, but when anxiety increases,
> it immediately involves the most vulnerable other person to become a triangle." (373)

Structural restatement. Two parties hold together while nothing disturbs them; under disturbance a third is
drawn in, and the three hold where the two would not. Stability is the capacity to remain a system under
anxiety.

## C2 — Calm: a close twosome and an outsider; tension: the third is involved

> "In periods of calm, the triangle is made up of a comfortably close twosome and a less comfortable outsider.
> The twosome works to preserve the togetherness, lest one become uncomfortable and form a better togetherness
> elsewhere. The outsider seeks to form a togetherness with one of the twosome, and there are numerous moves
> to accomplish this." (373)
>
> "Moderate tension states in the twosome are characteristically felt by one, while the other is oblivious.
> It is the uncomfortable one who initiates a new equilibrium toward more comfortable togetherness for self."
> (373)
>
> "When emotional tension in a two-person system exceeds a certain level, it 'triangles' a third person,
> permitting the tension to shift within the triangle." (174)

Structural restatement. In calm the unit is the twosome and the third is outside it, attending to the two
who do not attend back. Under tension one insider turns to the third, and the unit becomes three.

## C3 — Under stress the outside position is the comfortable one

> "In periods of stress, the outside position is the most comfortable and most desired position. In stress,
> each works to get the outside position to escape tension in the twosome." (373–374)
>
> "A triangle can contain much more tension without involving another person because the tension can shift
> around three relationships." (Kerr and Bowen 1988, as restated by the Bowen Center)

Structural restatement. Anxiety at the outsider costs the system least; anxiety at an insider costs it most.
The triangle absorbs disturbance by moving it among its three sides.

## C4 — Interlocking triangles

> "When tension in the triangle is too great for the threesome, it involves others to become a series of
> interlocking triangles." (373)
>
> "When it is not possible to shift forces in the triangle, one of the involved twosome triangles in a fourth
> person, leaving the former third person aside for reinvolvement later. The emotional forces duplicate the
> exact patterns in the new triangle." (374)
>
> "An emotional system is composed of a series of interlocking triangles. The emotional tension system can
> shift to any of the old preestablished circuits." (174)

Structural restatement. A fourth party is drawn in by the same move that drew the third; the larger system
remains a set of triangles rather than becoming a unit of four, and spreading the tension stabilizes it.

## C5 — Detriangling: contact without reactivity resolves the twosome's tension

> "The emotional forces within a triangle are in constant motion, from minute to minute and hour to hour, in a
> series of chain reaction moves as automatic as emotional reflexes." (470)
>
> Bowen's clinical claim, stated at 174–175 and throughout: a tense twosome in contact with a third who
> "remains emotionally detached from the twosome yet in good contact with them" will find that "the tension
> between the two can resolve"; Bowen calls this "the magic of family psychotherapy" (175, as summarized in
> the secondary literature; the phrasing here follows the summary, the page his).

Structural restatement. A third who is read by both and reads both, but does not react, is not part of the
emotional unit; its presence lets the twosome hold under the anxiety that would otherwise have triangled.

## Claims not carried to hypotheses

Differentiation of self as a scale (472–475); the family projection process and multigenerational
transmission; sibling position; societal regression; the therapist's technique. These are the theory around
the triangle, not further claims about its structure.
