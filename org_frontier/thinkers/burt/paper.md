# The hole is a cliff: Burt's structural holes under exact Φ

## Abstract

Burt's theory of structural holes says a broker between two disconnected contacts holds an advantage that a
tie between them removes, that the same disconnection generates information benefits and control benefits,
that contacts with the same contacts are redundant whether or not they are tied, that advantage falls with a
graded measure of constraint, that a rival spanning the same hole removes structural autonomy, and — in the
later work — that closure inside the broker's group is what lets brokerage beyond it pay. This paper renders
the broker as a Boolean node that combines (AND) or chooses between (OR) its contacts, renders ties among
contacts as added sources, and reads each configuration with exact IIT-4.0 Φ, the major complex, and a
quantity for the broker's contribution: the Φ the whole's core loses when the broker is deleted. Five
hypotheses were fixed before computing. The hole is a cliff. A broker across two disconnected contacts adds
2.000 and holds a positional advantage of 2.000; one tie between them drives both to zero, evicting the
information broker and turning the control broker into a peer in a clique (H1 partial). Structurally
equivalent contacts count as one, but the broker adds the same 2.000 with one contact, two equivalent
contacts, or two nonredundant ones (H2 refuted). Over three contacts with zero to three ties, Burt's
constraint rises smoothly and value added falls 3.000 → 0 → 0 → 0 (H3 partial). A rival spanning the same
hole makes the whole reducible and each broker's presence a loss of 1.811 (H4 confirmed). Closure within the
group, across the hole, or everywhere all zero the broker's contribution; only the open network pays (H5
refuted). Burt's 1992 theory survives as a threshold claim; his graded measure and his 2001 reconciliation
with closure do not survive on this criterion. Results are about Boolean models, not about any manager.

## Introduction

Burt took Simmel's third who benefits and made a measurable theory of it. The unit is the structural hole,
"a relationship of nonredundancy between two contacts," and the claim is that a player whose relationships
span holes enjoys information and control benefits that players in dense networks do not (Burt, 1992: 18).
The theory comes with a graded measure, network constraint, that rises as one's contacts are tied to one
another, and with two decades of evidence that low-constraint managers are promoted faster, paid more, and
rated more creative (Burt, 2000; 2004). It also comes with a foil, Coleman's (1988) argument that closure
is the source of social capital, and with Burt's own settlement of the quarrel: "brokerage across structural
holes is the source of value added, but closure can be critical to realizing the value buried in structural
holes" (Burt, 2001: 31).

Each of these is a claim about what a position contributes and how that contribution changes with the ties
around it. The lab's criterion answers such claims directly. A configuration of parties with update rules is
an irreducible whole when its Φ is positive; its major complex is the set of parties it cannot be cut from
without loss; and the Φ the core loses when a party is deleted is what that party adds. The lab's q214 study
(probe #368) already put Burt's broker through one of these tests and found it *contingent*: a relay between
two parties stays in the core only while the direct tie is forbidden. The same study found a conjunctive
integrator *intrinsic*: it stays when a one-sided bypass opens. Burt says "the structural holes that generate
information benefits also generate control benefits" (1992: 30) — one condition, two benefits. The lab's two
verdicts say the two benefits may not stand on the same ground.

This paper runs five of Burt's claims: closure by cohesion against both brokers (H1), structural equivalence
as redundancy (H2), the constraint gradient (H3), the rival broker (H4), and the closure-within-brokerage-
beyond optimum (H5). Burt's account and its rendering come first, then hypotheses, methods, results, and
what they say about where his theory holds.

## Burt's account

Burt's first chapter builds the theory from a production equation. Profit is investment times rate of
return, and a player's relationships are social capital whose rate of return depends on their structure.
Contacts are redundant "to the extent that they lead to the same people, and so provide the same
information benefits" (1992: 17), and the separation between nonredundant contacts is the structural hole:
"a buffer, like an insulator in an electric circuit. As a result of the hole between them, the two contacts
provide network benefits that are in some degree additive rather than overlapping" (1992: 18).

Two conditions indicate a hole by its absence. Under cohesion, "two contacts are redundant to the extent that
they are connected by a strong relationship." Under structural equivalence, two contacts are redundant to the
extent that "they have the same contacts. Regardless of the relation between structurally equivalent people,
they lead to the same sources of information and so are redundant." Three contacts tied to one another and
three untied contacts who reach the same cluster both "provide one nonredundant contact at a cost of
maintaining three" (1992: 19).

The benefits are of two kinds. Information benefits — access, timing, referrals — come from reaching
separate flows of information. Control benefits come from the tertius gaudens, "an individual who profits
from the disunion of others," by "being the third between two or more players after the same relationship,
and being the third between players in two or more relations with conflicting demands" (1992: 30–31).
Control needs tension — "no tension, no tertius" (1992: 32) — and it needs players who "won't negotiate with
one another directly to the exclusion of the tertius" (1992: 33). The same holes generate both kinds of
benefit.

The measures are effective size and constraint. Contact *j* constrains player *i* by c_ij = (p_ij + Σ_q p_iq
p_qj)², where p_ij is the share of *i*'s relations invested in *j* and the sum runs over *i*'s other contacts
*q*; aggregate constraint C_i = Σ_j c_ij rises as *i*'s contacts are tied to one another, and advantage falls
with it (1992: 54–55). Structural autonomy is the summary: "numerous structural holes around your contacts and
none attached to yourself" — that is, contacts who cannot reach one another except through you, and no one
else who could replace you (1992: 45).

The later work adds closure. Coleman's dense networks create trust and sanctions, and Burt (2000; 2001;
2005) grants that this can be "critical to realizing the value buried in structural holes": social capital is
"closure within a group and brokerage beyond the group."

## From claims to forms

A **broker** E reads its contacts and is read by them. Burt's two benefits are two rules. The **information
broker** combines nonredundant contacts, E' = AND of contacts, since what they separately carry is additive.
The **control broker** chooses between substitutable contacts, E' = OR of contacts, since either bidder will
do. A **contact** reads E and any contact it is tied to, disjunctively — X' = OR of its sources — since
information from any source suffices. A **tie** between contacts (closure by cohesion) adds each to the
other's sources. A contact with no sources is a constant.

**Value added** by a party P is V(P) = Φ(major complex of the whole) − Φ(major complex of the network with P
and its edges deleted). It is Burt's phrase and the lab's quantity. **Positional advantage** is V(E) minus the
largest V among the contacts. **Membership** is E's presence in the major complex. **Constraint** C_E is
Burt's aggregate constraint with p_ij = 1/degree(i) on the undirected tie graph.

Five families follow. **Closure** (H1): each broker with two contacts, open (X' = E, Y' = E) and closed (X' =
E ∨ Y, Y' = E ∨ X). **Equivalence** (H2): E = X ∧ Y, with the contacts reading sources that read E back;
`equivalent` has one source Z read by both, `nonredundant` has Z₁ and Z₂, and `single` has one contact and
one source. **Constraint** (H3): E = X ∧ Y ∧ Z with 0, 1 (X–Y), 2 (X–Y, Y–Z), or 3 ties among the contacts,
which gives C_E = 0.333, 0.611, 0.840, 0.926. **Rival** (H4): E = X ∧ Y alone, or beside R = X ∧ Y with X' =
E ∨ R and Y' = E ∨ R. **Complement** (H5): E = A₁ ∧ A₂ ∧ O with group {E, A₁, A₂} and outsider O, in four
configurations — `open`, `within` (A₁–A₂ tied), `across` (A₁–O tied), `everywhere`.

## Hypotheses

Fixed in `hypotheses.md` before any form was run; the lab's prior beside each.

- **H1 (Burt) — closure removes the broker for both benefits.** Under a tie between the contacts, both
  brokers leave the core or add nothing. *Prior:* against on the information broker (intrinsic in q214).
- **H2 (Burt) — structural equivalence is redundancy.** Equivalent contacts: at most one in the core and V(E)
  at the single-contact value; nonredundant contacts: both in the core. *Prior:* with Burt on eviction.
- **H3 (Burt) — value added falls strictly with constraint.** *Prior:* open.
- **H4 (Burt) — a rival spanning the same hole removes autonomy.** V(E) falls and not both brokers are in the
  core. *Prior:* with Burt on V(E); open on membership.
- **H5 (Burt) — closure within, brokerage beyond.** V(E) is highest in `within`. *Prior:* open.

Decision rules are in `methods.md`.

## Methods

**Instrument.** Exact IIT-4.0 Φ (Albantakis et al., 2023) via PyPhi (Mayner et al., 2018), through the lab's
`org_frontier.probes.lib`: `verdict` for whole-system Φ_MIP and `major_complex` for the core and its Φ over
reachable states. The q214 bypass classifier (`contingency_test`) is reported for H1 alongside the symmetric
closure.

**Control.** Every probe first runs the conjunctive triad (A' = M, M' = A ∧ B, B' = M) and requires Φ =
2.000000 with core {A, M, B}. All five passed.

**Forms.** Specs of node → (combinator, sources), compiled to rules; deletion drops a node and its edges.
Three to five nodes; synchronous deterministic update.

**Procedure.** Five probes, one per hypothesis. Each prints the control line, per form Φ_MIP, core, core Φ,
core Φ without E, V(E), C_E, and per-node V with E's positional advantage, then a verdict. Results are saved
to `results/` and registered in `ci/reproduce.json` (probes #394–#398).

**Scope.** Results are about the Boolean models. No manager, market, or firm is measured.

### H1 — closure removes the broker for both benefits: PARTIAL

Open, the two brokers are indistinguishable: each is in the core with its contacts at Φ = 2.000, adds 2.000,
and holds a positional advantage of 2.000 over contacts who add nothing. Closed, they part. The information
broker is evicted: the contacts alone form an OR-dyad at Φ = 2.000 that excludes the triad (Φ_MIP = 0.830),
and E adds nothing. The control broker stays — but as something else. E = X ∨ Y beside X = E ∨ Y and Y = E ∨
X is a clique of three identical rules at Φ = 6.000, in which E adds 4.000 and so does every other member.
The advantage is zero. On the pre-registered rule (membership or value added) closure removes one broker of
two; on positional advantage it removes both. Burt's control benefit does not survive closure any better than
his information benefit; what survives is a seat in a group of equals.

The q214 one-sided bypass, run alongside, classifies both open brokers intrinsic with margin 0: when only one
contact gains a direct line, neither broker is displaced. Symmetric closure is the stronger test, and it is
the one Burt's "strong relationship" between contacts describes.

*Cross-reference.* The Coleman paper in this series (`../coleman/paper.md`, probe #430) re-reads these four
numbers for the collective rather than the broker: closure holds or raises the core's Φ (2 → 2; 2 → 6) and
takes the broker's positional advantage from 2.000 to 0 for both brokers, which is Coleman's (1988) claim
confirmed on Burt's own forms. The same paper finds that in a sanction form closure lowers the whole's Φ,
so which side the collective is on depends on whether the closed ties are returns among peers or
reinforcements among sanctioners.

### H2 — structural equivalence is redundancy: REFUTED

The equivalent form behaves as Burt says. X and Y both copy Z; the whole of four has Φ_MIP = 1.000 and its
major complex {E, Y, Z} at 2.000 excludes it; one of the pair is evicted, and each contact's own V is zero.
The nonredundant form does not. Z₁ and Z₂ are different sources, the whole of five is irreducible at Φ_MIP =
2.000 — and its major complex is reported as {E, Y, Z₂}, also at 2.000. Whole and sub-triad tie, and the
exclusion postulate picks one. On the pre-registered rule both contacts are not in the core, so the
hypothesis fails; what the numbers say is that the five-node whole is a whole, at exactly the Φ of its part.
What does not tie is the broker's value: V(E) = 2.000 with one contact, with two equivalent contacts, and
with two nonredundant ones. On this measure a second contact across a hole adds nothing to what the broker
adds — the benefits are not additive in this topology, whether or not the contacts are redundant.

### H3 — value added falls strictly with constraint: PARTIAL

Burt's constraint rises in four steps, 0.333 → 0.611 → 0.840 → 0.926. The broker's value added falls in one:
3.000 with three untied contacts, then 0 at one tie, 0 at two, 0 at three. The first tie takes everything.
At k = 1 the whole is reducible (Φ_MIP = 0) and the tied pair {X, Y} is the core; at k = 3 the contacts are an
OR-clique at Φ = 6.000, each adding 4.000, and the broker who combines them stands outside it with a
positional advantage of −4.000. The relation is monotone, as Burt says, and it is not graded, as his measure
is. Burt's constraint index measures how far the contacts have closed; the instrument reports only whether
they have begun to.

### H4 — a rival spanning the same hole removes autonomy: CONFIRMED

Alone, E adds 2.000. Beside a rival R with the same rule and the same contacts, the whole of four is
reducible, the largest complex is {R, Y} at Φ = 0.189, and every party's value added is −1.811: each broker's
presence lowers what the rest can integrate, because without either the other three are the conjunctive triad
at 2.000. Neither broker is in a core worth the name and neither has an advantage over the other. Burt's
"none attached to yourself" is confirmed in the strongest form the criterion allows: a hole at one's own end
does not reduce one's advantage, it removes the whole.

### H5 — closure within, brokerage beyond: REFUTED

Open — group untied, outsider untied — E adds 3.000 with an advantage of 2.000. Close the group (A₁–A₂) and
E adds nothing: the tied pair is the core and the whole is reducible. Close across the hole (A₁–O) and the
same. Close everywhere and the three contacts are a clique at 6.000 with E outside at −4.000. The
configuration Burt's later work names as the general form of social capital — closure within, brokerage
beyond — is on this criterion no better than closure across the hole, and both are worse than no closure at
all. Burt 1992 beats Burt 2001.

## Discussion

One pattern carries the five results. The broker's contribution is a threshold function of the ties among
his contacts: full at zero ties, zero at one. Burt's central intuition is right — "the hole is a buffer, like
an insulator" — and his phrase is more exact than his measure. An insulator either insulates or it does not.
Constraint, effective size, and the graded language of "to the extent that" describe a slope where the
instrument finds a cliff (H3), and the reconciliation with closure (H5) presupposes the slope: if closure
inside the group could coexist with a positive contribution from brokerage beyond it, the tied pair would
have to leave room for the broker, and it does not. The first tie among the contacts makes them a complex of
their own, and a complex of their own excludes the broker.

The mechanism is exclusion. Once two contacts read each other, the pair is an irreducible whole at Φ = 2.000
and the larger set containing the broker is either reducible (H3 k1, H5 within) or irreducible at a lower Φ
(H1 info_closed at 0.830, H3 k2 at 0.193). The major complex is the pair, and the broker who combines what the
pair already shares stands outside it. This is why the information broker is evicted and the control broker
is not (H1): the OR-broker's rule is the contacts' rule, so under closure he is one of them, and a clique of
three at 6.000 beats a dyad at 2.000. He keeps his seat by ceasing to be a broker. Positional advantage is the
quantity that sees this, and it is zero for both brokers under closure. Burt's "the same holes generate both
benefits" holds for the advantage and fails for the membership, and the direction is the reverse of the
lab's prior: q214's one-sided bypass kept the conjunctive integrator and would have dropped a relay; the
symmetric tie drops the integrator and keeps the chooser.

H4 is the theory's cleanest confirmation and shows what the criterion adds to it. Burt's structural
autonomy has two halves, holes around the contacts and none around oneself, and treats the second as a
matter of degree — secondary holes "between the contact and others outside the network who could replace the
contact." The instrument's answer is that a replacement does not divide the advantage; it annihilates the
whole. Two identical brokers across one hole leave a reducible system in which each is worth less than
nothing. Exchange-network experiments found that resources accumulate in brokers with exclusive partners
(Burt, 2000, citing Cook and Emerson); exclusivity here is not a bonus on brokerage but its condition of
existence.

H2 separates two things Burt's redundancy runs together. Structurally equivalent contacts do count as one:
the duplicate is evicted. But the broker gains nothing from a second contact even when it is nonredundant —
V(E) = 2.000 in all three forms — so the "additive" benefit of contacts across a hole is not visible in what
the broker adds to the whole's core, in this topology. Where the contacts read the broker directly (H3 k0,
H5 open), three contacts do give more than two (3.000 against 2.000). The addition depends on the contacts'
return path, not on their nonredundancy. And the nonredundant five-node whole is irreducible at exactly the
Φ of its sub-triad, which is the kind of tie Burt's concepts do not anticipate: a whole and a part equally
integrated, with the exclusion postulate deciding which is the complex.

What Burt's theory looks like after this: a claim about a threshold, confirmed; a claim about exclusivity,
confirmed in the strong form; a graded measure that indexes closure without indexing the broker's
contribution; and a settlement with Coleman that this criterion does not ratify. Coleman's closure produces
trust and sanctions, which are mechanisms with no rendering here, and Burt's 2001 claim is about them. On
structure alone, the broker's group cannot close without closing over him.

## Limitations

The forms are small and deterministic. Contacts read disjunctively and the information broker
conjunctively; a contact that combined its sources, or a broker that took a majority, would be other forms
with possibly other cliffs. Ties are symmetric and full; Burt's constraint is built for weighted and
asymmetric relations, and a weak tie between contacts might behave as a slope where a full tie behaves as a
step. Value added and positional advantage are the lab's quantities, chosen because Burt's phrase names one
of them, not because Burt used them. H2's nonredundant verdict rests on an exact tie in Φ between whole and
part. Burt's mechanisms for closure — trust, reputation, sanctions — have no rendering, so H5 tests the
structural claim and not the argument for it.

Results are about Boolean models. No manager, firm, or market is measured, and nothing here says how any
broker fares.

## Conclusion

Burt's structural hole is real on this criterion and it is binary. A broker across two disconnected contacts
adds a whole's worth of integration and holds it alone; a single tie between the contacts removes the
contribution entirely, evicts the broker who combines them, and turns the broker who chooses between them
into their equal. Structurally equivalent contacts are one contact, as Burt said, and so, for what the broker
adds, are nonredundant ones. His constraint measure grades a closure that the instrument reads as a
threshold. A rival across the same hole does not share the advantage but destroys the whole. And the later
settlement — closure within, brokerage beyond — has no support: any closure around the broker closes over
him. The theory that survives is the 1992 insulator, taken literally.

  Y = or(E, X).
- **H2**: single (E, X, Z): E = and(X), X = or(Z), Z = or(E). equivalent (E, X, Y, Z): E = and(X, Y), X = or(Z),
  Y = or(Z), Z = or(E). nonredundant (E, X, Y, Z₁, Z₂): X = or(Z₁), Y = or(Z₂), Z₁ = or(E), Z₂ = or(E).
- **H3** (E, X, Y, Z): E = and(X, Y, Z); each contact or(E, tied contacts); ties k0 ∅, k1 {XY}, k2 {XY, YZ},
  k3 {XY, YZ, XZ}.
- **H4**: alone as H1 info_open; rival (E, R, X, Y): E = and(X, Y), R = and(X, Y), X = or(E, R), Y = or(E, R).
- **H5** (E, A₁, A₂, O): E = and(A₁, A₂, O); open all or(E); within A₁ = or(E, A₂), A₂ = or(E, A₁); across A₁ =
  or(E, O), O = or(E, A₁); everywhere each contact or(E, both others).
