# Burt — Stage 2 literature

## Primary texts

*Structural Holes* (1992) turns Simmel's tertius into a theory of competitive advantage. Its first chapter,
reprinted the same year as "The Social Structure of Competition" and read in full for this paper, carries
the structural claims. A structural hole is "a relationship of nonredundancy between two contacts," "a
buffer, like an insulator in an electric circuit," across which benefits are "additive rather than
overlapping" (p. 18). Redundancy has two indicators, cohesion (the contacts are tied) and structural
equivalence (the contacts have the same contacts), and the second makes contacts redundant "regardless of
the relation between" them (p. 19). Information benefits — access, timing, referrals — and control benefits —
the tertius gaudens who "profits from the disunion of others" — are generated by "the structural holes"
alike (pp. 30–34); "no tension, no tertius." Chapter 2 gives the measures: effective size, and constraint
c_ij = (p_ij + Σ_q p_iq p_qj)², summed to an aggregate that rises with the ties among one's contacts (pp.
54–55). Structural autonomy closes the argument: "numerous structural holes around your contacts and none
attached to yourself" (p. 45).

The later work changes one thing. "The Network Structure of Social Capital" (2000) and "Structural Holes
versus Network Closure as Social Capital" (2001) concede Coleman's (1988) closure argument a role:
"brokerage across structural holes is the source of value added, but closure can be critical to realizing
the value buried in structural holes." *Brokerage and Closure* (2005) makes this the definition — "closure
within a group and brokerage beyond the group" — and "Structural Holes and Good Ideas" (2004) gives the
mechanism on the brokerage side as vision.

## What the literature did with it

**The empirical program.** Burt's constraint index became the standard operationalization of brokerage,
and the finding that low-constraint managers are promoted faster, paid more, and rated more creative has
replicated across firms and countries (Burt 2000 reviews; Burt 2004 is the type case). The measure is
graded and the theory is a monotone claim about it.

**The foil.** Coleman (1988) argued that closure — dense ties among one's contacts — creates social capital
by making sanctions and reputation possible. Burt's 2001 reconciliation keeps brokerage as the source of
value and gives closure the role of realizing it; the mechanisms "remain distinct."

**The neighbours.** Granovetter's (1973) bridge is a hole spanned by a weak tie; Burt reads the weak-tie
argument as a special case in which tie strength is a correlate of the hole and the hole is the cause (pp.
26–30). Simmel's tertius gaudens and the exchange-network experiments (Cook and Emerson, cited by Burt 2000)
supply the control side.

**In the lab.** q214 (probe #368) classified Burt's structural-hole broker with the bypass-counterfactual and
found it *contingent*: a relay held in the core by the forbidden tie, gone when the tie is restored. The same
probe classified a conjunctive integrator *intrinsic*: it stays under a one-sided bypass. That difference is
the opening for this paper, since Burt says one condition generates both benefits.

## The open gap

Burt's theory is a set of claims about a position: what a broker adds, and how that changes as the ties
around him change. The lab's criterion answers with a quantity — the Φ a party's presence adds to the whole's
core — and a membership. Three of Burt's claims are graded or comparative in a way no previous lab probe
tested: that advantage falls monotonically with constraint, that a rival spanning the same hole removes
autonomy, and that closure inside the group plus a hole beyond it is the optimum. Two are about counting:
that structurally equivalent contacts are one, and that closure by cohesion removes the broker for both
benefits. The gap is to render the broker's two rules — combining (AND) and choosing (OR) — and run the five.
