# Burt — Stage 1 exegesis: the claims in his own words

Sources: Ronald S. Burt, *Structural Holes: The Social Structure of Competition* (Harvard University Press,
1992), cited as Burt (1992); its first chapter was reprinted the same year as "The Social Structure of
Competition" in Nohria and Eccles, eds., *Networks and Organizations* (Harvard Business School Press,
57–91), and the reprint's full text was read and is cited by its own pages as Burt (1992b). The book's
pagination for the same passages, as given by secondary sources, is added in brackets. Burt (2000), "The
Network Structure of Social Capital," *Research in Organizational Behavior* 22, and Burt (2001), "Structural
Holes versus Network Closure as Social Capital," give the closure claim. Burt (2004), "Structural Holes and
Good Ideas," *AJS* 110(2), gives the vision mechanism.

## C1 — The hole: nonredundancy is an insulator, and its benefits are additive

> "It will be convenient to have a term for the separation between nonredundant contacts. I use the term
> 'structural hole.' Nonredundant contacts are connected by a structural hole. A structural hole is a
> relationship of nonredundancy between two contacts. The hole is a buffer, like an insulator in an electric
> circuit. As a result of the hole between them, the two contacts provide network benefits that are in some
> degree additive rather than overlapping." (1992b: 65 [1992: 18])
>
> "Under the cohesion criterion, two contacts are redundant to the extent that they are connected by a
> strong relationship. A strong relationship indicates the absence of a structural hole." (1992b: 66
> [1992: 18])
>
> "The structural holes that generate information benefits also generate control benefits, giving certain
> players an advantage in negotiating their relationships." (1992b: 76 [1992: 30])

Structural restatement. The party between two contacts who are not tied to each other holds a position the
contacts cannot reproduce. A tie between the contacts (closure by cohesion) removes it. The same
disconnection is the condition of both benefits — combining what the contacts separately know (information)
and choosing between them (control).

## C2 — Structural equivalence is redundancy without a tie

> "Two people are structurally equivalent to the extent that they have the same contacts. Regardless of the
> relation between structurally equivalent people, they lead to the same sources of information and so are
> redundant. Where cohesion concerns direct connection, structural equivalence concerns indirect connection
> by mutual contact. ... The three contacts have no direct ties with one another. They are nonredundant by
> cohesion. But each leads you to the same cluster of more distant players. ... Both networks in Figure 2-2
> provide one nonredundant contact at a cost of maintaining three." (1992b: 66 [1992: 19])

Structural restatement. Two contacts with no tie between them who read the same source are one contact, not
two. The broker who spans them spans no hole.

## C3 — The tertius: control from the disunion of others

> "The tertius gaudens is 'the third who benefits' (Simmel 1923 [1902]) ... the phrase describes an
> individual who profits from the disunion of others. There are two tertius strategies: being the third
> between two or more players after the same relationship, and being the third between players in two or
> more relations with conflicting demands. The first, and simpler, strategy is the familiar economic
> bargaining between buyer and seller. Where two or more players want to buy something, the seller can play
> their bids against one another to get a higher price." (1992b: 76 [1992: 30–31])
>
> "There is a presumption of tension here. Control emerges from tertius brokering tension between other
> players. No tension, no tertius." (1992b: 78 [1992: 32])
>
> "Successful application of the tertius strategies involves bringing together players who are willing to
> negotiate, have sufficiently comparable resources to view one another's preferences as valid, but won't
> negotiate with one another directly to the exclusion of the tertius." (1992b: 79 [1992: 33])

Structural restatement. The control broker chooses between substitutable contacts — either will do — and
holds the position only while the contacts do not deal directly.

## C4 — Constraint and structural autonomy

> "A player's entrepreneurial opportunities are enhanced by a relationship to the extent that (1) the player
> has invested substantial time and energy to secure a connection with the contact, and (2) there are many
> structural holes around the contact ensuring a high rate of return on the investment. ... the rate of return
> keyed to structural holes is a product of the extent to which there are (1) many primary structural holes
> between the contact and others in the player's network, and (2) many secondary structural holes between
> the contact and others outside the network who could replace the contact." (1992b: 82–83 [1992: 44–45])
>
> "There is also the issue of structural holes around the player. As the holes around contacts provide
> information and control benefits to the player, holes around the player can be developed by contacts for
> their benefit. Developing entrepreneurial opportunities depends on having numerous structural holes around
> your contacts and none attached to yourself. These considerations come together in the concept of
> structural autonomy. Players with relationships free of structural holes at their own end and rich in
> structural holes at the other end are structurally autonomous." (1992b: 83 [1992: 45])

The formal measure (1992: 54–55): contact *j* constrains player *i* by c_ij = (p_ij + Σ_q p_iq p_qj)², *q* ≠
*i, j*, where p_ij is the proportion of *i*'s relations invested in *j*; aggregate constraint C_i = Σ_j c_ij
rises as *i*'s contacts are tied to one another, and the player's advantage falls with it.

Structural restatement. Two graded claims. Advantage falls monotonically with constraint, i.e., with the
ties among one's contacts. And a hole "attached to yourself" — a second party who spans the same contacts
and could replace you — is the loss of autonomy.

## C5 — Brokerage and closure are complements

> "Brokerage across structural holes is the source of value added, but closure can be critical to realizing
> the value buried in structural holes." (2001: 31; the same sentence as "Structural holes are the source of
> value added, but network closure can be essential to realizing the value buried in the holes," 2000:
> abstract)
>
> "Closure is a complement to brokerage such that the two together define social capital in a general way in
> terms of closure within a group and brokerage beyond the group." (2005: Introduction)

Structural restatement. Of the ways a broker's group and its outside contact can be tied, the configuration
in which the broker's own group is closed and the tie beyond it is a hole is the one in which the broker adds
the most.

## Claims not carried to hypotheses

The information benefits of access, timing, and referrals (1992b: 62–63); the efficiency and effectiveness
design principles (1992b: 69–72); the vision mechanism — "people who stand near the holes in a social
structure are at higher risk of having good ideas" (2004: 349) — which is a claim about cognition; the
weak-tie argument as a special case of holes (1992b: 73–74); and the market-level theory of structural
autonomy in industries (1992: ch. 3). These are about what brokers know or do, not about what a structure is.
