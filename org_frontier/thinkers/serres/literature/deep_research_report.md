# Serres — Stage 2 literature

## Primary text

*The Parasite* (1980; Schehr 1982) opens on La Fontaine's city rat and country rat, feasting on a tax
farmer's leftovers until a noise at the door scatters them, and reads the fable as a chain: the farmer
produces; the tax farmer takes; the city rat takes from him; the country rat takes from the city rat; and
the noise, "the ultimate parasite, through its interruption, wins the game" (4). Three senses of the French
*parasite* — the biological freeloader, the uninvited guest, and static on a line — are held together
deliberately: "a parasite is an abusive guest, an unavoidable animal, a break in a message" (8). The
relation these share is one-directional. "There is no exchange, nor will there be one. Abuse appears before
use. ... it always goes in the same direction. The same one is the host; the same one takes and eats" (7);
"just one relation appears, the simple, irreversible arrow" (8); and this arrow "is the atomic form of our
relations" (8). Cascades of parasites have a last position, and "a given parasite seeks to eject the parasite
on the level immediately superior to his own" (4); the cascade "collapses when P1 = P4" (4). The book's
theorem is about noise: "noise gives rise to a new system, an order that is more complex than the simple
chain. This parasite interrupts at first glance, consolidates when you look again" (14). The chapter "The
Excluded Third, Included" carries over from *Hermès* the thesis that two parties communicate by excluding
the noise between them and that the noise is nonetheless constitutive. Late in the book the quasi-object —
the ball, the *furet* — "when being passed, makes the collective, if it stops, it makes the individual"
(225); it is "an astonishing constructor of intersubjectivity" (227).

## What the literature did with it

**Serres studies.** Brown (2002) gives the standard reading of the parasite as Serres's operator of
translation and reads the noise theorem against Shannon (1948): noise, for Serres, is not the enemy of the
channel but its third party. Brown (2013) carries the figure into organization theory: the parasite as the
organizational relation that exchange theory cannot see — one-directional, abusive before useful,
constitutive rather than pathological — and the quasi-object as what makes a collective from individuals.
Neither asks which of the positions in a parasitic chain is the structure.

**Information theory.** Shannon's noise is a random variable added to a channel and reduces capacity. Serres
inverts the valence and keeps the picture: the third on the line. Whether noise the system makes itself
stands in the same place as noise from outside is not a question the picture asks.

**Sociology of the triad.** Simmel's *tertius gaudens* profits from a pair; Serres's parasite profits from a
host who does not profit from him. Both are figures of the third; Simmel's is a third to a relation, Serres's
is the relation.

## The open gap

Serres's claims are structural and testable in the lab's terms. The one-way arrow as the atom of relation
contradicts the lab's standing result that a reader not read back does not bind. The noise theorem says a
chain with no structure acquires one when interrupted, and the lab can say whether a complex appears and who
is in it. The excluded-included third asks whether noise on a channel is a member of the structure it
disturbs. The last-position claim asks where the complex sits in a cascade and whose deletion costs most.
The quasi-object asks whether circulation makes a whole of the players. None has been computed; the roster
question was "a third that only perturbs the channel: does noise on the edge bind or factor?" — and the
answer turns out to depend on where the noise comes from and on how the instrument reads a channel that is
open to the outside.
