# Girard — Stage 2 literature

## Primary text

*Deceit, Desire, and the Novel* (1961; Freccero 1965) opens on Don Quixote, who "no longer chooses the objects
of his own desire — Amadis must choose for him" (1–2), and from that reads a structure into the European
novel. Desire is not a straight line from subject to object; "the mediator is there, above that line,
radiating toward both the subject and the object," and "the spatial metaphor which expresses this triple
relationship is obviously the triangle" (2). Girard adds at once that "the triangle is no Gestalt. The real
structures are intersubjective" (2). The mediator's prestige "transfigures" the object (17). The book's
main variable is distance: *external* mediation when the mediator's and the subject's "spheres of
possibilities" do not touch, *internal* when they "penetrate each other" (9), and "we have only to vary the
distance, in the triangle, separating the mediator from the desiring subject" (9). In internal mediation
the mediator becomes a rival — "the impulse toward the object is ultimately an impulse toward the mediator;
in internal mediation this impulse is checked by the mediator himself since he desires, or perhaps
possesses, the object" (10) — and the subject's feeling toward him is hatred, "the most submissive reverence
and the most intense malice" (10–11). *Double mediation* is the limit: each rival copies "the copy of his
own desire," "two identical but opposite triangles are thus superimposed" (99), and "differences among
classes and individuals gradually dissolve" (122).

## What the literature did with it

**Girard studies.** Fleming (2004) gives the standard exposition; the later work (*Violence and the Sacred*,
1977) moves from the triangle to the mimetic crisis and the scapegoat, and the triangle becomes the seed of
a theory of culture. Wilmes (2017) places double mediation against Kojève's Hegel: Kojève too dismissed
"desire taken as a straight line directly connecting a subject to an object," and the doubles are the
master–slave dialectic without a resolution. None of this literature asks which of the three positions the
structure actually occupies; "intersubjective" is the answer, and it is left there.

**Sociology of the triad.** Simmel's *tertius* and Caplow's coalitions are the triadic neighbors, and the lab
has computed both. Girard differs in that the third is not a party to a relation between two but the origin
of one party's relation to the other — the object is desired *because of* the mediator, and the subject's
tie to the object is borrowed.

**Organization studies.** Mimetic desire enters as isomorphism (organizations imitate the prestigious) and as
status competition; the structural question — what the imitating firm, the model, and the contested resource
are *as a unit* — is not asked.

## The open gap

Girard states the structure as a triangle and then denies it is one: "the triangle is no Gestalt. The real
structures are intersubjective. They cannot be localised anywhere." Which parties are one, in a triangle of
desire, and how that changes as the mediator draws near, are questions with a criterion in the lab's
instrument. The lab has found (probe 9) that a worker's internal model of a counterpart displaces the
counterpart in the core — the same shape as Girard's internal mediation, where the mediator's desire stands
in for the object. The gap is to render subject, mediator, and object as nodes, imitation as reading, and
distance as how much the mediator reads the subject, and ask what Φ says: who is the structure in external
mediation, in internal mediation, in double mediation, and as distance varies.
