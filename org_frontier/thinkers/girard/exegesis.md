# Girard — Stage 1 exegesis: the claims in his own words

Source: René Girard, *Deceit, Desire, and the Novel: Self and Other in Literary Structure* (1961), trans.
Yvonne Freccero (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 1965), cited by page.

## C1 — Desire is triangular; the straight line is not essential

> "Don Quixote has surrendered to Amadis the individual's fundamental prerogative: he no longer chooses the
> objects of his own desire — Amadis must choose for him. ... We shall call this model the *mediator* of
> desire." (1–2)
>
> "The straight line is present in the desire of Don Quixote, but it is not essential. The mediator is there,
> above that line, radiating toward both the subject and the object. The spatial metaphor which expresses
> this triple relationship is obviously the triangle. The object changes with each adventure but the
> triangle remains." (2)
>
> "The mediator's prestige is imparted to the object of desire and confers upon it an illusory value.
> Triangular desire is the desire which transfigures its object." (17)

Structural restatement. The subject's desire is determined by the mediator's, not by the object; the
object's value is conferred by the mediator. Subject and object are joined through the mediator and not
directly.

## C2 — External and internal mediation: distance

> "We shall speak of *external mediation* when the distance is sufficient to eliminate any contact between
> the two spheres of possibilities of which the mediator and the subject occupy the respective centers. We
> shall speak of *internal mediation* when this same distance is sufficiently reduced to allow these two
> spheres to penetrate each other more or less profoundly." (9)
>
> "To achieve this double objective, we have only to vary the distance, in the triangle, separating the
> mediator from the desiring subject." (9)

Structural restatement. In external mediation the mediator is unaffected by the subject — Amadis does not
read Don Quixote. In internal mediation the mediator is affected: he sees the subject's desire and responds
to it. Distance is how much the mediator reads the subject.

## C3 — The object is a means of reaching the mediator

> "The impulse toward the object is ultimately an impulse toward the mediator; in internal mediation this
> impulse is checked by the mediator himself since he desires, or perhaps possesses, the object." (10)
>
> "The subject is torn between two opposite feelings toward his model — the most submissive reverence and
> the most intense malice. This is the passion we call *hatred*." (10–11)

Structural restatement. The real relation is subject–mediator; the object is where it is played out. In
internal mediation the mediator becomes a rival — the two are bound to each other, the object between them.

## C4 — Double mediation: each copies the copy of his own desire

> "In the world of internal mediation, the contagion is so widespread that everyone can become his
> neighbor's mediator without ever understanding the role he is playing. This person who is a mediator
> without realizing it may himself be incapable of spontaneous desire. Thus he will be tempted to copy the
> copy of his own desire. ... Two identical but opposite triangles are thus superimposed on each other.
> Desire circulates between the two rivals more and more quickly, and with every cycle it increases in
> intensity." (99)

Structural restatement. Subject and mediator each read the other's desire; neither reads the object. The
object is forgotten, and the pair is the structure.

## C5 — Doubles: differences dissolve

> "Double mediation is a melting-pot in which differences among classes and individuals gradually dissolve.
> It functions all the more efficiently because it does not even appear to affect diversity." (122)

Structural restatement. In double mediation subject and mediator become interchangeable: neither is the
model, each is the other's. In single internal mediation the roles remain distinct.

## Claims not carried to hypotheses

Metaphysical desire and the deviated transcendency (chapters 3–4); the novelistic conversion (chapter 12);
masochism and sadism (chapter 8); the later theory of the scapegoat. These are the theory built on the
triangle, not further claims about its structure.
