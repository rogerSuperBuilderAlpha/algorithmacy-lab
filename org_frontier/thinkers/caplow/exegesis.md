# Caplow — Stage 1 exegesis: the claims in his own words

Caplow's theory of coalitions in the triad is in three texts: "A Theory of Coalitions in the Triad"
(*American Sociological Review* 21(4), 1956, 489–493), "Further Development of a Theory of Coalitions in the
Triad" (*American Journal of Sociology* 64(5), 1959, 488–493), and *Two Against One: Coalitions in Triads*
(Prentice-Hall, 1968). The 1956 and 1959 articles are behind JSTOR; the quotations below are taken from
Shenoy's 1978 mathematical reformulation (*Journal of Mathematical Psychology* 18: 177–194), which reproduces
Caplow's four assumptions and his per-type analyses verbatim, and from *Two Against One* as quoted in the
secondary literature. Where a passage is known only through a secondary source, the source is named.

## The four assumptions (1956)

> "A.1. Members of a triad may differ in strength. A stronger member can control a weaker member and will
> seek to do so.
> A.2. Each member of the triad seeks control over the others. Control over two others is preferred to
> control over one other. Control over one other is preferred to control over none.
> A.3. Strength is additive. The strength of a coalition is equal to the sum of the strengths of its two
> members.
> A.4. The formation of coalitions takes place in an existing triadic situation, so that there is a
> precoalition condition in every triad. Any attempt by a stronger member to coerce a weaker member into
> joining a nonadvantageous coalition will provoke the formation of an advantageous coalition to oppose the
> coercion." (Caplow 1956, as reproduced in Shenoy 1978: 183)

## C1 — The eight types and the coalition each predicts

Caplow's claim is that "the formation of coalitions depends upon the initial distribution of power, and other
things being equal, may be predicted under certain assumptions when the initial distribution of power is
known" (1956, quoted in Shenoy 1978: 183). Six types in 1956, two more in 1959; A is the strongest member.
The predictions, as tabulated by Shenoy (1978, Table 1) from Caplow's texts:

| type | distribution | representative weights | Caplow's predicted coalition(s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A = B = C | (1, 1, 1) | AB, AC, or BC |
| 2 | A > B, B = C, A < B + C | (3, 2, 2) | BC |
| 3 | A < B, B = C | (1, 2, 2) | AB or AC |
| 4 | A > B + C, B = C | (3, 1, 1) | none |
| 5 | A > B > C, A < B + C | (4, 3, 2) | AC or BC |
| 6 | A > B > C, A > B + C | (4, 2, 1) | none |
| 7 | A > B > C, A = B + C | (3, 2, 1) | AB or AC |
| 8 | A = B + C, B = C | (2, 1, 1) | AB or AC |

On Type 1: "each member strives to enter a coalition within which he is equal to his ally and stronger (by
virtue of the coalition) than the isolate" (1956, in Shenoy 1978: 185).

Structural restatement. Which pair forms is a function of the weights alone. A member joins the coalition
that gives him control over the most others, where a member controls another when he is stronger than that
other inside a winning coalition or when he is in a winning coalition and the other is not.

## C2 — Strength is weakness

In Type 2 the two weaker members combine against the strongest, and in Type 5 the predicted coalitions both
include the weakest member. The experimental literature named the pattern "strength is weakness": in Vinacke
and Arkoff's (1957) Type 2 games, A was included in 28.9% of coalitions against 86.5% and 85.4% for B and C
(Wissink et al. 2022). Caplow's statement of the mechanism is A.4: a stronger member's attempt to coerce
"will provoke the formation of an advantageous coalition to oppose the coercion."

Structural restatement. Among the coalitions that form, the strongest member is the one most often excluded
and the weakest the one most often included, whenever the strongest cannot dominate the other two combined.

## C3 — When the strongest exceeds the other two, no coalition

On Type 6: "A is stronger than B and C combined and has no motive to form a coalition. As in Type 4, true
coalition is impossible. However, while in Type 4 both of the weaker members seek to join the stronger
member, only C can improve his position by finding some extraneous means of inducing A to join him" (1956,
in Shenoy 1978: 188).

On Types 7 and 8: "the combined strength of B and C is exactly equal to A, so that no effective coalition of
B and C is strategically possible. In other words, although a coalition of B and C can block the dominance of
A, it is not sufficient to control the situation, and, therefore, the probable coalitions under the standard
assumptions are AB or AC" (1959, in Shenoy 1978: 189).

Structural restatement. When A ≥ B + C, the weaker pair cannot control the outcome — at equality it can
only block — and no coalition against A is effective; what remains are coalitions of A with one weaker
member, in which A controls the partner.

## C4 — Conservative, revolutionary, and improper coalitions (1968)

In *Two Against One* Caplow sorts coalitions by their effect on the existing order. A conservative coalition
does not alter the power structure; a revolutionary coalition dominates the strongest member; an improper
coalition is neither (Caplow 1968, as summarized in the coalition literature, e.g. *Current Research in
Social Psychology* 1(3), 1995). In Type 5, AB is conservative, BC is revolutionary, and AC is improper. On
Type 5 in 1956, Caplow adds the reservation that "whether the differential strength of A and B will make
them differentially attractive to C lies outside the scope of our present assumptions" (in Shenoy 1978: 190).

Structural restatement. In Type 5 every pair wins, and the three coalitions differ in kind: BC removes A
from control, AB keeps A in control and gives B control over C, AC keeps A in control and gives C control
over B. In each, both partners gain control over the isolate (A.2).

## C5 — Strength is additive

A.3: "Strength is additive. The strength of a coalition is equal to the sum of the strengths of its two
members." The coalition's effectiveness against the isolate is a function of the sum, and the prediction of
which coalition wins is made from sums alone.

Structural restatement. Two winning coalitions of the same summed strength stand in the same relation to the
isolate; how the strength is divided between the partners does not enter into whether the coalition
controls the situation.

## Definition of the triad (1968)

"A social system containing three related members in a persistent situation" (Caplow 1968: 1). The claim
that the triad's "most significant property" is the tendency to divide into two against one is the book's
title and organizing thesis.

## Claims not carried to hypotheses

Caplow's continuous / episodic / terminal situations (1959), which vary how often the coalition question
recurs, and his linked-triad rule for larger organizations (1968) — "a coalition partner in one triad may
not be an opponent in another" — concern the sequence and nesting of coalition episodes, which a single
Boolean form does not carry.
