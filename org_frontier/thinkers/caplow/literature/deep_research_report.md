# Caplow — Stage 2 literature

## Primary texts

Caplow's theory is stated in five pages of the *American Sociological Review* (1956): four assumptions, six
"types" of power distribution among three members, and a predicted coalition for each. The 1959 *American
Journal of Sociology* note adds two types in which the strongest member exactly equals the other two
combined, and three "situations" — continuous, episodic, terminal — in which the coalition question recurs
differently. *Two Against One* (1968) is the book-length statement: it defines the triad as "a social
system containing three related members in a persistent situation," extends the eight types to families,
organizations, and states, and sorts coalitions into conservative, revolutionary, and improper by their
effect on the standing order. The 1956 and 1959 texts are behind JSTOR; Shenoy's 1978 reformulation quotes
them at length, and `exegesis.md` quotes Caplow through Shenoy where it cannot quote him directly.

## What the literature did with the theory

**The experimental line.** Vinacke and Arkoff (1957) built the pachisi game that became the paradigm —
weights 3-2-2 for Type 2, 4-3-2 for Type 5 — and found the pattern the field named "strength is weakness":
in Type 2 the strongest player was in the coalition 28.9% of the time against 86.5% and 85.4% for the two
weaker ones, and in Type 5 the two weaker players over-chose each other. Kelley and Arrowood (1960)
argued the effect was an artifact of players misperceiving the game, since in Type 5 every pair wins and
strength is irrelevant; the effect survived their correction, weakened. Wissink et al. (2022) replicated it
sixty years on. The line tests Caplow's *predictions* and treats his assumptions as given.

**The formal line.** Gamson (1961) replaced Caplow's control motive with a payoff rule — the cheapest
winning coalition forms, and partners split in proportion to resources — and derived the same predictions
with a different mechanism. Shenoy (1978) restated both theories as solutions of a weighted majority game,
defined a "Caplow power index" that counts how many others a player controls in a coalition structure, and
showed that Caplow's predictions fall out of the index in six of eight types, with disagreements in Types 4,
6, 7, 8 where Caplow predicts fewer structures than the index admits. Shenoy also noticed two places where
Caplow's argument assumes a control relation that his own definition does not license — that B controls C
before any coalition forms in Type 6, and that B is "superior to A" in Type 3 — and called them errors.

**Organization studies.** Caplow's triad is the coalition triad in the small-groups and family-therapy
literatures, and *Two Against One*'s linked-triad rule for organizations was picked up by writers on
committees and boards. None of this asks what a coalition *is* structurally — whether the pair that forms
is a whole of two or a controller with a follower.

## The open gap

Every treatment of Caplow, his own included, evaluates a coalition by what it lets its members *control*:
who beats whom, who splits what. The lab's criterion evaluates a set of parties by whether they are *needed*
— whether the whole's determination depends on each of them. Caplow's first assumption, that "a stronger
member can control a weaker member and will seek to do so," makes the weaker partner's stance irrelevant
inside the coalition, and the lab's standing result on read-only parties (probe #5) says such a party is
not in the core. So the two criteria agree only where the coalition's partners are equal. Caplow's
predictions in Types 1 and 2 concern equal pairs; his predictions in Types 3, 5, 7, and 8 concern unequal
pairs, and there the theory says the coalition forms and the criterion is expected to say it is a
dominance dyad with a spectator. The gap is the computation: render each of the twenty-four coalitions by
Caplow's own assumptions and read which ones are wholes. Shenoy's two "errors" and Kelley and Arrowood's
critique both turn on the same question — whether control in a coalition has any structural reality — and
the computation bears on it.
