# Coleman — Stage 2 literature

## Primary text

Coleman (1988) introduces social capital as a resource defined by its function (S98), names three forms —
obligations and expectations, information channels, and norms with sanctions — and argues that the first and
third depend on one property of the surrounding structure: closure. The argument is made on a triangle.
In an open structure (Figure 1a) A has ties to B and C, who have none to each other; A can impose costs on
both, and "they cannot combine forces to sanction A ... A's actions can continue unabated" (S105). With
closure (Figure 1b) "B and C can combine to provide a collective sanction, or either can reward the other
for sanctioning A" (S106). The same property makes obligations — "credit slips" (S102) — trustworthy:
"Reputation cannot arise in an open structure, and collective sanctions that would ensure trustworthiness
cannot be applied" (S107–S108). The rotating-credit association (S102–S103, after Geertz 1962) is the case:
n members, n contributions each, one payout each, and one early absconder destroys it. Intergenerational
closure (S106–S107) is the four-node version: two parents whose children are friends, with or without a tie
between the parents. Coleman closes on the public-good property: "the actor or actors who generate social
capital ordinarily capture only a small part of its benefits, a fact that leads to underinvestment in
social capital" (S119).

## What the literature did with it

**The closure–brokerage debate.** Burt (2001) framed Coleman's closure and his own structural holes as rival
accounts of social capital and proposed a synthesis — closure within a group, brokerage beyond it — which
Burt (2005) develops at length. Portes (1998) reviews the concept's spread and its conflation of source,
mechanism, and outcome. Granovetter (1985) is the embeddedness argument that Coleman's trust rests on.
None of this literature has a criterion for whether the closed structure is one thing; "closure" is a
density, and its benefit is argued through mechanisms — sanction, reputation, trust — rather than through
what the structure is.

**The lab.** The Burt paper in this series found that closure removes the broker's value added: an
information broker is evicted from the core by one tie between its contacts; a control broker under closure
becomes a peer in an OR-clique at Φ = 6 with no positional advantage over anyone. Those numbers were read
for the broker. Read for the collective they are Coleman's claim: the whole gains and the broker's edge
goes.

## The open gap

Coleman's claims are about what closure does to a structure as a structure: it lets two parties combine, it
makes a norm hold, it makes an association a unit, it benefits the whole more than the one who closes it.
Each has a rendering. Whether A yields to a joint sanction only when B and C read each other; whether
compliance becomes a fixed point; whether the sanctioners are one complex and whether A is in it; whether
the parents' tie makes the four one; whether the credit ring is one complex of all its members; and whether
the party who closes captures the whole's gain in Φ. The Burt forms are already computed and can be re-read
against Coleman's prediction without change. What is not known is whether an effective norm — a structure
that rests — is a more or a less irreducible whole than the open structure that cannot rest. The Heider paper
found that tension binds and balance dissolves; if that holds here, Coleman's closure will make the norm
hold and the whole's Φ fall, and his public good will not be Φ.
