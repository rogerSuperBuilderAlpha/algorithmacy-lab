# Emerson — Stage 2 literature

## Primary text

Emerson (1962) defines power as the other's dependence — "power resides implicitly in the other's
dependency" (p. 32) — and dependence as investment over alternatives: A's dependence on B is "directly
proportional to A's motivational investment in goals mediated by B, and inversely proportional to the
availability of those goals to A outside of the A-B relation" (p. 32). Pab = Dba. A power advantage is
Pab − Pba (p. 33), and the paper's contribution is that "the formal notation adopted here suggests exactly
four generic types of balancing operation" (p. 35): B withdraws investment; B cultivates alternatives; A
raises investment in B ("giving status"); A is denied alternatives (coalition). On the C–A–B network — A
served by B or C, each of whom depends on A alone — Emerson distinguishes operation 2, closing the network
with a B–C tie, which "reduces the power of the stronger actor" but leaves "still just a power network,"
from operation 4, which "increases the power of weaker actors through collectivization" by collapsing B and
C into a "collective actor" in a "(BC)-A" relation (p. 37). Legitimation is a special case of operation 4
(p. 39).

## What the literature did with it

Blau (1964, p. 118 n. 7) took the definition and faulted the emphasis: attention to balancing diverts from
the imbalance that produces power. Cook and Emerson (1978) brought power-dependence into the laboratory,
and Cook, Emerson, Gillmore, and Yamagishi (1983) showed that in larger exchange networks power does not
follow centrality but the availability of alternatives — Emerson's second variable made structural.
Markovsky, Willer, and Patton (1988) built the graph-theoretic power index on the same foundation. In all of
it the network is a set of exchange opportunities and power is measured in resource splits; whether a
balanced relation is one thing, or whether balancing by extension and by coalition produce different
wholes, is not asked.

**The lab.** The Blau paper found a gradient in the superior's position — in the core and indispensable,
outside and indispensable, outside and dispensable — as Blau's alternatives opened. Emerson's four
operations are a finer partition of the same moves, each a single change of reading, and his
extension–coalition distinction is a claim that two operations which both balance leave different
structures. The Burt paper's closed control broker (Φ = 6, every V 4) is Emerson's operation 2 already
computed; the Ostrom paper's self-governance clique (Φ = 6, every V 6) is his operation 4.

## The open gap

Whether the C–A–B network reads as Emerson's imbalance in the instrument's terms (A indispensable, dependents
adding nothing); whether each of the four operations zeroes A's advantage; where each leaves the parties —
withdrawal should drop B from the complex, status-giving should balance A–B and leave C dependent — and
whether extension and coalition, both balancing, differ as Emerson says: one reducing the strong, the other
raising the weak, one a network and the other a group.
