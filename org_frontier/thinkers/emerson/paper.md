## Abstract

Emerson (1962) defined power as the other's dependence and derived from the definition "exactly four generic
types of balancing operation": the weaker party withdraws, cultivates alternatives, gives status to the
stronger, or forms a coalition. On the C–A–B network he separated the second from the fourth — closing the
network with a B–C tie "reduces the power of the stronger actor" but leaves "still just a power network,"
while coalition "increases the power of weaker actors" by collapsing them into a "collective actor." This
paper renders the network and the four operations as Boolean forms, one change of reading each, and asks
exact IIT-4.0 Φ what each does. The network is a whole of three in which A adds everything and the dependents
nothing (H1 confirmed). Withdrawal drops B from the complex and halves A's value added (H2 confirmed);
extension makes a clique in which everyone adds 4.000 (H3 confirmed); status-giving makes A and B a pair and
leaves C outside (H4 confirmed). Both extension and coalition zero A's advantage, and coalition raises the
weak from 0 to 6.000 — but extension raises A too, from 2.000 to 4.000, rather than reducing him (H5 partial).
Emerson's network–group distinction survives in a form he did not name: under extension a member adds 4.000
to a whole of 6.000, so a pair outlives any deletion; under coalition a member adds the whole, so nothing
does. Results are about Boolean models.

## Introduction

The Blau paper in this series found a gradient. As Blau's alternatives to compliance opened, the superior
moved from inside the core and indispensable, to outside it and indispensable, to outside and dispensable.
Blau took the alternatives from Emerson and faulted him for what he did with them: Emerson's "focus on
balancing operations is unfortunate and somewhat confusing inasmuch as it diverts attention from the
analysis of power imbalance" (Blau, 1964, p. 118 n. 7). Emerson's balancing operations are the moves that
produce the gradient, and they are more finely cut than Blau's — four, each a single change in who reads
whom, each "dictated by the logic of the scheme" (Emerson, 1962, p. 35).

The scheme is two propositions. "Power resides implicitly in the other's dependency" (p. 32); and
dependence is "directly proportional to A's motivational investment in goals mediated by B, and inversely
proportional to the availability of those goals to A outside of the A-B relation" (p. 32). From Pab = Dba
and dependence as investment over alternatives, an unbalanced relation can be balanced in four ways: B
lowers investment, B finds alternatives, A raises investment, A loses alternatives (p. 35). Emerson then
draws a line through the four that Blau does not. On a C–A–B network — A with two dependents who have only
him — a B–C tie balances by "operation number two" and "reduces the power of the stronger actor," but "it
is still just a power network"; coalition, "operation number 4," balances by "collapsing the two-relational
network into one group-person relation with the emergence of a 'collective actor'" and "increases the power
of weaker actors" (p. 37). Two operations that both balance, and Emerson says they leave different things.

Five hypotheses were fixed before computing: whether the C–A–B network reads as imbalance (H1); whether
withdrawal (H2), extension (H3), and status-giving (H4) each balance and leave the parties where Emerson
says; and whether extension and coalition differ as he says — one reducing the strong, the other raising
the weak (H5). Four confirmed, one partial, and the partial is where the instrument says something Emerson
did not.

## Emerson's account

Power is potential: "the amount of resistance on the part of B which can be potentially overcome by A"
(p. 32). It is located in the other: A holds power over B to the degree that B depends on A, and B depends
on A to the degree that B wants what A mediates and cannot get it elsewhere. A relation is balanced when
Pab = Pba; a power advantage is Pab − Pba (p. 33); and "balancing operations" are "structural changes in
power-dependence relations which tend to reduce power advantage" (p. 33).

The four follow from the two variables of dependence. "In the unbalanced relation ... balance can be
restored either by an increase in Dab or by a decrease in Dba": "1. If B reduces motivational investment in
goals mediated by A; 2. If B cultivates alternative sources for gratification of those goals; 3. If A
increases motivational investment in goals mediated by B; 4. If A is denied alternative sources for
achieving those goals" (p. 35). Emerson names them: "motivational withdrawal by B," "the cultivation of
alternative social relations by B," "'giving status' to A," and "coalition and group formation" (p. 35).

The network–group distinction is his own. When B and C, both dependent on A, form a tie, "we have what
appears to be a coalition of the two weaker against the one stronger. This, however, is not technically
the case, for A is not involved in the B-C interactions; he simply exists as an alternative playmate for
both B and C. ... a triadic network reduces to a coalition only if two members unite as a single actor in
the process of dealing directly with the third" (p. 37). Legitimation is "one special case of balancing
operation number four" (p. 39).

Cook and Emerson (1978) and Cook, Emerson, Gillmore, and Yamagishi (1983) took the scheme into experimental
exchange networks, where power followed alternatives rather than centrality; Markovsky, Willer, and Patton
(1988) built a graph-theoretic index on it.

## From claims to forms

An **actor** is a Boolean node and **dependence** is reading. **Alternatives** are a disjunction of sources.
The **C–A–B network**: A' = B ∨ C; B' = A; C' = A — A served by either dependent, each dependent served by A
alone. The four operations are one change of reading each. **Withdrawal**: B stops reading A and holds its
own state, B' = B. **Extension**: B and C read each other, B' = A ∨ C and C' = A ∨ B. **Status-giving**: A
comes to read B alone, A' = B. **Coalition**: B and C act only together and A is denied alternatives,
A' = B ∧ C, B' = A ∧ C, C' = A ∧ B. **Value added** V and **positional advantage** are the Coleman paper's
quantities; a deleted variable reads 0. A relation is **balanced** when A's advantage is 0.

The rendering's risk is in withdrawal and coalition. B holding its own state is one reading of "reduces
motivational investment"; a B who reads nothing and is constant would be another. Coalition as B and C
acting only together *and* A denied alternatives is Emerson's operation 4 as written; a coalition that does
not deny A alternatives is the Blau paper's organized form, and the two are compared in the discussion.

## Hypotheses

Fixed in `hypotheses.md` before any form was run.

- **H1 (Emerson) — the unbalanced network.** A's advantage > 0; V(A) = core Φ; V(B) = V(C) = 0. *Prior:* with.
- **H2 (Emerson) — withdrawal balances.** B leaves the complex; A's advantage is 0. *Prior:* open.
- **H3 (Emerson) — extension balances, three actors remain.** A's advantage 0; core all three; equal V.
  *Prior:* with.
- **H4 (Emerson) — status-giving balances A–B only.** V(A) = V(B), both in the core; V(C) = 0. *Prior:* with.
- **H5 (Emerson) — extension reduces the strong, coalition raises the weak.** Both zero A's advantage;
  V(A) falls under extension; V(B), V(C) rise under coalition. *Prior:* open on the directions.

## Methods

**Instrument.** Exact IIT-4.0 Φ (Albantakis et al., 2023) via PyPhi (Mayner et al., 2018); expression
machinery from the Coleman paper's `forms.py`.

**Control.** Every probe first runs the conjunctive triad (Φ = 2.000000, core {A, M, B}). All passed.

**Procedure.** Five probes, one per hypothesis; each prints the control, one line per form with value added
by party, A's advantage, and fixed points, and a verdict. Results in `results/`, registered in
`ci/reproduce.json` (#444–#448); each runs in seconds.

**Scope.** Results are about Boolean models. No exchange is measured.

## Results

*Table 1. The network and the four operations. All five have fixed points 000 and 111; withdrawal also 101.*

### H1 — the unbalanced network: CONFIRMED

The C–A–B network is a whole of three at Φ = 2.000. A's value added is 2.000 — the whole — and B's and C's
are 0: delete either dependent and A with the other is still a pair at 2.000. A's advantage is 2.000.
Emerson's imbalance is, in the instrument's terms, a whole whose strong member is indispensable and whose
weak members are interchangeable.

### H2 — withdrawal balances: CONFIRMED

When B stops reading A, B leaves the complex: the core is {A, C} at 2.000, and the whole of three is
reducible. A's value added falls from 2.000 to 1.000, C's rises from 0 to 1.000, and A's advantage is 0.
Withdrawal is the only operation of the four that takes from A. It balances A–B by dissolving it and
balances A–C by the same act, because A's alternative to C has gone.

### H3 — extension balances and keeps three actors: CONFIRMED

The B–C tie makes an OR-clique — three identical rules — at Φ = 6.000, all three in the core, every value
added 4.000, A's advantage 0. This is the Burt paper's closed control broker, and it is Emerson's "still
just a power network": three actors, each with two sources, none with an edge.

### H4 — status-giving balances A–B only: CONFIRMED

When A comes to read B alone, A and B are a pair at 2.000 with V 2.000 each, and C — still reading A, unread
by anyone — is outside with V 0. The A–B relation is balanced; the A–C relation is what it was, and C is now
what B was to the network: a dependent who adds nothing.

### H5 — extension reduces the strong, coalition raises the weak: PARTIAL

Both operations zero A's advantage. Coalition raises B's and C's value added from 0 to 6.000 — the weak
become, each of them, the whole. Extension does not lower A's value added; it raises it, from 2.000 to
4.000, along with everyone else's. The first direction Emerson named is wrong on this form and the second
is right. Two of three clauses held.

## Discussion

Emerson's four operations all balance, and the instrument adds where each leaves the parties. Withdrawal
balances by subtraction: B is gone, A and C are a pair, and A's value added is halved. Status-giving
balances by pairing: A and B are a dyad, C is outside. Extension and coalition balance by addition: the
whole's Φ triples and every member's value added rises. Only the first takes anything from A. Emerson wrote
that balancing operations "tend to reduce power advantage" (p. 33), and they do — but in three of four cases
they do it by raising the weak to the strong, not by lowering the strong to the weak. On these forms
balance is nearly always a larger whole, not a redistributed one.

That is why the first clause of H5 failed. Emerson's "operation number two reduces the power of the
stronger actor" reads power as Pab − Pba, a difference, and the difference does go to zero. Read as value
added, A's contribution doubles under extension. Both readings are correct; the second is the one the
instrument has, and it reports that closing the network makes A more necessary to a whole that is now three
times as integrated, not less necessary to the same one. Blau's objection — that balancing diverts
attention from imbalance — misses this the other way: the balanced clique is not the imbalanced network with
the advantage removed; it is a different and tighter thing.

The network–group distinction survives, and in a form Emerson did not name. Extension and coalition both
give Φ = 6.000 with three members and no advantage. They differ in value added: 4.000 each under extension,
6.000 each under coalition. Under extension, delete any member and the remaining two are a pair at 2.000 —
the network loses a node and stays a network. Under coalition, delete any member and nothing remains — the
collective actor is dissolved by the loss of any part. Emerson's "still just a power network" against "a
collective actor" is, in the instrument's terms, a whole that survives deletion against one that does not,
and the difference is the 2.000 that a surviving pair is worth.

His coalition is also the rendering that keeps A inside. The Blau paper's organized subordinates — B and C
act only together but A keeps his alternatives, A' = B ∨ C — gave a core of {B, C} with A outside and
indispensable. Emerson's operation 4 denies A alternatives too, A' = B ∧ C, and the result is the
AND-clique with all three in. The difference between a coalition that excludes the strong actor and one that
absorbs him is whether the strong actor is made to need both of them. Emerson's "(BC)–A" is a group-person
relation; the instrument finds that when A must deal with the group as a group, A is in it.

## Limitations

One rendering per operation. Withdrawal as B holding its own state is a choice; a constant B would remove B
from the network altogether rather than leaving it as a background that switches A on. Coalition as
operation 4 written in full — B and C together and A denied alternatives — is one of two coalition forms in
this series, and the other (the Blau paper's organized form) gives a different core. Value added reads a
deleted variable as 0. Forms are three nodes; Emerson's larger networks and Cook et al.'s (1983) result that
power follows alternatives rather than centrality are not reached, though H1's interchangeable dependents
are consistent with it.

Results are about Boolean models. No exchange is measured.

## Conclusion

Emerson's four balancing operations each zero the strong actor's advantage, and the instrument says how:
withdrawal by subtraction, status-giving by pairing, extension and coalition by making a larger whole in
which the weak rise to the strong. Only withdrawal takes from A. Extension does not reduce the stronger
actor's power, as Emerson said; it doubles his value added while erasing his edge. The distinction he drew
between the balanced network and the collective actor holds as a difference in what survives a deletion: a
pair, or nothing.
