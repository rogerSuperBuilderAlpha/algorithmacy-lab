# Heider — Stage 1 exegesis: the claims in his own words

Source: Fritz Heider, "Attitudes and Cognitive Organization," *The Journal of Psychology* 21 (1946), 107–112.
Page numbers follow the original; the text is public at York University's Classics in the History of
Psychology. Cartwright and Harary's generalization (*Psychological Review* 63, 1956, 277–293) is quoted where
it supplies the formal criterion Heider's paper implies.

## The hypothesis

> "(b) A balanced state exists if all parts of a unit have the same dynamic character (i.e., if all are
> positive, or all are negative), and if entities with different dynamic character are segregated from each
> other. If no balanced state exists, then forces towards this state will arise. Either the dynamic
> characters will change, or the unit relations will be changed through action or through cognitive
> reorganization. If a change is not possible, the state of imbalance will produce tension." (pp. 107–108)

Two relations: L, the attitude ("to like, to love, to esteem, to value, and their opposites"), written pLo or
p~Lo; and U, the unit relation ("similarity, proximity, causality, membership, possession, or belonging"). "All
examples refer to p's life space" (p. 108).

## C1 — The balanced triad: all positive, or one positive and two negative

> "In the case of three entities, a balanced state exists if all three possible relations are positive in all
> respects." (p. 110)
>
> "For instance, (pLo) + (o~Ux) + (p~Lx) is balanced. Examples: p likes o because o got rid of something p
> dislikes. In this case two entities, p and o are related positively to each other, while both are related
> negatively to the third entity x. This holds generally: the triad of relations is in balance if two
> relations are negative and one positive." (p. 110)
>
> "Therefore, the second part of the hypothesis must be stated as follows: (b) In the case of three entities,
> a balanced state exists if all three relations are positive in all respects, or if two are negative and one
> positive." (p. 110)

Cartwright and Harary make the criterion a sign product: "Heider's balanced state is depicted as an s-graph
of three points whose cycle is positive" (1956: 284), and a signed graph is balanced "if and only if all of
its cycles are positive."

Structural restatement. Of the four sign patterns on three mutual relations — +++, ++−, +−−, −−− — two are
balanced (+++ and +−−) and two are not (++− and −−−). Balance is a property of the pattern, not of any one
relation.

## C2 — Forces toward balance; imbalance is tension

> "If no balanced state exists, then forces towards this state will arise. Either the dynamic characters will
> change, or the unit relations will be changed through action or through cognitive reorganization. If a
> change is not possible, the state of imbalance will produce tension." (pp. 107–108)
>
> "A tendency exists to make the different dynamic relations agree with each other by means of cognitive
> restructuring (excuses or rationalizations)." (p. 108)

Structural restatement. A balanced configuration is at rest: there is an assignment of attitudes that
satisfies every relation. An unbalanced configuration has no such assignment; something must change, and if
nothing can, the configuration is under continuing strain.

## C3 — Attitudes and relations influence each other; L is psychologically symmetric and transitive

Heider's first sentence: "Attitudes towards persons and causal unit formations influence each other"
(p. 107).

> "pLo is a non-symmetrical relation logically, but psychologically it tends to become symmetrical." (pp.
> 108–109)
>
> "Logically, L is not transitive but there exists a psychological tendency to make it transitive when
> implications between U relations do not interfere with transitivity." (pp. 109–110)
>
> "This statement can be derived from the assumption that L and U are, in a balanced configuration,
> exchangeable, symmetrical, and transitive. L and U can then be treated as formally analogous to an identity
> relation." (p. 110)

Structural restatement. Attitudes toward an object move toward the attitudes of liked others and away from
those of disliked others, and relations move toward agreement of attitude: like those who agree with you.
The two processes act on each other and, in balance, settle together.

## C4 — The all-negative triad is "too indetermined"

> "By substituting L or U for the identity sign one obtains the balanced cases for these relations, though the
> case with three negative relations does not seem to constitute a good psychological balance, since it is too
> indetermined." (p. 110)

Cartwright and Harary count −−− unbalanced (its cycle is negative). Davis (1967) later admitted it as
"clusterable" — balance in a weak sense, with three factions instead of two.

Structural restatement. Heider treats −−− as a third kind: not balanced, but not the tense imbalance of ++−
either — under-determined rather than over-constrained. The formal line says two kinds; the clustering line
says −−− is a weak balance.

## C5 — Segregation: balance partitions the unit into two camps

Heider's clause "entities with different dynamic character are segregated from each other" (p. 107) and his
reading of +−−: "two entities, p and o are related positively to each other, while both are related
negatively to the third entity x" (p. 110). Cartwright and Harary's structure theorem states it for any
size: "An s-graph is balanced if and only if its points can be separated into two mutually exclusive subsets
such that each positive line joins two points of the same subset and each negative line joins points from
different subsets" (1956: 286). They also grade imbalance: "we define the degree of balance of an s-graph as
the ratio of the number of positive cycles to the total number of cycles" (1956: 288).

Structural restatement. In a balanced structure the members split into at most two camps, alike within and
opposed between, and the degree of balance of a larger structure is the share of its cycles that are
positive. More balance, more unit.

## Claims not carried to hypotheses

The L/U distinction and its asymmetries ("often the U relation is weaker than the L relation," p. 111); the
one-many implications behind envy, jealousy, and competition (pp. 109, 111); self-regard pLp (p. 111); and
the means-end use of configurations (p. 111). These concern the content of relations, which a sign does not
carry.
