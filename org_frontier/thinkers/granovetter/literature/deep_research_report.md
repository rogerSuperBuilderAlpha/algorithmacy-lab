# Granovetter — Stage 2 literature

## Primary text

"The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) is a micro-macro argument in five moves. Tie strength is defined as a
graded, symmetric combination of time, intensity, intimacy, and reciprocity (p. 1361). From the overlap
hypothesis — the stronger A–B, the more of their friends they share — follows the forbidden triad: two
strong ties from A with nothing between their far ends is "the triad which is most unlikely to occur," and
Granovetter "exaggerate[s]" this to a rule that the third tie is always present (p. 1363). From the rule
follows the theorem that "no strong tie is a bridge" and hence "all bridges are weak ties" (p. 1364), where
a bridge is Harary, Norman, and Cartwright's line providing the only path between two points. From that
follows the diffusion claim: removing a weak tie does more damage to transmission than removing a strong
one, and what diffuses travels farther through weak ties (p. 1366). And from that the community claim:
cliques without weak ties between them are "extremely fragmented" though "the local phenomenon is
cohesion," and weak ties are "indispensable ... to their integration into communities" (pp. 1373–1378). The
1983 sequel restates the individual-level claim — those with few weak ties are "deprived of information from
distant parts of the social system" — and concedes that weak ties which are not bridges do little.

## What the literature did with it

**The graph-theoretic line.** Granovetter builds on Harary et al. (1965) for bridges and paths and on Davis
(1967) for clusterability; the forbidden triad is a transitivity condition on strong ties, and the whole
argument is that transitivity among strong ties confines them to cliques. Burt (1992) later read the weak-tie
argument as a special case of structural holes: the hole is the cause, tie strength a correlate.

**Complex contagion.** Centola and Macy (2007) showed that the weak-tie advantage holds for simple contagions
— one exposure suffices — and reverses for complex ones, which need reinforcement from multiple contacts;
long ties spread information but not behavior that requires social confirmation. This is the one line of
work that separates transmission from something else a tie does, and it is the closest neighbor of the
present question.

**Organization studies.** The weak-tie argument entered as a theory of information access — job search,
knowledge transfer across units, innovation — and its structural core was seldom reexamined. "Bridge" is used
as a synonym for "weak tie between groups," and the question of what the bridge does beyond transmit is not
asked.

## The open gap

Granovetter's theory is about paths. Every one of his structural claims — bridge, damage, fragmentation,
integration — is stated in terms of whether information or influence can get from one point to another, and
his word for what weak ties do at the community level is *integration*. The lab's criterion is a different
thing called integration: whether a set of parties forms an irreducible whole, which requires not that
influence pass through but that it return. Bridges, by Granovetter's own definition, are lines with no
alternative path, and so no return except through themselves. The gap is to render tie strength as
Granovetter defines it — symmetric, graded, its first dimension time — and ask, form by form, where
transmission and irreducibility agree and where they part: at the dyad, the forbidden triad, the bridge, the
cut, and the community of cliques.
