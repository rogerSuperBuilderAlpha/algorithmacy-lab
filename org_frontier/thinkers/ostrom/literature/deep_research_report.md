# Ostrom — Stage 2 literature

## Primary text

*Governing the Commons* (1990) answers Hardin's (1968) tragedy and the two remedies attached to it — a
central authority that monitors and sanctions ("Leviathan," pp. 8–12) and private division (pp. 12–13) —
with field cases of appropriators who devise, monitor, and enforce their own rules: the Alanya fishers'
site rotation (pp. 18–21), Swiss and Japanese commons, Spanish *huertas*, Philippine *zanjeras*. Chapter 3
distills the long-enduring cases into eight design principles (Table 3.1, p. 90). The ones with structural
content are boundaries (1: who may withdraw "must be clearly defined"), monitoring (4: monitors "are
accountable to the appropriators or are the appropriators"), graduated sanctions (5), and nested enterprises
(8: governance "organized in multiple layers of nested enterprises"). The mechanism that ties them is the
contingent self-commitment: "I commit myself to follow the set of rules we have devised in all instances
except dire emergencies if the rest of those affected make a similar commitment and act accordingly"
(p. 100). Monitoring is "a by-product" of appropriators' own use (p. 95), and the observed sanctions are
"surprisingly low" (p. 94) — compliance is, in Levi's phrase, quasi-voluntary.

## What the literature did with it

Ostrom (2000) restates the argument in norm-evolution terms; Ostrom (2009) generalizes it into a framework
for social-ecological systems. Cox, Arnold, and Villamayor Tomás (2010) review 91 studies and find the
principles broadly supported, proposing to split boundaries (1) and monitoring (4) into resource-facing and
user-facing halves. The design principles are read throughout as institutional prescriptions; no one asks
what the structure they describe is as a structure — whether a group of mutual monitors is one thing, or
whether nesting preserves the parts.

**The lab.** The Coleman paper found that an effective norm — compliance as a fixed point — is reached by
closure among the sanctioners and drops the sanctioned party out of the complex: the collective it produces
is the two sanctioners, with the third party outside as a background condition. Ostrom's fourth principle
says there is no such third party. The Serres paper found that a one-way arrow binds nothing, which bears on
the outsider who watches the commons and is not read; and the Coleman parents form found that a tie in the
upper layer makes that layer the core and drops the lower, which is the pattern Ostrom's nesting would have
to avoid.

## The open gap

Whether a group in which every party both complies and sanctions is one complex with every party in it,
against Coleman's ejection of the sanctioned; whether self-governance is a tighter whole than the enforcer's
star, and whether the enforcer holds an edge; whether nested layers are one complex with the layers still
irreducible inside it; whether compliance can rest with the sanction retired rather than standing; and
whether a defined boundary leaves the enterprise unchanged by an outsider while a breach changes it. The
Coleman paper's forms are the baseline, and the contest runs on the same machinery.
