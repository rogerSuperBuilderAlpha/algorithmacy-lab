# Where the superior stands: Blau's exchange theory of power under exact Φ

## Abstract

Blau (1964) derived power from exchange: a person who supplies what others need and cannot repay "attains
power over others by making the satisfaction of their need contingent on their compliance," and holds it
unless one of four alternatives — repayment, another supplier, coercion, doing without — is open. A
collectivity of subordinates then either legitimates that power, so that their own norms enforce
compliance, or opposes it. The Serres paper in this series found that a one-way arrow from a constant
producer binds nothing. This paper asks whether Blau's power is that arrow. It is not: a supplier who serves
regardless binds nothing, and one whose service is contingent on compliance is a complex of two at
Φ = 2.000 (H1 confirmed). The superior's position then falls in steps as Blau's alternatives open. An
alternative supplier moves the value from the suppliers to the dependent (H2 confirmed). Two subordinates
who comply only together become the core, with the superior outside it and still indispensable (H3
confirmed). Legitimation — a subordinate complies because the peer does — leaves the subordinates complying
after the superior stops serving, makes them the core, and makes the superior dispensable (H4 confirmed).
Opposition is not legitimation's mirror: shared opposition rests with the superior yielding where isolated
opposition never rests, but disapproval binds the pair at 0.369 where approval binds it at 2.000 (H5
partial). Across the forms the superior moves from in the core and indispensable, to outside and
indispensable, to outside and dispensable. Results are about Boolean models.

## Introduction

Blau's theory of power is a theory of a relation with one side heavier than the other. A person who supplies
services others need, "and who is independent of any at their command, attains power over others by making
the satisfaction of their need contingent on their compliance" (1964, p. 22). The dependence runs one way;
the superior needs nothing the subordinate has. The Serres paper in this series computed a structure of that
description — a producer who gives regardless and a parasite who takes — and found that it binds nothing:
the producer is outside any complex and the receiver has none to be in. If Blau's superior is Serres's
producer, Blau's power is not a relation in the instrument's sense.

It is not the producer. Blau's superior is independent of what the subordinate has to offer, but his service
is contingent on the subordinate's compliance, and contingency is a return. That is the first question. The
rest follow Blau's own sequence. Power holds "unless one of four alternatives to submission exists" (p. 140):
the dependent may repay, obtain the service elsewhere, coerce the supplier, or do without (pp. 118–119).
Alternatives two and three have structural renderings — a second supplier, and two subordinates who withhold
together. Then the collective turn. "The weakness of the isolated subordinate limits the significance of
his approval or disapproval of the superior"; a collectivity that approves generates "group pressures that
promote compliance with the ruler's directives," legitimating his authority, and one that disapproves
generates opposition (p. 23). Legitimate authority is defined by the fact that the subordinates' own norms
"enforce compliance with the superior's commands" (p. 12).

Five hypotheses were fixed before computing. Whether contingency makes power a relation the arrow is not
(H1); whether an alternative supplier removes the superior's power (H2); whether organizing the dependent
shifts it (H3); whether legitimate authority is carried by the subordinates (H4); and whether opposition
mirrors legitimation (H5). Four confirmed, one partial, and the results line up as a gradient in where the
superior stands.

## Blau's account

Exchange obligates. "An individual who supplies rewarding services to another obligates him. To discharge
this obligation, the second must furnish benefits to the first in turn" (p. 89). When the second has nothing
the first wants, compliance is what he furnishes: "Willingness to comply with another's demands is a generic
social reward, since the power it gives him is a generalized means, parallel to money" (p. 22). Power is the
credit that unilateral supply establishes.

Chapter 5 gives the conditions. "Individuals who need a service another has to offer have the following
alternatives: First, they can supply him with a service that he wants badly enough to induce him to offer
his service in return ... Second, they may obtain the needed service elsewhere, assuming that there are
alternative suppliers ... Third, they can coerce him to furnish the service, provided they are capable of
doing so ... Fourth, they may learn to resign themselves to do without this service" (pp. 118–119). Blau
faults Emerson's (1962) power-dependence schema for attending to the balancing operations rather than to
the imbalance that gives rise to power (p. 118 n. 7), and closes the chapter: "The supply of needed benefits
creates an undeniable claim to power unless one of four alternatives to submission exists" (p. 140).

The collective turn is set out in chapter 1 and developed in chapters 8 and 9. "Collective approval of
power legitimates that power. People who consider that the advantages they gain from a superior's exercise
of power outweigh the hardships that compliance with his demands imposes on them tend to communicate to
each other their approval of the ruler and their feelings of obligation to him. The consensus that develops
as the result of these communications finds expression in group pressures that promote compliance with the
ruler's directives, thereby strengthening his power of control and legitimating his authority" (p. 23).
And its reverse: "Collective disapproval of power engenders opposition. People who share the experience of
being exploited by the unfair demands of those in positions of power ... are likely to communicate their
feelings of anger, frustration, and aggression to each other" (p. 23).

Homans (1958) opened the exchange line; Cook and Emerson (1978) made alternatives measurable in
experimental networks; Emerson (1976) reviews the field.

## From claims to forms

A **superior** S has a service bit (1 = serves) and a **subordinate** B a compliance bit (1 = complies).
**Contingent service**: S' = B. **Dependence**: B' = S. **Independence** is a constant supplier, S' = 1 — the
Serres producer. An **alternative supplier**: B' = S1 ∨ S2 with each S_i' = B. **Organization** of the
dependent: B_i' = S ∧ B_j, comply only if the peer does. **Legitimation**: B_i' = S ∨ B_j, comply when served
or when the peer complies. **Opposition**: B_i now means opposes, B_i' = ¬S ∨ B_j, and S yields to joint
opposition, S' = B1 ∧ B2. The **structure** is the major complex; **value added** V and **positional
advantage** are the Coleman paper's quantities, and a deleted variable reads 0.

Ten forms in five pairs: **independent** / **contingent**; **single** / **alternatives**; **unorganized**
(S' = B1 ∨ B2, B_i' = S) / **organized**; **power** (S' = B1 ∧ B2, B_i' = S) / **authority**;
**opposition_isolated** (B_i' = ¬S) / **opposition_shared**. The two opposition forms are the Coleman paper's
open and closed norm relabeled; their numbers are re-derived here.

## Hypotheses

Fixed in `hypotheses.md` before any form was run.

- **H1 (Blau) — contingency makes power a relation.** contingent is a complex of {S, B}; independent leaves B
  unbound. *Prior:* with.
- **H2 (Blau) — an alternative supplier removes power.** S1's advantage and value added in alternatives are
  below S's in single. *Prior:* with.
- **H3 (Blau) — organizing the dependent shifts power.** S's advantage falls and each B's value added rises
  from unorganized to organized. *Prior:* open.
- **H4 (Blau) — authority is carried by the subordinates.** From (S = 0, B1 = B2 = 1), authority keeps both
  complying and power does not; B1 and B2 are co-members under authority. *Prior:* with on the dynamics.
- **H5 (Blau) — opposition mirrors legitimation.** opposition_shared and authority have the same core size
  and Φ; shared opposition rests with S yielding and isolated opposition never rests. *Prior:* with on rest;
  open on the mirror.

## Methods

**Instrument.** Exact IIT-4.0 Φ (Albantakis et al., 2023) via PyPhi (Mayner et al., 2018); expression
machinery from the Coleman paper's `forms.py`.

**Control.** Every probe first runs the conjunctive triad (Φ = 2.000000, core {A, M, B}). All passed.

**Procedure.** Five probes, one per hypothesis; each prints the control, one line per form with value added
by party and fixed points, and a verdict. Results in `results/`, registered in `ci/reproduce.json`
(#439–#443); each runs in seconds.

**Scope.** Results are about Boolean models. No superior or subordinate is measured.

### H1 — contingency makes power a relation: CONFIRMED

A supplier who serves regardless and a subordinate who complies when served have no complex at all: S is
constant and B copies a constant. Make the service contingent on compliance and the two are a complex at
Φ = 2.000, each adding 2.000. Blau's superior is not Serres's producer. What the arrow lacked was not a
second direction of dependence but a second direction of reading, and "contingent on their compliance" is
exactly that.

### H2 — an alternative supplier removes power: CONFIRMED

Alone, supplier and dependent are a pair at 2.000 with no advantage to either. Add a second supplier and
the three are a whole at 2.000 — but the value has moved. The dependent adds 2.000 and each supplier adds 0;
the first supplier's advantage falls from 0 to −2.000. The form is the Burt paper's control broker seen from
the other side: B, with two sources, is the broker, and the suppliers are the contacts who add nothing
because either will do. Blau's second alternative and Burt's structural hole are the same figure with the
roles named from opposite ends.

### H3 — organizing the dependent shifts power: CONFIRMED

Two subordinates who each comply when served, under a superior who serves if either complies, are a whole
of three in which the superior adds 2.000 and the subordinates add nothing; his advantage is 2.000. Let each
comply only if the other does and the core becomes the two subordinates at 2.000, each adding 2.000, and the
superior's advantage falls to 0. He is outside the core — but his value added is still 2.000: delete him and
the pair collapses. Organization moves the superior out of the whole without making him dispensable to it.

*Cross-reference.* The Emerson paper in this series (`../emerson/paper.md`, probe #448) runs the coalition
as Emerson's operation 4 in full — the subordinates act only together *and* the superior is denied
alternatives (A' = B ∧ C). The superior is then inside an AND-clique at 6.000 with every member adding 6.000.
Whether a coalition excludes the strong actor or absorbs him turns on whether he is made to need both of
them.

### H4 — authority is carried by the subordinates: CONFIRMED

Under power, if the superior stops serving while both subordinates comply, both defect at the next step
(011 → 100). Under authority, they keep complying (011 → 111) and the superior, seeing compliance, resumes.
The subordinates are the core at 2.000 and the superior's value added is 0: delete him and the pair is
unchanged. Blau's legitimate authority is a structure in which the subordinates carry the compliance, and
the instrument adds that the one they comply with is no longer necessary to the structure that complies.

### H5 — opposition mirrors legitimation: PARTIAL

The dynamical clause held. Isolated subordinates who oppose when exploited, under a superior who yields to
joint opposition, never rest — the Coleman cycle — and subordinates who also oppose when the peer does
bring the superior to a standing yield. The mirror clause did not. Both forms have the subordinate pair as
core; authority binds it at 2.000 and shared opposition at 0.369. Approval and disapproval have the same
shape and are not the same structure.

## Discussion

Blau's alternatives are the instrument's gradient. Take the superior's two properties — is he in the core,
and is he necessary to it — and read the forms in Blau's order. Under simple power (H3's unorganized, H4's
power) he is in the core and indispensable, with an advantage of 2.000 over subordinates who add nothing.
Open the second alternative and the value moves to the dependent (H2). Open the third — the subordinates
organize — and he leaves the core but the core still needs him (H3): outside and indispensable, which is the
position Serres's parasite held and the Ostrom paper's federation and outsider held. Legitimate his power
and he is outside and dispensable (H4): the subordinates are the structure, and he is a condition they can
do without for a step and would resume serving if he returned. Blau wrote that legitimate authority "is the
basis of organization" (p. 23). On these forms it is the point at which the organization no longer contains
the one it obeys.

That is a sharper claim than Blau's and it matches the Coleman and Ostrom results from a third direction.
Coleman's one-way sanction ejected the sanctioned party; Ostrom's mutual sanction kept everyone in; Blau's
legitimation ejects the superior. In each case the party who leaves is the one whom the norm among the
others makes redundant. Under Coleman the others' norm is the sanction and the sanctioned is redundant to
it; under Blau the others' norm is compliance and the superior is redundant to it. Ostrom's appropriators
stay in because each is both the norm's subject and its carrier.

The failed mirror is the one result Blau would not have expected. He treats approval and disapproval as the
two outcomes of one process — a collectivity communicates its judgment and the judgment becomes pressure —
and the forms differ by a single negation. The instrument finds the approving pair five times tighter than
the disapproving one. The reason is in what the pair's state says about the superior. Under authority the
subordinates comply when served or when the peer complies, and at rest all three are on; the pair's states
constrain and are constrained by a superior who is also on. Under shared opposition the subordinates oppose
when exploited or when the peer opposes, and at rest the superior has yielded — S = 1 in the encoding, but
the disjunct that reads him is ¬S, which is off. The pair holds itself up by its own second disjunct alone,
and a pair held up by itself is weakly bound. Approval binds because it points at something present;
opposition, having won, points at nothing.

## Limitations

One rendering per claim. The first alternative (repay with a counter-service) and the fourth (do without)
were not modeled; the first would be a second service bit and the fourth a subordinate who reads nothing.
Value added reads a deleted variable as 0, which is what makes the organized superior indispensable (delete
him and the conjunction that read him is 0). The opposition forms are the Coleman norm forms relabeled and
their numbers agree with that paper's. Forms are two and three nodes; Blau's macrostructural chapters, in
which legitimation and opposition play out across collectivities, are not reached.

Results are about Boolean models. No superior or subordinate is measured.

## Conclusion

Blau's power is a relation because the service is contingent, and it is a relation that dissolves in the
order Blau gave. An alternative supplier moves the value to the dependent; organized subordinates put the
superior outside a core that still needs him; legitimation puts him outside a core that does not. The
subordinates' norm, once it carries compliance, carries the structure, and the superior it obeys is the one
part it can lose. Approval and disapproval are not mirrors: the pair that approves is bound to what it
approves of, and the pair that has won its opposition is bound to little.

## Appendix A — Forms

All in `forms.py`. `∧` AND, `∨` OR, `¬` NOT; a deleted variable reads 0; `1` the constant.

- **independent** S' = 1; B' = S.
- **contingent** / **single** S' = B; B' = S.
- **alternatives** S1' = B; S2' = B; B' = S1 ∨ S2.
- **unorganized** S' = B1 ∨ B2; B1' = S; B2' = S.
- **organized** S' = B1 ∨ B2; B1' = S ∧ B2; B2' = S ∧ B1.
- **power** S' = B1 ∧ B2; B1' = S; B2' = S.
- **authority** S' = B1 ∧ B2; B1' = S ∨ B2; B2' = S ∨ B1.
- **opposition_isolated** S' = B1 ∧ B2; B1' = ¬S; B2' = ¬S.
- **opposition_shared** S' = B1 ∧ B2; B1' = ¬S ∨ B2; B2' = ¬S ∨ B1.
