# Blau — Stage 2 literature

## Primary text

*Exchange and Power in Social Life* (1964) derives power from exchange. A person who supplies a service
others need and cannot repay holds a credit that they discharge by complying: "A person who commands
services others need, and who is independent of any at their command, attains power over others by making
the satisfaction of their need contingent on their compliance" (p. 22). Chapter 5 sets the conditions.
Compliance is the residual after four alternatives fail — repay with a counter-service, obtain the service
elsewhere, coerce the supplier, or do without (pp. 118–119) — so that "the supply of needed benefits creates
an undeniable claim to power unless one of four alternatives to submission exists" (p. 140). The book's
second movement is collective. "The weakness of the isolated subordinate limits the significance of his
approval or disapproval"; a collectivity of subordinates that approves the superior generates "group
pressures that promote compliance with the ruler's directives," which legitimates his authority, and a
collectivity that disapproves generates opposition (p. 23; chs. 8–9). Legitimate authority is defined by the
fact that "the normative constraints of the collectivity of subordinates ... enforce compliance with the
superior's commands" (p. 12). Beneath both runs "a strain toward imbalance as well as toward reciprocity"
(p. 26).

## What the literature did with it

Homans (1958) opened the exchange line; Emerson (1962) formalized power as dependence — A's power over B is
B's dependence on A — and named the balancing operations (withdrawal, network extension, status-giving,
coalition) that Blau (p. 118 n. 7) faults for diverting attention from imbalance. Cook and Emerson (1978)
took power-dependence into experimental exchange networks, where an actor's power is a function of
alternatives, which is Blau's second alternative made measurable; Emerson (1976) reviews the field. In all
of it a relation is a channel along which resources move and power is a ratio of dependences. None of it
asks whether the superior and subordinate are one thing, or what legitimation does to the structure that
the superior heads.

**The lab.** The Serres paper found that a one-way arrow from a constant producer binds nothing — the
producer is outside any complex and the receiver has no complex to be in. Blau's superior, if truly
independent, is that producer. But Blau's power is not the arrow: the service is contingent on compliance,
and contingency is a return. The Coleman paper found that an effective one-way norm ejects the sanctioned
party; the Ostrom paper found that mutual sanction keeps everyone in. Blau's legitimation — compliance
carried by the subordinates' norms — sits between: the superior does not sanction the subordinates' norm,
and the norm does not need him.

## The open gap

Whether contingency makes power a relation the arrow is not; whether an alternative supplier and an
organized pair of subordinates remove the superior's positional advantage; whether legitimated compliance
holds when the superior stops serving and whether it makes the subordinates a complex; and whether
opposition is legitimation with the sign of the superior reversed. The Coleman forms give the opposition
pair for free — isolated and shared opposition are the open and closed norm relabeled — and the question is
whether approval and disapproval bind alike.
