**Manuscript:** "Algorithmacy and Sovereignty: Toward a Coordinative Sovereignty for the Platform Era"
**Venue:** IGI Global edited volume, *Organizational Digital Sovereignty*
**Reviewer standpoint:** digital-sovereignty scholarship (Floridi; Pohle & Thiel; Couture & Toupin; Fratini et al.; the concept-stretching debate, Repetto; European digital-sovereignty policy)

## Step 0 — Register and bar

Register read correctly: this is an impersonal, agentive academic chapter in APA 7, double-anonymized, and the house style forbids "I/we." I judge it against that target ("the chapter argues," "Floridi shows"), not against a first-person or agentless-passive standard. The bar is a top venue in the digital-sovereignty conversation: the construct must be shown to survive Repetto's concept-stretching charge, the digital-vs-coordinative line must be clean and defensible, and the DS literature must be deployed accurately rather than tidied into a foil.

## Part 1 — Theory and structure, §1–§10

### §1 Introduction
The opening does the hardest job well. The compliance-yet-helpless gap (a GDPR-clean vendor, seller, or creator whose market position is decided inside ranking systems it cannot contest) is a genuine and specific puzzle, and it lands the chapter squarely in the volume's territory. The three-move setup — sovereignty always paired with a communicative sensibility; the platform situation as a new kind; the factorization preview ("could the mediating third party be replaced by a direct tie") — is efficient. The load-bearing sentence for this reviewer is line 51–53: "Digital sovereignty asks who controls the infrastructure. Coordinative sovereignty asks what autonomy remains for the actor coordinating through it." That contrast is the whole chapter, and it is only as strong as its characterization of digital sovereignty. See §2.3 and §6, where that characterization is where the manuscript is most exposed.

### §2.1–§2.2 Oracy, literacy
Clean, and analytically load-bearing rather than ornamental. The three properties of writing (persistence, portability, comparability) are put to work later, and the Innis/Eisenstein/Scott/Habermas/Anderson apparatus is deployed accurately and without overclaim. The explicit disclaiming of strong media determinism (line 106–108) is the right defensive move and will satisfy a media-theory referee. No objection.

### §2.3 Digital sovereignty as extended literacy — **the first load-bearing weak point**
The reframe is elegant and genuinely clarifying: digital sovereignty as "the literate sensibility carried into the digital domain," its instruments "technologies of reading and writing" (GDPR notice/consent/access, AI Act documentation/transparency, NIS2 written governance). For the *regulatory-transparency* strand of digital sovereignty this is accurate and productive.

The problem is that this strand is not the whole of digital sovereignty, and arguably not its center of gravity in the European policy discourse the chapter addresses. The dominant strand of EU digital-sovereignty policy is *infrastructural and industrial*: reducing dependence on US hyperscalers, sovereign cloud, Gaia-X, chip capacity, data localization, procurement rules, export controls. None of that governs "by making the digital object legible and attaching consequences to what the legible object shows" (line 122–124). It governs by jurisdiction, ownership, and capacity. Roberts et al. (2021) — cited by the chapter — frame digital sovereignty around *strategic autonomy* and values, not reading. Fratini et al. (2024) — the chapter's own warrant — include infrastructural and territorial models precisely because so much of the concept is not about legibility.

So the "extended literacy" thesis is a *partial fit*: it assimilates digital sovereignty to reading by quietly selecting the reading-heavy instruments and setting the capacity-building instruments aside. A digital-sovereignty referee will see the selection. Two defensible repairs: (a) restrict the claim to "regulatory digital sovereignty" and name the infrastructural strand as a second face the literacy frame does not capture (then fold it back in under §8's openness/control discussion, which already handles the sovereign-cloud case well); or (b) argue explicitly that ownership/capacity is itself in service of legibility-plus-consequence and defend that. As written, line 112 ("Digital sovereignty, in most of its current forms, is the literate sensibility carried into the digital domain") overstates and will draw fire. The hedge "in most of its current forms" is doing a lot of unearned work.

A second, subtler issue: the chapter treats "digital sovereignty" as a stable construct with a settled meaning ("the ability of states, organizations, and communities to exercise control over their digital infrastructures, data, and technologies"). But Pohle and Thiel (2020) — cited here — argue that digital sovereignty is a *discursive boundary object*, a contested and strategically deployed signifier, not a settled capability. Couture and Toupin (2019) — also cited here — write a critical genealogy showing the term carries plural and sometimes emancipatory meanings. The chapter cites both while flattening exactly the contestation those two papers exist to document. This flattening is what lets the later typology look clean; a referee steeped in Pohle & Thiel will read it as building the neat contrast on a tidied-up opponent. At minimum, one sentence should concede that digital sovereignty is internally contested and state that the chapter engages its control/legibility strand specifically.

### §3 The platform turn
Strong and fair. The three-way separation (coordination logic old; triadic topology old, Simmel/Burt/Obstfeld; the *conjunction* of opacity + interested adaptivity new) is the right way to defuse the "algorithmic management is just hierarchy" objection (Kellogg et al.; Vallas & Schor; Robinson & Meijerink), and the chapter states that objection at full strength before answering it. §3.2's four-part "why literacy cannot reach it" (Burrell's third opacity; Ananny & Crawford's transparency-without-institution; Cutolo & Kenney and Rahman on leverage; Hildebrandt on text-driven vs code/data-driven law) is the most persuasive stretch in the chapter and is deployed accurately. §3.3's bottleneck-rent argument (Rochet & Tirole; Armstrong & Wright's competitive bottleneck) is a clean political-economy anchor. Note that line 230's claim — "Surveys of the sovereignty concept confirm the gap ... none of them addresses the algorithmic coordination through which platforms enroll and bind (Fratini et al., 2024)" — hangs a strong exhaustiveness claim on a single survey. Soften to "the most systematic survey to date does not include a coordination-layer model," or add a second source. As stated it invites the referee to produce one counterexample and sink the sentence.

### §4 Algorithmacy
Largely outside the DS lens (this is the competence, developed in the companion work). It reads well and is honest about resting on the anonymized companion for the formal core. One DS-relevant strength: the closing move (line 318–328) that opacity is a *rent-preserving strategy* and that algorithmacy therefore distributes "like capital" connects the competence back to political economy and to the volume's equity concerns. Keep it.

### §5 The necessary/contingent diagnostic — the engine, and the real novelty
This is where coordinative sovereignty earns a place the neighbors do not occupy. The bypass counterfactual (restore the forbidden direct tie; recompute whether the mediator still binds) yields a four-way sort — necessary, contingent, partial, reducible — and §5.4's "a platform is a portfolio of mediators" is the analytically richest idea in the manuscript. The worked cases (payment network; marketplace's four simultaneous functions; app store distribution vs payment gate; the OTA billboard-vs-rate-parity split) are concrete and persuasive. §5.3's mapping of Hirschman exit→bypassability, voice→necessity is the hinge, and it is clean.

Two cautions for the DS referee. First, the diagnostic is presented as "computable" and "a designable fact," but the formal measures live in the companion (Author, 2026); the chapter itself offers only the informal bypass counterfactual. That is appropriately hedged (line 364–365), but the abstract and conclusion say "computable" without the hedge, and a referee will ask to see that the counterfactual is genuinely decidable on real coordinations rather than stipulated case by case. Second, the OTA empirics carry specific numbers that need sourcing (see VERIFY below).

### §6 The definition and its neighbors — where the concept-stretching charge is won or lost
The definition is tight: "the autonomy proper to being irreducibly coordinated: the power to shape the terms on which one is mediated, to contest the mediator's arbitrary power, and to hold a share in the governance of the arrangement one cannot leave," currency = effective voice. The four-strand grounding (Pettit/Skinner non-domination; Mackenzie & Stoljar/Nedelsky relational autonomy; Markell's correction that non-domination protects without including; Ostrom commons governance) is accurate and well-chosen. Muldoon & Raekstad (2022) is correctly read as the republican-domination bridge.

**Does the construct escape Repetto's concept-stretching charge?** Partly, and the chapter does not make its strongest available defense. Sartori's stretching is *extension up, intension down*: apply a word to more cases by dropping defining attributes, producing fog. Coordinative sovereignty does the opposite — it *adds* attributes (irreducible coordination, effective voice, non-domination, a governance share) and *narrows* extension (only the coordinated actor, only necessary mediators). Structurally it is concept *specification*, not stretching. That is a real and citable answer to Repetto, and the chapter should make it explicitly rather than leaving it implicit in the definition.

But there is a deeper stretching charge the chapter walks past. Sovereignty's classical core is *supreme, indivisible, ultimate authority* — the chapter itself invokes "Bodin's indivisible authority" (line 404). "A share in governance / effective voice within a coordination one cannot leave" is close to the *opposite* of that: it is partial, divided, participatory standing. Naming divided participatory standing "sovereignty" is exactly the relabeling move Repetto warns about, and it can be pressed harder against coordinative sovereignty than against the digital kind, not more weakly. Why is this not "coordinative *citizenship*," "coordinative *standing*," or "platform *voice*"? The concepts it actually draws on — non-domination, involvement, commons voice — are the vocabulary of republican citizenship and democratic participation, not of sovereignty.

The chapter has an answer buried in the conclusion (line 753): it stipulates a *functional* redefinition of sovereignty as "the standing to hold power to account through the medium in which power was exercised," a thread it runs through oracy and literacy. Under that stipulation coordinative sovereignty is consistent. But the stipulation is smuggled in through the historical narrative rather than defended as a redefinition, and it drops sovereignty's supremacy/indivisibility core without argument. A digital-sovereignty referee will demand that this stipulation be surfaced early (in §2 or §6), defended head-on, and reconciled with Krasner (1999) — cited but unused on exactly this point — who catalogs sovereignty's several meanings. The single most consequential revision for surviving Repetto is: *state and defend why "sovereignty" is the right genus for something whose content is voice and standing.* Right now the chapter distinguishes coordinative sovereignty carefully from *digital* sovereignty and never defends its claim to the word *sovereignty* at all.

**Is the digital-vs-coordinative distinction (who controls infrastructure vs what standing the coordinated hold) clean?** Mostly yes, and it is the chapter's best contribution. The §8 independent-variation cases make it concrete and are genuinely illuminating (see below). But the cleanness depends on digital sovereignty being *only* about infrastructure/data control. Two blurring points a referee will press:

- **Data sovereignty already carries a standing dimension.** Hummel et al. (2021) — cited — review "data sovereignty" as control over how data about a subject is used, and community/indigenous data-sovereignty frameworks are explicitly about self-determination over one's data within a relationship. That is close to "standing within a coordination." The boundary between "control your data" (filed as digital) and "hold standing within the coordination that runs on your data" (filed as coordinative) is not as sharp as the typology needs.
- **The chapter's own §7 data trusts are a data-sovereignty instrument.** Data trusts and data cooperatives (Micheli et al.; Delacroix & Lawrence) are squarely data-sovereignty mechanisms, yet the chapter lists them under *coordinative* sovereignty. The chapter's defense is that here they function as *voice* levers, not ownership. Fair, but the boundary needs an explicit sentence, or a referee will say the typology relabels digital-sovereignty instruments as coordinative when convenient.

**Fairness to the §6 neighbors — Bannerman, Pasquale, Cohen, Muldoon & Raekstad.**
- *Pasquale (2023), functional sovereignty; Cohen (2017), the platform sovereign* — handled cleanly and fairly. These theorize the authority the platform holds *over* the coordinated; coordinative sovereignty theorizes the standing of the coordinated *within* that authority. That is a genuine complementarity, correctly stated (line 520–524). No objection.
- *Bannerman (2024), relational sovereignty* — the weakest differentiation, because relational sovereignty is the *closest* competitor: both reject the independence/control model in favor of the relationship, both attend to structural asymmetry, both are actor-relational. The chapter's distinguisher ("looks at the same relationship from the other end" plus "comes paired with a diagnostic") under-sells the real difference, which is the necessary/contingent diagnostic and the exit/voice determination it yields. Lead the Bannerman contrast with the diagnostic, not the "other end" metaphor, and say what coordinative sovereignty asserts that relational sovereignty cannot (that whether voice is the *only* remaining autonomy is a computable property of the coordination's rules).
- *Muldoon & Raekstad (2022)* — **a fairness-plus-consistency problem.** Line 519–520 says they "center protection from arbitrary power and often recommends exit through cooperative ownership." But cooperative ownership does not let a worker *exit* the coordination — the worker still coordinates through the co-op platform; ownership changes *who governs* the mediation. By the chapter's own framework that is voice/ownership at a necessary mediator, not exit. And §7 (line 559–565) treats platform cooperativism as exactly that: "voice becomes ownership." So the chapter calls cooperative ownership *exit* when attributing it to Muldoon & Raekstad and *voice* when adopting it in §7. That is internally inconsistent, and it may also misread Muldoon & Raekstad. Resolve it: either cooperativism is a voice-at-necessary-mediator institution throughout (my reading, and the chapter's in §7), in which case the M&R gloss should not call it exit; or specify the sense in which member-ownership counts as exit from the *domination relation* while remaining voice within the *coordination*. As written a referee will catch the slippage and it dents the exit/voice distinction the whole argument turns on.

The three clarifications guarding the definition (more than worker rights; transparency does not produce it; not anti-platform) are well-judged and pre-empt the obvious misreadings. Keep them.

### §7 Realizing coordinative sovereignty
A competent institutional survey (cooperativism/Scholz; data trusts/Micheli, Delacroix & Lawrence; collective bargaining/Lei; contestability/Alfrink et al.; interoperability as an exit move; oversight boards/Klonick; DSA researcher access and the platform-work directive). The organizing discipline — the diagnostic sorts exit-restoring instruments to contingent functions and voice-building institutions to necessary ones — is the section's value and is maintained. Two notes: the data-trust/data-sovereignty boundary flagged above surfaces here and should be named; and the interoperability-as-exit framing is good but should acknowledge that mandated interoperability is itself a flagship *digital-sovereignty* instrument (DMA), which strengthens rather than threatens the chapter — it shows the two sovereignties calling for different tools on different functions of the same firm.

### §8 Implications — the strongest empirical illustration
The independent-variation cases are the best evidence for the two-construct thesis and should be foregrounded: high digital / low coordinative (a marketplace seller owning its code and data, GDPR- and DMA-compliant, with no leverage over ranking) and low digital / high coordinative (a platform worker owning no infrastructure but holding information, human-review, data-limitation, and collective-bargaining rights under Directive 2024/2831). This is exactly the "operationalization of digital sovereignty" the volume asks for, and it makes the distinction concrete rather than merely asserted. The openness/control reframe (line 686–702) is a genuine contribution to the European digital-sovereignty debate: a closed national sovereign platform whose users have no voice "reproduces the co-optation it was meant to escape, now under a domestic flag." That is a quotable, policy-relevant result and directly answers a live question in the field. Keep it prominent. VERIFY the "market-conduct remedies of 2025" claim (line 636) — it is vague; name the instrument or decision.

### §9 Future research
Honest and appropriately modest: no validated instrument for algorithmacy, none for coordinative sovereignty, and the central empirical claim (independent variation, differential prediction) "can be argued but not tested" until instruments exist. The discriminant-validity targets (against Dogruel et al. algorithm-literacy scales; against Spreitzer psychological empowerment; against digital-sovereignty measures) are the right ones. The reported-vs-afforded-standing validity test (survey vs formal model) is a real strength that the construct's formal companion uniquely enables. No objection.

### §10 Conclusion
Ties the three-sensibilities arc to the exit/voice payoff and states the stakes without inflation. The "constitutive priesthood" image is apt. The one substantive issue is the one already raised: the conclusion is where the functional redefinition of sovereignty ("the standing to hold power to account through the medium") finally becomes explicit — it needs to appear far earlier and be defended, not arrive as a summary.

### What a digital-sovereignty referee would demand
1. **Defend the word "sovereignty."** Surface the functional redefinition early, reconcile it with Bodin's indivisibility (which you cite against yourself) and with Krasner's catalog, and say why not "citizenship"/"standing." This is the make-or-break for Repetto.
2. **Fix the §2.3 over-reduction.** Concede the infrastructural/capacity strand of digital sovereignty that "extended literacy" does not cover; restrict the literacy claim or fold the capacity strand in explicitly.
3. **Stop flattening digital sovereignty into "a move to centralize control" (line 510).** Couture & Toupin — whom you cite in the same sentence — document grassroots, emancipatory, non-centralizing variants (technological sovereignty, community data sovereignty). The claim as written misreads a source you rely on. Qualify to "in its state- and firm-centric variants."
4. **Resolve the Muldoon & Raekstad exit/voice inconsistency** between §6 and §7.
5. **Sharpen the Bannerman contrast** around the diagnostic.
6. **Soften line 230's exhaustiveness claim** and source the OTA and 2025-remedy empirics.

### Do the load-bearing weak points survive?
- The **"sovereignty" naming** survives *if and only if* the functional redefinition is made explicit and defended; as currently smuggled in, it does not survive a determined concept-stretching referee.
- The **§2.3 literacy reduction** survives with a scope restriction; it does not survive as the unqualified "in most of its current forms."
- The **digital-vs-coordinative distinction** survives — it is the chapter's real contribution — but needs the data-sovereignty boundary named to stay clean.
- The **flattening of digital sovereignty to centralization** does *not* survive contact with the chapter's own citations and must be qualified.
- The **necessary/contingent diagnostic and the §8 variation cases** survive comfortably and are the chapter's strength.

## Verdict

**Major revision.** The core contribution — the necessary/contingent diagnostic, the platform-as-portfolio-of-mediators, and the independently varying two-construct typology with its concrete high-digital/low-coordinative and low-digital/high-coordinative cases — is novel, well-grounded in the digital-sovereignty and platform-governance literatures, and squarely on-topic for the volume. It is not a reject: the construct is specified, not stretched, and the empirical illustrations are strong. But it is not yet a minor revision, because the piece has not defended its claim to the word *sovereignty*, over-reduces digital sovereignty to literacy in §2.3, misreads a source it relies on (Couture & Toupin) when it flattens digital sovereignty to "centralize control," and carries an internal exit/voice inconsistency around cooperativism. Each is fixable without new research.

**Single most important fix:** surface and defend the functional redefinition of sovereignty (standing-to-hold-power-to-account-through-the-medium) early and explicitly, reconciling it with Bodin's indivisibility and Krasner's plural meanings and saying why the object is "sovereignty" rather than "citizenship" or "standing." This is what determines whether the construct survives Repetto's concept-stretching charge — and right now the defense exists only implicitly, in the conclusion.

**Biggest genuine strength:** the necessary/contingent bypass diagnostic and its exit/voice payoff, made concrete by the portfolio-of-mediators reading (one firm, four kinds of mediator) and the independent-variation cases in §8. This is the part no neighbor in the crowded field supplies, and it converts "who controls the infrastructure vs what standing the coordinated hold" from a slogan into an analytically usable, potentially measurable distinction.

**The one thing only the author can supply:** a defense — and honest test — of the claim that whether a mediator is necessary or contingent is genuinely *computable* on real coordinations rather than assigned case by case by the analyst. The chapter asserts computability (abstract, §5, conclusion) but relegates the formal measures to the anonymized companion. A digital-sovereignty audience will accept the typology's payoff only if the classification is shown to be decidable and reproducible on at least one non-toy coordination; only the author, holding the companion apparatus, can demonstrate that the OTA, app-store, and payment-network sorts fall out of the rules rather than out of the author's intuition.
