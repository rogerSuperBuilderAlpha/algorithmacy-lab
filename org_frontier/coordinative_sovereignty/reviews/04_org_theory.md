## Part 1 — Theory and structure, §1–§10

### §1 Introduction
The opening move is strong and correctly org-theoretic: the gap it names is not a data-protection gap but a coordination gap, and the ISV/marketplace-seller/creator anchors are the right ones. Two structural cautions.

First, the load-bearing sentence — "Model a coordination as a small causal system and ask whether the mediating third party could be replaced by a direct tie" (lines 43–46) — front-loads the *bypass* counterfactual as *the* distinction, but §5 actually runs **two** counterfactuals (factoring/irreducibility in §5.1, bypass in §5.2) and treats them as one diagnostic. The introduction should not promise a single test if the body deploys two (see §5 below — this is the sharpest analytic problem in the chapter).

Second, "cannot see, predict, or contest" (line 29) and "neither see nor predict nor contest" (line 46 region) plant a triad that then recurs verbatim across the chapter. Flagged in Part 2.

### §2 Sovereignty and its sensibilities
The oracy→literacy→algorithmacy scaffold is the chapter's rhetorical engine and it is well built. §2.2's persistence/portability/comparability triad is genuinely load-bearing (each term does distinct work — passes the CLAUDE.md triad test). The Innis/Eisenstein media-theory hedge (lines 102–108) is exactly the right amount of self-inoculation against media determinism, and §2.3's "nesting" argument (each sovereignty layers rather than replaces) is the correct org-theoretic frame — it mirrors how coordination mechanisms coexist rather than supersede. No major objection. Minor: the "characteristic error" diagnosis (line 135, literate actors demand more disclosure because reading is what they know) is one of the best ideas in the chapter and is buried mid-paragraph; it deserves to surface as a claim-first sentence.

### §3 The platform turn — coordination through an opaque, interested mediator
This is where an org-theory referee earns their keep, and the chapter mostly survives the encounter because it **declines** the overclaim rather than making it. Lines 158–178 are the pivotal passage: "The observation is important, and it is easy to overclaim. Do platforms really introduce a new coordination logic in Powell's sense? What they run is largely the existing logics in algorithmic form." Relocating the novelty from *logic* (old: price/authority/trust) and *topology* (old: the triad, the broker) to the **conjunction of opacity + interestedness + adaptivity in the mediator** is a defensible and disciplined thesis. This is Paper-1-grade care. Keep it.

Three demands a coordination referee will still press:

1. **The missing Thompson.** The chapter's coordination-forms citation set is "Adler, 2001; Powell, 1990; Thompson, 2003; Williamson, 1975" (line 150), where Thompson is **G. F. (Grahame) Thompson, *Between Hierarchies and Markets* (2003)** — a networks book. The canonical organization-theory treatment of *mediation as a coordination technology* is **James D. Thompson, *Organizations in Action* (1967)**, whose typology of long-linked / **mediating** / intensive technologies defines "mediating technology" as the linking of clients who wish to be interdependent (the bank between depositors and borrowers; the employment agency; the telephone exchange). That is *precisely* the chapter's "mediated triad," named forty years earlier and in org theory's own vocabulary. Its absence is the most conspicuous citation gap in the chapter. Adding J. D. Thompson (1967) both strengthens the lineage and sharpens the novelty claim: the mediating *technology* is old (Thompson); what is new is the mediating *agent's* opacity, interest, and adaptivity. **[VERIFY the two Thompsons are distinct — I am confident: J. D. Thompson 1967 ≠ G. F. Thompson 2003 — but confirm before citing.]**

2. **The hybrids literature is under-engaged.** The chapter waves at "hybrids of market and hierarchy" via Robinson & Meijerink (2026) and Vallas & Schor (2020) (line 164) but never engages the transaction-cost hybrids tradition (Ménard, "The economics of hybrid organizations," 2004; Williamson's own hybrid governance). Since the chapter's whole strategy is "the logic is old, only the mediator's properties are new," a referee will ask why the platform is not simply a *hybrid governance form* with an automated coordination device. The answer is available in the chapter (the interested/opaque/adaptive third party is not a governance *structure* but a governance *agent* with its own objective function), but it should be made explicitly against Ménard/Williamson, not against the two most recent citations.

3. **Selznick and the co-optation overclaim — the real hazard.** This needs care equal to what Paper 1 demands. The chapter adopts Stark and Vanden Broeck's (2024) "co-opted" as the platform's coordination verb (command/contract/collaborate/co-opt) and then correctly notes the Selznick heritage: "Selznick's (1949) co-optation absorbed outside actors into an organization's leadership to secure its stability... Where Selznick's co-opted gained a limited seat, the platform-enrolled gain no seat and no share, only a determination" (lines 154–156). The problem is that this reading **strips co-optation of its defining feature and then keeps the word.** Selznick's co-optation is a *reciprocal* mechanism: an organization absorbs a threat *by conceding participation* (formal co-optation shares the appearance of power; informal co-optation shares actual power in response to real pressure). The *concession* is the analytic content — co-optation is how an organization buys legitimacy and stability by granting a seat. The platform case grants **no** seat and **no** concession; it is enrollment *without* the co-optative bargain. Calling that "co-optation" is close to using the term for its antonym. The chapter half-sees this ("the harder case," "no seat, no share") but treats the difference as a matter of *degree* ("harder") when it is a difference in *kind* (the reciprocal concession is absent). An org-theory referee will either want (a) an explicit argument that "co-optation" survives the loss of its reciprocal core — perhaps that the platform performs *formal* co-optation (the simulacrum of participation, e.g., ratings "voice," appeals theater) without even informal concession — or (b) a more honest label (enrollment, subsumption, capture). My recommendation: keep the word but confront the concession problem head-on in one sentence, distinguishing Selznick's *formal* co-optation (appearance without power) from the platform's enrollment-without-appearance, so the term is earned rather than borrowed. As written, "the platform form is the harder case" (line 155) papers over the strongest objection.

Note also: the chapter's Selznick gloss ("absorbed outside actors into an organization's **leadership**") is a slight narrowing — Selznick's TVA co-optation operated through *program administration and grassroots absorption*, not only leadership. Minor, but a Selznick specialist will notice. **[VERIFY the "leadership" framing against TVA and the Grass Roots.]**

### §3.1 The mediated triad — the Simmel treatment
The triadic reframing is correct and the driver/rider/system exposition (lines 182–188) is vivid and accurate. But there is a terminological tension that a Simmel-literate referee will press hard, and it runs through the whole chapter: **"mediator" is the wrong Simmelian role for what the chapter describes.**

Simmel's typology of the third (*tertius*) has three roles: (1) the **non-partisan / mediator** (the *disinterested* go-between who reconciles), (2) the ***tertius gaudens*** (the third who *profits* from the two others' relation), and (3) **divide et impera** (the third who actively *sets the two against each other* to hold power). The chapter's third party is emphatically **not** the mediator (role 1): it is "interested and adaptive," it "pursues objectives of its own," it "extracts the surplus," and its opacity keeps the two sides from closing the tie. That is *tertius gaudens* fused with *divide et impera* — roles 2 and 3 — the *interested* third, which is exactly the role Simmel contrasts with the mediator. Using the neutral term "mediator" (and "mediated triad") throughout thus fights the chapter's own argument: every time the prose says "mediator," a Simmel reader hears "disinterested go-between," which is precisely what the chapter is at pains to deny.

Two ways out, either acceptable: (a) own "mediator" openly as a coined term of art and *say so* once — "this chapter uses *mediator* for any third party through which coordination runs, disinterested or not, departing from Simmel's narrower usage"; or (b) engage Simmel's three roles explicitly and locate the platform as *tertius gaudens*/divide-et-impera, which would actually *strengthen* the political-economy argument in §3.3 (the rent and the opacity-as-strategy claims are textbook *tertius gaudens*). Right now Simmel is cited once (line 171) for "the triad" in general and the richer, more apt part of his analysis is left on the table. This is the same shape of missed-lineage problem as the J. D. Thompson gap: the classical source that most directly grounds the argument is cited thinly and for the wrong sub-claim.

On the brokerage engagement (Burt 2004 / Obstfeld 2005, lines 169–171): correctly used, and the *tertius iungens* / *tertius gaudens* pairing is exactly the right frame. But see §5.2 — this pairing is so close to the necessary/contingent test that the chapter must say what the test *adds* to it.

Is "fourth mechanism / co-optation" overclaimed? **The chapter itself resolves this well** — it explicitly refuses the "fourth logic in Powell's sense" reading (lines 158–164) and relocates novelty in the mediator's properties. That refusal is the correct answer and matches the dissertation's altitude discipline. The residual risk is only that it *adopts* Stark and Vanden Broeck's "co-opted" as a settled fourth verb in line 152 before qualifying it in line 158; a referee reading linearly meets the overclaim before the correction. Tighten by front-loading the hedge.

### §3.2 Why literacy cannot reach it

The closing line "This is the precise content of co-optation" (line 256) inherits the §3 co-optation problem: it defines co-optation as "enrollment into a coordination authored by an interested third party, without the delegated authority of a hierarchy or the negotiated terms of a contract," which is a *good* definition of the platform relation but is, again, not Selznick's co-optation (no concession). ### §5 The diagnostic — the analytic core, and where the referee bites hardest

**The central problem: §5.1 and §5.2 are two different counterfactuals presented as one diagnostic.**

- §5.1 asks the **irreducibility/factoring** question: does the *current* modeled system decompose without loss? "does some cut of the modeled system lose causal information that exists only in the joint behavior of its parts" (line 361). This sorts *conduit* (factors → the three parties never acted as one) from *constitutive* (survives no cut).
- §5.2 asks the **bypass** question: *add a direct tie that the current system forbids* and recompute whether the mediator still binds (lines 371–374). This sorts *necessary* (keeps its grip) from *contingent* (loses it).

These are not the same test, and their relationship is left implicit. A mediator can be **irreducible as things now stand yet contingent under bypass**: the contingent gate (rate-parity clause, in-app-payment gate, franchise exclusivity) genuinely carries all the coordination *today* — the current system does not factor, because everything really does flow through the gate — but adding the forbidden direct tie dissolves it. So "genuinely triadic / irreducible" (§5.1) does **not** entail "necessary" (§5.2). The chapter needs one explicit paragraph reconciling them: irreducibility (§5.1) establishes that the triad is *real* — the mediator is constitutive, not a mere conduit/reducible — and the bypass test (§5.2) then sorts the *real* triads into necessary vs. contingent by asking whether the constitution depends on a *forbidden tie* or on *genuine integration*. Without that reconciliation, §5.1's closing sentence — "For the argument here, the runnable counterfactual of the next section is enough" (line 364) — reads as though §5.2 *subsumes* §5.1, which it does not: the bypass test presupposes you have already established the triad is constitutive. If §5.2 alone were enough, §5.1 would be decorative, and it is not. This is the one place where the IIT/computational grounding, cited as "Author, 2026," risks doing hidden work: the reader is told the companion paper's integrated-information measures "make the check exact" (line 363), but *which* check — factoring or bypass? The chapter should say the measures formalize the *factoring/irreducibility* question (§5.1) and that the bypass counterfactual (§5.2) is a *separate*, cheaper, org-theoretic move that the org-theory reader can run without buying IIT. Keeping those two straight is also what keeps the apparatus properly subordinated — right now the conflation lets the formal apparatus stand behind a distinction it may not fully cover.

**Is necessary/contingent a genuine contribution or a restatement of make-vs-buy / substitutability / structural-hole-dependence?** This is the question the chapter must answer and currently under-answers. The distinction has at least four close neighbors the chapter does not confront:

1. **Burt's structural holes (already cited).** A Burtian broker's advantage *is* the hole; if the brokered parties can close it, the advantage evaporates. "Contingent mediator" = broker across a *fillable* hole; "necessary mediator" = value from integration, not from the hole. The chapter cites Hahl et al. (2016) for the informal version (line 371) and claims the counterfactual "turns that observation into a computable property." A referee will ask: is computability the *whole* contribution? Because Burt already distinguishes hole-dependent from value-adding intermediation qualitatively.
2. **Williamson make-vs-buy / asset specificity.** "Necessary" ≈ genuine integration you cannot internalize cheaply; "contingent" ≈ a function held outside only by an external constraint, i.e., could be internalized/bypassed. The chapter never names TCE here even though its own franchise-dealer example is a *textbook* vertical-restraint / make-vs-buy case.
3. **Essential-facilities doctrine (antitrust).** "Necessary mediator" is, almost exactly, an *essential facility*: an input competitors cannot practicably duplicate and must access. This is the legal-economic analog and it is missing. It matters because §7's policy claims (interoperability vs. voice-building) map directly onto the essential-facilities remedy debate.
4. **Multihoming / bypassability (two-sided markets).** The chapter *does* cite Eisenmann et al. (2006) on multihoming (line 411–413), which is good, but only in §5.3, not as a rival account of the same distinction.

The contribution *is* real, and the chapter can defend it, but it must state the value-add explicitly rather than implying novelty. The genuine additions over the four neighbors are: (a) it is a property of the coordination's **causal rules**, applied by a *stated counterfactual* rather than a case-by-case judgment call; (b) it is applied at the **function** level, not the firm or market level (§5.4 — this is the strongest differentiator and is genuinely more granular than essential-facilities or make-vs-buy, which are usually run at the market/firm level); (c) it is tied to a **remedy taxonomy** (exit vs. voice) rather than left as a positive description. Say this. As written, the sentence "Brokerage research reached the difference informally... A single counterfactual turns that observation into a computable property" (lines 370–374) claims priority a little too breezily — the essential-facilities and multihoming literatures reached it fairly *formally*, not just informally.

**The bypass counterfactual is treated as binary; real bypass is a cost continuum.** "Restore the direct tie... and recompute whether the mediator still binds" (line 374) is binary (tie open/closed). But the hotel case (§5.4) shows bypass is a *matter of degree and demand segment*: direct booking climbs "to roughly a third and then plateaus" (line 458). That is not "the mediator loses its grip"; it is *partial* substitution at the margin, segment-dependent (the traveler who already knows the hotel can bypass; the one who does not, cannot). So *necessity is relative to a demand segment*, not a property of the function alone. The four-way typology's "partial" category (line 389–392) is meant to absorb this, but the binary framing of the counterfactual in §5.2 sits awkwardly with the continuous reality the chapter's own best example displays. Recommend acknowledging that the counterfactual yields a *degree* (how much of the coordination survives the open tie) and that necessary/contingent are the endpoints, with partial the interior — which the typology already implies but the prose contradicts.

### §5.3 Exit and voice
The Hirschman mapping (exit↔bypassability, voice↔necessity) is clean and the monopoly-restricts-exit-elevates-voice point (line 400–402) is correctly attributed. Two gaps a coordination referee will note:

1. **Loyalty is dropped.** Hirschman's framework is exit/voice/**loyalty**, and loyalty is the term that governs *whether an actor uses exit even when it is technically available*. In platform contexts, lock-in, sunk investment in a store of ratings/reputation, and habituation are exactly Hirschman's loyalty — they suppress exit against *contingent* mediators, which is why "exit is available in principle" (line 408) so often fails in practice. Dropping loyalty lets the chapter treat contingent = exitable too cleanly. One sentence restoring loyalty would make the exit case honest.
2. **Voice without an exit threat is Hirschman's *weak* voice.** Hirschman's core worry is that voice gets its teeth from the *credible threat of exit*; under a necessary mediator (no exit), what disciplines the mediator into hearing voice? The chapter's answer is the §7 institutional stack (cooperatives, bargaining, contestability), but §5.3 asserts "only voice remains" (line 418) without acknowledging that voice-without-exit is precisely the case Hirschman flags as *structurally weak*. This is not fatal — it is the chapter's whole reason for §6–§7 — but §5.3 should name the tension rather than let it surface only implicitly.

### §5.4 A platform is a portfolio of mediators — the strongest section
The function-not-firm move is the chapter's best organization-theoretic contribution and it is exactly right: coordination theory's unit of analysis has always been the *transaction/function*, not the firm (this is Williamsonian, and it is more granular than essential-facilities analysis, which usually runs at the firm/market level). The four-function decomposition of a marketplace (fulfillment = necessary; matching = partial; gate/placement = contingent; resale = reducible; lines 428–433) is sharp, and "One firm, four kinds of mediator at once" (line 433) is a genuinely useful diagnostic slogan. The travel/OTA worked case (lines 450–461) is excellent and honest — billboard (necessary) vs. rate-parity gate (contingent), with the empirical plateau doing real work. Keep all of it.

Three refinements:

1. **"Mediator" consistency — firm vs. function.** §3.1–§3.3 treat "the mediator" as a *unitary interested agent with objectives*; §5.4 declares it *four mediators*. The section fixes the *classification* ambiguity ("classifies functions, not firms," line 423) but not the *interest* ambiguity: the objective function (opacity, rent-seeking) is a **firm-level** property, while necessary/contingent is a **function-level** property. The chapter never reconciles these, and the reconciliation is where the argument gets *more* powerful, not less — because the single firm-level interest operates *across* functions by using the **necessary** functions to protect the **contingent** rents. That is **tying / bundling / monopoly leverage**, the classic antitrust and org-theory concern, and it is exactly why the OTA bundles the billboard (necessary) with the parity clause (contingent), and why the app store bundles distribution (necessary) with the payment gate (contingent). The chapter gestures at it ("interoperability aimed at the contingent functions relieves pressure on the necessary ones," line 616) but never names the leverage mechanism. Naming tying/leverage would (a) resolve the firm/function tension, (b) explain *why* platforms assemble the portfolio the way they do, and (c) connect directly to §7's remedy logic. This is the single most valuable *addition* available to the chapter.

2. **The dealer "integrates nothing" is overstated.** "A franchised car dealer relays a vehicle from manufacturer to buyer and integrates nothing" (line 383). A dealer in fact integrates inventory-holding, financing, test drives, trade-ins, and warranty service — an org theorist reads this as *at least partial*, not purely contingent. The example is chosen for clean contrast but overstates to get there, and the chapter's own four-way typology gives it the honest label (partial). Either soften to "integrates little that the direct tie cannot reproduce" or move the dealer to the *partial* column. **[VERIFY the franchise-law direct-sale prohibition claim — accurate for US state dealer-franchise laws and the Tesla direct-sales litigation, but "franchise law" stated generically should be scoped.]**

3. **The IIT cite lands here too.** "The diagnostic disaggregates the dependence into the parts that can be exited and the parts that can only be governed (Author, 2026)" (line 440). This portfolio/function disaggregation is a *conceptual* org-theory move that needs no formal apparatus; attaching "Author, 2026" to it risks implying the formal paper is doing work the plain argument already does. Consider dropping the cite here (or making explicit that the companion paper *illustrates* the classification formally, rather than supplying it).

### §6 Definition
The four political-theory strands (Pettit/Skinner non-domination; Mackenzie & Stoljar / Nedelsky relational autonomy; Markell's correction; Ostrom commons) are outside my primary lens, and the non-domination and relational-autonomy imports are handled competently. The org-theory-relevant strand is **Ostrom**, and here the analogy strains in a way a referee should catch. Ostrom's commons is a shared resource governed by *symmetrically positioned appropriators* who face **each other and the resource** — there is *no interested owner extracting rent*. The platform is not a common-pool resource the coordinated co-own; it is a **privately owned bottleneck with its own objective function**. Carrying Ostrom "from the shared pasture to the shared platform" (line 504) glosses over exactly the feature that makes the platform hard — the third party's *interest*, which Ostrom's framework does not model because her appropriators have no owner sitting across from them. The import is still useful (design principles as institutional forms of voice) but needs the caveat that the platform "commons" has a *rent-seeking owner*, which is why the Ostromian solution (self-governance without a central authority) may be *unavailable* precisely in the necessary-mediator case the construct is built for. State the limit of the analogy in one sentence.

The neighbor-differentiation (Bannerman relational sovereignty; Muldoon & Raekstad; Pasquale functional sovereignty; Cohen platform sovereign; lines 517–525) is well done and the "looks at the same relationship from the other end" framing is a clean way to claim the unoccupied space. The three clarifications (lines 527–537: more-than-rights, not-transparency, not-anti-platform) are each earned. §6 is strong apart from the Ostrom caveat.

### §7 Realizing coordinative sovereignty
The institutional survey is well-organized by the diagnostic (exit-restoring instruments at contingent functions; voice-building at necessary ones). Platform cooperativism, data trusts, collective bargaining, contestability, oversight boards, and regulation are each correctly characterized, and the honesty about limits (cooperatives' scale/capital problem, line 563; oversight boards' dependence on the platform, line 601) is appropriate. The "institutions compose" point (line 614) and the stack argument are good.

One org-theory demand: the section reads as a *list* of instruments rather than an analysis of *why some are structurally harder than others*. The deepest determinant — whether contention can *organize at all* — is cited once (Lei, 2021, "the architecture of a platform shapes where grievances pool," line 580) and then dropped. That is the most organization-theoretic point in §7 and it deserves to frame the section rather than sit inside the bargaining paragraph: the platform's *architecture* is itself a variable that determines which voice institutions are buildable, so the mediator does not merely resist voice — it shapes the *feasibility set* of voice institutions. Elevating Lei would make §7 an argument rather than a catalog.

### §8 Implications
The two-construct disaggregation (digital sovereignty = literacy construct = capacity to read/control; coordinative sovereignty = algorithmacy construct = standing within coordination) is clearly stated and the independent-variation cases (high-digital/low-coordinative ISV; low-infrastructure/rising-standing platform worker under the 2024 directive) are concrete and correct. The managerial decision procedure (contingent → exit; necessary → voice; misclassification wastes effort either way, lines 660–665) is genuinely useful and is the chapter's clearest practitioner payoff. The openness/control reframing (lines 686–702) is a good policy point — standing is orthogonal to national control, and the "closed sovereign national platform reproduces co-optation under a domestic flag" argument is sharp. No major objection. The honest limit ("until both can be measured, the typology's central empirical claim... can be argued but not tested," lines 649–650) is exactly the right altitude.

### §9 Future research
The four directions (algorithmacy instrument; coordinative-sovereignty instrument; two-step classify-then-measure design; reported-vs-afforded correspondence) are well-sequenced and the discriminant-validity worries (vs. Dogruel's algorithm-literacy scale; vs. Spreitzer's psychological empowerment) are the right ones. The behavioral-criterion anchoring proposal (line 714–718) is methodologically sound. The two-step design (line 731–737) is where the diagnostic earns its empirical keep and is the most promising item. No objection.

### §10 Conclusion
Competent and mostly non-repetitive, though it re-runs the who-controls-vs-what-standing antithesis and the exit/voice mapping one more time (see Part 2). "constitutive priesthood" is a strong close. "Neither reading alone nor control alone secures it" (line 784) is an acceptable closer, not empty.

## Part 2 — Style / slop audit (register-aware)

## Verdict

**Major revisions.** The chapter is conceptually ambitious, well-cited, honest about its limits, and written cleanly in the target register — it is far above the drafts the house style was built to correct. But three things stand between it and acceptance by a coordination-theory referee, in priority order: (1) the **§5.1/§5.2 conflation** — two distinct counterfactuals presented as one diagnostic, which the whole apparatus rests on; (2) the **classical-lineage gaps** — J. D. Thompson's mediating technology absent, Simmel's *tertius gaudens* under-used while the neutral word "mediator" fights the argument, and the necessary/contingent test not defended against Burt / Williamson / essential facilities; (3) the **Selznick co-optation term** kept after its defining concession is stripped. None is fatal; each is fixable with the additions in Part 3, and several *strengthen* the argument (the tying mechanism in R7, the Simmel relabel in R3).

**Most important fix:** R4 — reconcile the factoring test (§5.1) and the bypass test (§5.2) explicitly, because "irreducible" and "necessary" are not the same property and the chapter's central claim depends on the reader not conflating them.

## Biggest strength

The **function-not-firm portfolio view (§5.4)**, paired with the exit/voice remedy split. Decomposing one platform into necessary/partial/contingent/reducible *functions* and routing exit-instruments at the contingent ones and voice-institutions at the necessary ones is a genuinely useful, more granular unit of analysis than the firm-level or market-level treatments in essential-facilities, make-vs-buy, or digital-sovereignty scholarship. The OTA billboard-vs-parity-gate case makes it concrete and the managerial decision procedure in §8 makes it operational. This is the chapter's real contribution and it is defensible against a hostile referee.

That determination governs how the apparatus is subordinated in §5 and whether the "Author, 2026" cites in §5.1, §5.2, and §5.4 are each pointing at the work they claim.
