## 1. Who I am

I teach media and communication and I wrote a book about writing technologies and political order — how the charter, the register, the printed edict and the file each produced a distinct relation between a ruler and the people who had to live under one. That book spent its first hundred pages disassembling the Great Divide: the idea that there are oral societies and literate societies, that the second are a different kind of thing from the first, and that the difference explains politics. I did not disassemble it because it is stupid. I disassembled it because it is *attractive*, because it produces beautiful three-act architecture, and because every generation of media scholarship rebuilds it under a new name — print culture, the digital native, the network society — and has to be talked down again. I read the algorithmic-literacy literature closely and with some sympathy; DeVito, Dogruel, Sutherland, Sander, Gagrčin and Eslami are on my syllabus. This chapter reached me because a colleague forwarded it with the subject line "oracy → literacy → algorithmacy" and no message body, which I understood as a dare. I came to it expecting to find the Divide readmitted through the coordination door. I found something more interesting: a chapter that knows the objection, tries to pay it off in one paragraph of §4, and pays with a coin the creditor does not accept. I also found, in §5, an argument that does not need the arc at all — which is the best news in this review and the hardest thing for the author to hear.

## 3. Part 1 — the read

Ranked by damage.

### F1. §4 — the triadic index does not separate algorithmacy from DeVito's own cases. **[FIX NOW]**

The chapter's novelty claim, after the prior panels narrowed it, is the index: "algorithmacy describes an actor working a system that is simultaneously matching it to a counterpart and scoring it on how that match goes," and "the novelty claimed against these accounts is that index and nothing larger." Key Terms repeats it: "What distinguishes it from adjacent literacy constructs is the triadic setting rather than the parts or their integration."

The whole claim rests on the assumption that DeVito's cases are dyadic — an actor and a system. They are not. DeVito (2021) studies creators doing self-presentation on TikTok, Instagram and BeReal; the system in those cases is *matching the creator to viewers* and *scoring the creator on how that match goes* (completion, watch time, follow-through). That is the chapter's own definition, word for word, of the triadic setting. The same is true of Eslami et al. (2016), where the folk theories concern which friends' posts reach whom, and of Bucher et al. (2021), where anticipatory compliance is compliance about how one will be delivered to someone. The chapter has narrowed its novelty to a property its principal rival already has.

Why it fails *for me*: I have watched three papers in the last two years claim a new literacy construct on a distinction the incumbent already covers, and this is the version that will get caught first, because DeVito is the person the chapter concedes most to and therefore the person a reader will check most carefully. Prior panel finding #9 ("algorithmacy's novelty narrowed to the triadic index; DeVito's coverage conceded") is not a regression in the sense of a failed fix — the concession is real and well made — but the fix relocated the novelty onto ground DeVito also occupies. That is worth reporting as a fix that does not hold.

There *is* a real difference, and the chapter's own §5 already runs on it. In DeVito's cases the counterpart is a diffuse audience that transacts nothing and whose conduct the system does not separately score. In the chapter's cases — eBay seller, driver, ISV, hotel — the counterpart is a named party whose behavior the same system reads and rates, and the two are bound into a determination (a price, a match, a dispute record) neither authored. That is exactly why the eBay seller's move is available: she manages the buyer in order to manage how the system reads her. A creator cannot manage an audience that way. The index should be *co-scored transacting counterpart*, not *triad*. Paste-ready text in Part 3.

Damage if unfixed: the chapter's stated contribution to my field evaporates on the first close reading, and it takes the Key Terms entry with it.

### F2. §4 — Street's caution is assigned to the half he was not arguing about. **[FIX NOW]**

The chapter's toll payment reads: "its strong version, that writing restructures the mind, did not survive the ethnographers … The claim advanced here concerns coordination, whatever the truth about individual cognition … Street's caution applies to the competence rather than to the regime, since there is no single algorithmacy any more than there is a single literacy."

This gets Street backwards. Street (1984) is not primarily a cognitive claim and his target is not the psychology of individual readers. His target is the **autonomous model** — the position that literacy has consequences of its own, independent of the social arrangements that deploy it — and his alternative is the ideological model, on which literacy practices are always constituted inside particular institutions and power relations. Scribner and Cole (1981) carry the cognitive half of the refutation. Street carries the *structural* half.

So when the chapter says the change-in-kind claim has been moved from cognition to "the regime of coordination," it has not escaped Street; it has walked into him. "Each regime makes possible forms of authority the prior regime cannot instantiate, and in that infrastructural sense the transitions are changes in kind" is the autonomous model restated one level up: a medium, considered apart from the arrangements deploying it, carries a form of authority with it. Street's whole book says you cannot make that claim about literacy. The chapter cites him and then makes it about coordination.

The relocation is *legitimate in principle*. A claim about what an institutional arrangement makes possible is a different claim from one about what a technology does to a mind, and it is a claim I would defend in some forms. But it has to be made as a claim about **arrangements**, not about media, and the chapter's own §3 already shows how: the novelty is a conjunction of opacity, adaptivity and interest, which are properties of an *arrangement* — a firm's business model, a market structure, a legal regime — not of algorithms as such. Written text under a licensing monopoly is opaque and interested too. Say that, and Street becomes an ally instead of an unpaid creditor.

### F3. §2.1 — the oral order is a stylized backdrop, and one of its three load-bearing claims is false. **[FIX NOW]**

"Beyond its range it stopped, because an oral order could not fix its law in a text, check today's ruling against last year's, or transmit a rule past the reach of living memory."

Three things asserted, no citation attached to the sentence. The first is true by definition. The third is **false as a general claim**, and falsifiable by examples that any reader in my field has to hand: Vedic recitation transmitted a fixed text across more than a millennium using redundant recitation schemes (*padapāṭha*, *kramapāṭha*, *jaṭāpāṭha*) that function as error-correcting codes; Homeric epic, West African griot lineages, and Icelandic genealogy all transmit past living memory as their ordinary business. The second claim is *half* false: the Icelandic *lögsögumaður* recited a third of the law to the Alþingi every year, which is precisely an institution for checking this year's ruling against the received rule without writing. Oral orders built formidable machinery against forgetting. What they could not do cheaply was give a *third party who was not present* the ability to lay one text beside another — which is a claim about cost and distribution, not about capacity, and which is both true and more interesting than what the chapter wrote.

Preceding it, "law was what the elders could recite," "an oath bound because the community witnessed it," and the list of who might address the assembly (restricted "by age, sex, birth, and office") are asserted with no source at all; the only citation in the paragraph, `(Goody, 1977; Ong, 1982)`, is attached to a different sentence and neither work is a source on who could swear an enforceable oath in any actual polity. Goody 1977 is *The Domestication of the Savage Mind*, whose subject is the intellectual technology of **writing** — lists, tables, formulae; orality is its foil, not its object.

Why it fails for me: this paragraph exists to make the arc symmetrical, and it reads that way. Its function in the chapter is to supply a first term so that literacy can be a second and algorithmacy a third. A reader who works on orality will stop at the "living memory" clause and will not extend credit to §4.

The fix costs one short passage and it makes the chapter *better*, because the corrected claim — writing made comparison cheap and available to the absent third party — is exactly the property §2.2 goes on to need ("persistence, portability, comparability"). Paste-ready text in Part 3.

### F4. §2.1 — *oracy* is Wilkinson's coinage and the chapter does not say so. **[FIX NOW]**

Verified: Andrew Wilkinson coined *oracy* in 1965, in *Educational Review* 17(4) (and in *English in Education* the same year), explicitly by analogy with *literacy* and *numeracy*, as an argument that English education had neglected speaking and listening. It is a twentieth-century curriculum term for a school competence. It is not a classical term, it is not a term from the orality literature, and Goody and Ong — whom the chapter cites for the substance — do not use it.

Two consequences, and the second is the serious one. First, an uncited coinage in a chapter that is otherwise scrupulous about attribution; that is a hole a reviewer will put a finger in. Second, and worse for the argument: the chapter's three-era symmetry is partly *manufactured by the word*. "Oracy, literacy, algorithmacy" sounds like three members of one series with equal standing, and the reason it sounds that way is that Wilkinson built the first term on the pattern of the second in 1965 for reasons having nothing to do with sovereignty. The reader is being persuaded by a suffix. Cite Wilkinson, say plainly that the term is being extended well beyond its educational origin, and the persuasion becomes an argument instead of a rhyme.

### F5. §4 — Dogruel et al. (2022) is misdescribed, and §4 contradicts §9. **[FIX NOW]**

§4: "Dogruel et al. (2022) built a validated scale for algorithm literacy as awareness, understanding, and skill."

Verified against the article (Dogruel, Masur & Joeckel, *Communication Methods and Measures*, 16(2), 115–133, 2022): the scale has **two** interrelated dimensions — (1) awareness of algorithm use and (2) knowledge about algorithms — validated via IRT across two studies (N = 331; N = 1,041), yielding 11 items per subscale. There is no third "skill" dimension. §9 of this chapter describes it **correctly** ("algorithm-literacy scales already measure awareness that algorithms operate and knowledge of how they work in principle"), which makes §4 an internal inconsistency as well as an error.

Small in words, large in consequence: §9 rests its discriminant-validity discussion on what Dogruel's instrument does and does not capture. Getting the instrument's dimensionality wrong in §4 tells the one class of reader who would actually build the §9 scale that the author has not read the instrument. Fix: "built and validated a two-dimensional scale separating awareness that algorithms operate from knowledge of how they work."

### F6. Reference list — Gagrčin is misspelled throughout. **[FIX NOW]**

The chapter writes **Gagričin** in §4, in §9's vicinity, and in the reference list. The correct spelling is **Gagrčin** — Emilija Gagrčin, Teresa K. Naab, Maria F. Grub. Verified against the publisher record. The reference also omits the issue number: it should read *New Media & Society, 28*(1), 423–447. In a double-anonymized review this is the kind of thing that survives to the printed volume, and misspelling a living scholar's name in the sentence where you adopt her research agenda is a bad look for a chapter whose argument depends on her call for domain-specific constructs.

### F7. §2.3 and §10 — Ong's own *secondary orality* contradicts the succession model. **[FIX IF TIME, high value]**

"Two changes in kind mark the sequence, and only the first is complete. Speech to writing is finished."

Speech to writing is not finished, and the source the chapter cites for orality says so at length. Ong (1982) devotes a chapter to **secondary orality** — the orality of radio, telephone and television, which depends on writing and print for its existence and which restores participatory, present-tense, formulaic communication at industrial scale. In 2026, with voice assistants, podcasts, live commerce and synthetic speech, secondary orality is not a footnote.

This is offered as a gift rather than a complaint. The chapter's succession model — one regime finishing, the next beginning — is its weakest structural commitment and the one that makes it look like Great Divide theory. A **layering** model is stronger, it is what the media-theory tradition actually holds, and it costs one clause: regimes do not replace each other, they accumulate, and the political problem is that governance instruments built for one layer are applied to another. That reframing also rescues §2.3's genuinely good line about the compliance officer meeting a new situation with the competence he already has.

### F8. §2.2 — "every qualification of that tradition," with no qualifier named. **[FIX IF TIME]**

"Media theory has long paired forms of authority with the media that carry them, from Innis (1951) … to Eisenstein (1979) … One modest claim survives every qualification of that tradition."

The qualifications have names, and citing Innis and Eisenstein in 2026 without any of them is not a defensible position in my field. Adrian Johns, *The Nature of the Book* (1998), is a book-length demonstration that Eisenstein's fixity was not a property of print but a social achievement constructed over two centuries of contested practice; the Eisenstein–Johns exchange is standard graduate reading. Ruth Finnegan, *Literacy and Orality* (1988), is the standard riposte to Ong's divide and to the notion of "the oral society" as a type. Naming one qualifier each and then extracting the surviving claim costs half a sentence and converts a rhetorical shield into a scholarly move. As written, "survives every qualification" is a claim the chapter has not earned because it has not stated a single qualification.

### F9. §9 — the chapter reinvents Brandt's *sponsors of literacy* without citing her. **[FIX IF TIME, highest value per word]**

§9's closing paragraph is the best thing in the back half of the chapter: mass literacy spread by state compulsion for the sake of order (Paglayan, 2024); the current market for algorithmic competence coaches compliance with platform objectives (Bishop, 2020; MacDonald, 2023); whether algorithmacy becomes general depends on which path carries the diffusion.

That argument has a name and a twenty-eight-year-old citation in my field. Deborah Brandt, "Sponsors of Literacy," *College Composition and Communication* 49(2) (1998), 165–185, and *Literacy in American Lives* (Cambridge UP, 2001): sponsors are the agents who underwrite, teach and regulate a literacy, and they do it because they extract advantage from the competence they supply, so the *shape* a literacy takes is set by who paid for it. That is precisely the chapter's claim about the platform-expertise industry, arrived at independently and stated less sharply. One citation converts an observation into a claim standing on a literature, and it is the single strongest bid the chapter can make for readers in literacy studies.

### F10. §2.2 / §10 vs. §9 — Paglayan undercuts the arc, and the chapter has not noticed. **[FIX IF TIME]**

§2.2: "the reach of democratic sovereignty tracked the spread of the competence rather than the invention of the medium."
§10: "democratic sovereignty followed the diffusion of the competence that made it possible."
§9: states compelled literacy through schooling, following episodes of internal unrest, not extensions of the franchise; "compulsion was the mechanism and order the motive."

If Paglayan is right, competence did not lead and sovereignty did not follow; a state extended schooling to *produce order*, and whatever democratic consequence came later did not run through the causal channel §2.2 and §10 assert. The chapter deploys Paglayan as a warning about algorithmacy's future while leaving the historical narrative in §2.2 and §10 untouched. Either the arc absorbs her — one clause in §2.2: the diffusion was compelled by states pursuing order, and the democratic use of the competence was a by-product actors seized rather than a purpose the sponsors held — or the chapter is citing evidence against its own thesis without saying so. The absorbed version is better, and it prepares §9.

### F11. §1 — Habermas is used for the rise and not the refeudalization. **[FIX IF TIME]**

"Habermas (1962/1989) traced the pairing for the literate state: private persons reasoning together in print constituted a sphere in which public power could be called to answer."

Accurate, and a partial reading. The second half of *Strukturwandel* is the story of the sphere's collapse into staged publicity managed by organized interests — publicity as display rather than as reason. That half is a *closer* analogue to what this chapter says platforms do than the half it uses. Habermas's account of a public sphere hollowed out by interested intermediaries who control the terms of appearance, while the forms of participation persist, is remarkably close to the chapter's own Selznickian formal co-optation in §3. Two sentences would let the chapter say: the pairing of competence and accountability held while the medium's terms were set by readers, and it broke when they were set by interested parties who owned the channel — which is the thesis. Also worth one clause: Habermas's public was explicitly bourgeois and class-bounded, which is the chapter's own "the competence was never evenly held" theme, available for free.

### F12. §4 — the deficit-model imagery, in a chapter that cites Street. **[FIX IF TIME]**

"Her position beside a competitor who lacks the competence is the position of a literate merchant beside an illiterate one in an economy run on written contracts." And: "A citizen with excellent legal and technical literacy who cannot model the system coordinating her stands where a fluent speaker once stood before a written contract in a script she could not read. She can perceive that something binds her, and she cannot work within it."

Both images are effective and both are the autonomous model in miniature: the "illiterate" as a person lacking a thing, rather than as a category produced by an institution that chose to conduct business in writing. Street's central move is to relocate the deficit from the person to the arrangement, and the chapter's *own §4 political-economy paragraph* does exactly that — opacity preserves the rent, "gaming" accusations fall hardest on the unestablished, the gradient is held in place. The images and the paragraph are in tension. The cheapest repair is a clause that names the arrangement in the image itself: not "an illiterate merchant" but a merchant in an economy that has decided to bind people in a script it does not teach them. Nkemelu (reviewer 03) will hit this from labour; I hit it from literacy studies, and the two objections meet.

### F13. The regime claim is unfalsifiable as stated, and §3 has already conceded the argument. **[TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]**

The large one, for the *Organization Theory* submission rather than for 30 August.

"Each regime makes possible forms of authority the prior regime cannot instantiate, and in that infrastructural sense the transitions are changes in kind."

Nothing in the chapter states what would count against this. No form of authority is identified that is available in one regime and unavailable in a later one; no purportedly new form is tested and found old. Meanwhile §3 has already conceded that the coordination logic is old ("platforms largely run the existing logics in algorithmic form") and the topology is old ("the sociological analysis of the triad is itself a century old"), leaving the novelty resting on a conjunction of three properties — opacity, adaptivity, interest — each of which comes in **degrees** and each of which has clear pre-digital instances. A guild that priced by secret rule was opaque and interested. A royal favourite's discretion was adaptive and unannounced. A commission agent with private information about both sides is the *tertius gaudens* with all three.

That is a difference of degree along three dimensions, argued honestly in §3, and it is presented in §2, §4, §10 and Key Terms as a third regime and a change in kind. The phrase "in that infrastructural sense" is carrying the entire load and it is not defined anywhere.

The reason this matters beyond pedantry: the chapter's real contribution — the bypass test, the necessary/contingent/partial/reducible taxonomy, the exit/voice mapping, §7's instrument sorting — **does not need the arc at all**. Delete oracy and the regime claim, open the chapter on the ISV in §1 and the disclosure-versus-standing gap, and §§3–8 stand undamaged. The arc is the part that will be attacked and the part that carries no load. My honest advice, which the calendar makes impossible for this venue and which I therefore file as carry-forward: for the journal version, demote the three-era arc to a framing paragraph and let the diagnostic be the paper.

### F14. The literacy-studies bibliography stops in 1984. **[TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]**

Street 1984, Scribner & Cole 1981, Ong 1982, Goody 1977, Eisenstein 1979, Innis 1951. The newest literacy-studies source in a 2026 chapter is forty-two years old, while the algorithmic-literacy side runs 2016–2026. The New Literacy Studies did not stop in 1984 — Gee (1996), Barton & Hamilton (1998), Brandt (1998, 2001) are the obvious continuations, and Brandt in particular is load-bearing (F9). ## 4. Part 2 — style and slop audit

The register rules are being followed, and I want that on the record before I complain. No antithesis machine, no "two qualifications keep the case honest," no metronomic openers, low agentless-passive density, and the eBay vignette in §4 is genuinely good writing — the best prose in the chapter and the passage the merge pass most nearly left alone, which is not a coincidence.

Four items.

**S1. The thesis formula is stated near-verbatim three times.** "A competence equal to the medium" appears in the abstract, §1 and §10; "the standing to hold power to account through the medium in which power is exercised" appears in §1 and again in §10. Abstract-to-conclusion echo is conventional. §1-to-§10 verbatim is the refrain the house style bans, and it makes the conclusion sound as though the chapter learned nothing between the two.

*Rewrite (§10 opening):* "Sovereignty has always been the standing to hold power to account through the medium in which power is exercised, and the standing has always required a competence equal to the medium." → "Each of these orders paired an authority with a competence, and in each the pairing decided who could answer back. Oracy answered the oral order, literacy answered the literate state, and the digital extension of literacy answers for data, infrastructure, and code."

**S2. "One modest claim survives every qualification of that tradition" (§2.2).** Failure type: unnamed opposition — the sentence invokes qualifications in order to be seen surviving them, without letting the reader see one. *Rewrite:* "Johns (1998) has since shown that print's fixity was constructed rather than given, and Finnegan (1988) that no clean line separates oral from literate societies. One claim survives both qualifications: a sovereignty and its competence form together, and no sovereignty can be contested by those who lack the competence its medium requires."

**S3. "None of that is reading, because the seller guidelines do not contain what she needs to know, and none of it is modeling a system on its own" (§4).** Verbatim refrain inside a single sentence; the second "none of it" is doing rhythm, not work. *Rewrite:* "None of that is reading — the seller guidelines do not contain what she needs to know — and it is not modeling a system in the abstract either."

**S4. "Speech to writing is finished" (§2.3).** Not slop; a factual overreach that reads as slop because it is the kind of clean claim a paragraph wants. See F7.

The remaining style findings are all artifacts of the v2 pass and are collected in the final section, where they belong.

## 5. Part 3 — paste-ready revisions

In the chapter's register: impersonal, active, named agents, no first person.

**R1 — §2.1, replacing the oracy sentence and the "beyond its range" sentence.**

> Wilkinson (1965) coined *oracy* by analogy with literacy and numeracy, for a curriculum of speaking and listening; extended here, the term names the competence that let a member of an oral order participate in its authority — give testimony, hold others to their word, carry the customary rules that constituted the group (Goody, 1977; Ong, 1982). Everyone in such an order could speak, and not everyone could speak in the forms that bound. Who might address the assembly, swear an oath the community would enforce, or recite the law as law was restricted by age, sex, birth, and office; the competence was general and the authorized exercise of it was not, which is the difference that decided who could hold power to account.
>
> Within its range that sovereignty was real, and the range was set by what recitation could reach. Oral orders built formidable machinery against forgetting: Vedic schools carried a fixed text across more than a millennium through redundant recitation schemes, and the Icelandic lawspeaker recited a third of the law to the assembly each year. What the machinery could not do was cheap. It was held by specialists, it bound only those present when it was performed, and it could not lay one year's ruling beside another's for a party who had attended neither. Finnegan (1988) warns against reading that limit as a divide between two kinds of society, and the warning holds: what changes across the transition is the cost and the distribution of comparison, not the mental equipment of the people doing the comparing.

*(Add to references: Finnegan, R. (1988). Literacy and orality: Studies in the technology of communication. Basil Blackwell. — Wilkinson, A. (1965). The concept of oracy. Educational Review, 17(4), 11–15. **VERIFY page range.**)*

**R2 — §4, replacing "What algorithmacy adds is the setting …" through "… that index and nothing larger."** Fixes F1.

> What algorithmacy adds is the setting, and the setting has to be specified more narrowly than a triad alone allows. DeVito's creators already work a system that matches them to viewers and scores them on how the match goes, so triadic structure by itself does not separate the two constructs. The difference is what stands on the other side. A creator faces an audience that is diffuse, that transacts nothing, and whose conduct the system does not separately score; a seller, a driver, or a vendor faces a named counterpart whose behavior the same system reads and rates, and the two are bound into a determination — a price, a match, a dispute record — that neither authored and both carry. That is what makes the seller's move available at all: she manages the buyer in order to manage how the system reads her, which is not a move a creator can make on an audience. Reviewing algorithm-literacy research, Gagrčin et al. (2026) call for domain-specific constructs rather than a single general capacity. Algorithmacy is one, the competence indexed to coordination with a co-scored counterpart, and the novelty claimed against these accounts is that index and nothing larger.

*(Key Terms, **Algorithmacy**, final sentence: "What distinguishes it from adjacent literacy constructs is not triadic structure as such, which those constructs also describe, but a counterpart the same system separately reads and rates.")*

**R3 — §4, replacing the Street sentence.** Fixes F2.

> Street's objection reaches further than the competence. His target was the autonomous model, on which a medium carries consequences of its own independent of the arrangements that deploy it, and a claim about coordination regimes restates that model at the level of the arrangement unless it is stated with care. Two concessions follow. There is no single algorithmacy any more than there is a single literacy, and what an actor needs to know differs across a dispatch system, a ranking system, and a credit-scoring system. And the claim advanced here is not that algorithmic mediation has effects of its own: it is that a particular arrangement — an opaque, adaptive, interested third party holding the only channel between two parties — supports forms of authority that a persistent and comparable document does not, and that European regulation was built for the second and now meets the first. Whether that arrangement has spread far enough to warrant the word *regime* is an empirical question this chapter leaves open.

**R4 — §4, the Dogruel sentence.** Fixes F5.

> Dogruel et al. (2022) built and validated a two-dimensional scale for algorithm literacy, separating awareness that algorithms operate from knowledge of how they work, and critical-literacy scholarship adds the reflexive and tactical capacity to identify how one's data are used and to act on it (Pangrazio & Selwyn, 2019; Sander, 2020).

**R5 — §9, inserted before "Studies of the platform-expertise industry find it coaching producers …".** Fixes F9.

> Literacy studies has a name for the pattern. Brandt (1998) calls the agents who underwrite, teach, and regulate a literacy its sponsors, and finds that sponsors extract advantage from the competence they supply, so the shape a literacy takes is set by who paid for it. Algorithmacy's present sponsors are the platforms and the expertise market that serves them, and what they underwrite is compliance.

*(Add: Brandt, D. (1998). Sponsors of literacy. College Composition and Communication, 49(2), 165–185.)*

**R6 — §2.3, replacing "Two changes in kind mark the sequence, and only the first is complete. Speech to writing is finished, and a scribe works a different medium from a better speaker".** Fixes F7.

> Regimes of coordination layer rather than replace. Writing did not end speech, and Ong (1982) traced a secondary orality carried by broadcast and telephony that depends on print for its existence — a reminder that a later regime can restore the conditions of an earlier one. What changes is which regime the governing instruments are built for. A scribe works a different medium from a better speaker, and section 4 argues that algorithmic mediation stands in the same relation to the document: not its successor, but a layer the instruments built for the document do not reach.

**R7 — §5.3 and §6, v2 only: restore the defined term.** Four one-word reversions, detailed in the final section.

## On the v2 rewrite

I was asked whether merging roughly fifty adjacent sentence pairs against four published model essays reads as a person writing or as a different optimization. It reads as a different optimization, and I can show it rather than assert it.

**The pass did not do what it was for.** The stated aim was to raise sentence-length variation. Measured over the body text of both drafts (headings and tables excluded), mean sentence length rose from 22.1 to 25.4 words, and standard deviation rose from 12.5 to 13.7 — but the *coefficient of variation*, which is what "variation" means when the mean is moving, **fell**, from 0.565 to 0.540. The absolute SD went up only because everything got longer. Relative to its own scale, v2 is *less* varied than the draft it was meant to fix.

**It went one direction only.** Sentences of eight words or fewer: 62 → 41, a third of them gone. Twelve words or fewer: 136 → 87. At the other end, sentences of forty words or more: 55 → 75. Fifty-five words or more: 6 → **15**. Across roughly seventy changed sentences I found **not one split** — no pair became three, no long sentence was broken, and no merged pair produced a sentence shorter than the longer of its two parents. A person revising for rhythm merges some and splits others, and occasionally leaves a four-word sentence standing precisely because the floor needs to drop out from under the paragraph. This pass had one move.

**It had one favourite joint.** Body-text semicolons went from 25 to 44. Tallying the joints introduced at the merge points: `, and` ×55, `;` ×23, `:` ×11, `, because / since / so / while / but` ×27, `—` ×6. The same three connectives applied to the Habermas paragraph, the eBay vignette, the instrument catalogue and the conclusion, with no sensitivity to what any of them was doing. That is the signature. Uniformity of *optimization* is what gives a text away, and this pass is more uniform than the text it edited.

**It damaged the manuscript in four specific places, and two of them are substantive.**

*Terminology, §5.3.* The chapter defines *mediator* in Key Terms and builds §5's entire taxonomy on it. v2 swaps the defined term for an undefined one in the two sentences that carry the exit/voice hinge:

- chapter.md: "…one available against contingent **mediators** too and the only one left where **the mediator** is necessary."
  v2: "…one available against contingent **third parties** too and the only one left where **the third party** is necessary."
- chapter.md: "…because **the mediator's** position never rested on the tie's absence. To demand independence from **a necessary mediator** is to demand the dissolution…"
  v2: "…because **its** position never rested on the tie's absence, and to demand independence from **such a third party** is to demand the dissolution…"

That is elegant variation — avoid repeating a word — applied to a technical term in a chapter with a glossary. It is the one thing a revision pass must never do, and it happened in the definitional sentences of the chapter's central distinction. The panel brief states that v2 differs from chapter.md "in prose only." It does not.

*Referent damage.* Three merges replaced an explicit noun with a pronoun to keep the joined sentence from bloating, and three times the pronoun lost its anchor. §6: "worker ownership can change who sets a necessary mediator's rules and cannot make **it** leaveable" — *it* now reads most naturally as *the rules*. §6: "guarantees against a mediator's conduct; coordinative sovereignty is a share in **its** governance" — *its* is now ambiguous between the mediator and the conduct. §5.3: "because **its** position never rested on the tie's absence" — recoverable, but only just.

*Cross-references buried.* §1's "and section 3.2 takes them up" was a standalone clause in chapter.md; in v2 it is "as section 3.2 takes up," parenthetical inside a fifty-five-word sentence, where a reader looking for a signpost will not find one.

*Cadence flattened where it was earned.* chapter.md §6: "The platform is not an unowned pasture. **It is enclosed**, owned and tuned by the mediator" — a two-beat correction, and the beat is the point. v2: "The platform is not an unowned pasture but enclosed, owned and tuned by its proprietor" — an apposition the reader glides past. And §10: "Oracy held the oral order to account. / Literacy held the literate state to account, and…" became "Oracy held the oral order to account, literacy held the literate state to account, and the digital extension of literacy governs data, infrastructure, and code" — a comma splice dressed as a tricolon, closing the chapter on a list where it used to close on a cadence.

**What survived, and what that shows.** The eBay vignette in §4 is the best writing in the chapter and it came through nearly untouched: "They are fine." "She has read nothing. There was nothing to read." Those short sentences are exactly the class the pass otherwise deleted, and they survived because the pass found no adjacent pair to merge there. The vignette is proof that the author can write the rhythm the pass was trying to manufacture, and proof that the manufacture was unnecessary.

**Which draft should ship.**

**Ship `chapter.md`.** If the author has time for only one decision, that is the decision. v2's failure mode — long, semicolon-jointed, uniformly twenty-five-word sentences with the short ones sanded off — is a *more* recognizable machine signature to a 2026 reader than the staccato it was hired to cure. Staccato reads as a stylistic tic; monotone periodic sentences read as output.

If the author has an hour rather than a decision: start from `chapter.md` and hand-adopt the ten or so v2 merges that fixed genuine three- and four-sentence staccato runs. The best of them are real improvements — §2.3's AI Act / GDPR / NIS2 triple, §3.3's application-store example, §5.2's Burt/Williamson/essential-facilities sequence, and §7's contestability sentence all read better joined. What they have in common is that the original had *three or more* consecutive short declaratives, which is a real fault. The merges that ate a *deliberate* two-sentence beat are losses, and there are more of those than there are gains.

Whichever file ships, R7 applies to it: restore *mediator* in the two §5.3 sentences and the explicit nouns in the two §6 pronouns, and pull §1's cross-reference back out of its host sentence. Those four repairs take five minutes and they are not stylistic.

One last thing, said plainly because the author will get this advice from nowhere else. The instinct behind the v2 pass — measure the prose against published models, find the gap, close it — is the same instinct the chapter attributes to the literate response to platform coordination in §2.3: meet a new situation with the competence you already have, apply it harder, and address the layer it can grasp. Sentence length was the legible object. Rhythm was the thing that mattered, and it was not on the instrument.
