## 1. Who I am

I teach hospitality and tourism management and I have spent fifteen years on hotel distribution
channels — commission structures, the economics of the direct-booking push, and what parity
enforcement actually did to the hotels it was supposed to help. I have published on OTA channel mix
and I have refereed for *IJCHM*, *Tourism Management*, and *JTR*, which means I have read a great many
papers that reach for the HOTREC series as a decorative statistic and get it wrong. Roland Schegg's
biennial study is a fine instrument for the question it was built to answer and a trap for anyone who
mistakes it for a national accounts table. I came to this chapter because a colleague forwarded §5.5
with the note "someone has finally written down the thing we say at conferences." She is right that
the split verdict — parity clause contingent, demand aggregation necessary — is the correct reading
of my sector, and I believe it independently of this chapter. That is exactly why I am hard on the
paragraph that is supposed to prove it. The chapter has picked the right case, made the right call,
and then evidenced it with the one series that cannot carry the weight, in a form my field will
recognize as an error within a page. A good argument shot down by a bad statistic is worse than no
argument, because the statistic is what gets quoted back.

The manuscript's own framing raises the stakes. §8 tells managers that "the hotels of section 5.5 show
what that waste looks like at sector scale," and §10 ends the chapter on them. This is not an
illustration. It is the chapter's only sustained encounter with data, and it is loaded twice.

### F1 — [FIX NOW] The two headline percentages are not each other's complement, and the sentence is built as though they were

**What the chapter claims.** "Between 2013 and 2023 the agencies' share of European hotel overnight
stays rose from 19.7% to 29.6% while direct bookings fell from 57.6% to 50.9% (Schegg, 2024).
Dependence did not fall as the gate came open. It deepened."

**What fails.** The word doing the work is *while*. It builds a two-channel world in which the agency
took what the hotel lost. Schegg's data describe at least five channel families, and the arithmetic
does not close: the agencies gained 9.9 points and direct lost 6.7. Three points arrived from
somewhere the sentence does not mention.

Where they came from is the whole story. In Schegg's unweighted sample — the only place he publishes
the components — the decade runs like this:

- Telephone 21.1 → 16.4 (−4.7)
- Mail/fax 3.1 → 1.3 (−1.8)
- Walk-in 6.0 → 3.5 (−2.5)
- Contact form on own website 6.1 → 5.9 (−0.2)
- Email 15.8 ← 16.1 (−0.3)
- **Real-time booking on the hotel's own website 6.9 → 12.1 (+5.2)**
- Tour operator / travel agency 9.6 → 7.1, wholesaler 2.8 → 1.7, chain CRS 1.4 → 1.0, events 1.9 → 1.8 (**−4.1 as a bloc**)
- OTA 19.3 → 29.1 (+9.8)

The weighted series confirms the direction on the one component Schegg weights: the hotel's own
booking engine rose from **7.4% to 12.3%** (Exec. Summary II).

So the decade's losers were the *analog* direct channels — telephone, fax, the front desk — and the
*traditional offline intermediaries* — tour operators, travel agencies, bed wholesalers. The hotel's
own online booking channel was the fastest-growing direct channel in Europe, up roughly 70%. The
chapter's own cited source, the ECN, found the same decomposition over the enforcement window and
said so in a sentence the chapter could have quoted: between 2013 and H1 2016 the offline share of
hotel room sales fell nine points "mainly to the benefit of the OTAs (eight percentage point
increase), rather than hotels' direct online channel (increase of one percentage point)" (ECN, 2017,
para. 37). Its Table 1, for independent hotels specifically: offline 53 → 44, OTA 35 → 42, direct
online 12 → 13.

**Why it fails for me.** This is the first thing anyone in my field checks, because the Schegg
"direct" bucket is a well-known composite of six sub-channels with opposite trends, and reading its
decline as a failure of direct-booking strategy is the standard error. It is the error the trade press
makes every two years when the study lands. A reviewer at *IJCHM* or *Tourism Management* would stop
at the word *while* and not resume.

**The damage.** Severe, and asymmetric — the finding survives, the sentence does not. Nothing here
touches the split verdict. Demand aggregation is still necessary; the parity clause was still
contingent; dependence on the aggregating function still did not fall when the gate opened. What
collapses is the specific evidentiary move, and with it §8's "the hotels of section 5.5 show what that
waste looks like at sector scale" and §10's "came out of it more dependent than they went in." All
three sentences are cashing the same bad cheque.

Rewrite in Part 3.

### F2 — [FIX NOW] The series is not the same instrument across the two endpoints, and one clause would cover it

**What the chapter claims.** By implication: a decade-long comparable series on European hotels.

**What fails.** Schegg's design is a self-selected online survey distributed by national hotel
associations to their members, in 27 languages, with response rates that "vary strongly by country"
(Schegg, 2024, methodology). The European figure is a mean of hotel-level shares, weighted to Eurostat
2022 country overnights — a weighting applied to every wave including 2013. The composition underneath
that weighting moves:

- **"Rest Europe" carries 25.6%** of the weight in the 2023 without-chains column — a quarter of the
  European estimate is countries the survey barely reaches.
- **Spain** carries 14.6% of the weight on **n = 71** hotels in 2023.
- **France** carries 14.5% on n = 146 in 2023 and **n = 49 in 2013** — below Schegg's own reliability
  floor. France's 2019 figure had to be collected separately and published as its own report; the
  study still prints two different 2019 columns, with and without France (n = 3,044 vs n = 2,166).
- **Italy** carries 13.3%, and its **2013 direct-booking value is not reported at all**; the 2013
  Italian sample (n = 101) carries Schegg's asterisk for "reliability issue."
- **Denmark has n = 0 in 2019. Croatia has n = 0 in 2015. Sweden's 2013 n = 17**, asterisked.
- The 2023 sample skews small — median 34 rooms, 44% under 30 — and Schegg reports that small hotels
  are markedly more OTA-dependent. An unweighted-within-country mean therefore sits above the
  overnight-weighted sector share.

**Why it fails for me.** None of this makes Schegg a bad study. It makes it a survey of hotelier-
reported channel mix, which is what it says it is on the cover. The chapter promotes it to "the
agencies' share of European hotel overnight stays," a phrase that means a sector aggregate. It is not
one, and the gap runs in the direction that flatters the chapter's claim.

**The damage.** Moderate on its own, fatal in combination with F1 — a reader who catches the
composition problem will assume the complement problem was also careless, and will stop trusting the
section. The fix is one clause plus one sentence, and it costs the argument nothing.

### F3 — [FIX IF TIME] The clock is set to the wrong window, and the chapter's own window points the other way

**What the chapter claims.** "What the decade covers is the sequence from the 2015 abandonment of wide
parity to the national enforcement of 2021."

**What fails.** The chapter nominates 2015–2021 as its treatment window and then reports 2013–2023 as
its evidence. Run the weighted series over the window the chapter itself chose:

| | 2013 | 2015 | 2017 | 2019 | 2021 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct | 57.6 | 52.9 | 52.0 | 47.0 | 55.2 | 50.9 |
| Online intermediaries (OTA + GDS) | 22.2 | 26.4 | 29.0 | 31.9 | 30.6 | 31.6 |

**2015 → 2021: direct rises, 52.9 to 55.2.** 2015 → 2023: direct falls 2.0 points, not 6.7. And 4.7 of
the decade's 6.7-point decline — 70% of it — lands in the single 2013→2015 wave, before or exactly
alongside the wide-parity abandonment the chapter treats as the intervention. Since 2019 the online
intermediary share has been flat and slightly down (31.9 → 31.6). Germany, the only country with a
well-populated series in every wave (n ≈ 1,000 throughout), shows direct at 58.5, 58.9, 58.5 across
2019–2023 and OTA+GDS at 32.7, 34.3, 33.8. The deepening happened, and it happened mostly before the
window the chapter draws around it.

The chronology also compresses. France's statutory ban on narrow parity is **August 2015**, not
"the years that followed"; the Bundeskartellamt prohibited Booking.com's narrow clause in **December
2015**, and 2021 is when the BGH ended the appeal (KVR 54/20, 18 May 2021). Enforcement against narrow
clauses began within months of the wide-parity retreat, not six years later.

**Why it fails for me.** A distribution economist reads "the decade lets the two readings be compared
against an outcome" as a claim to a before-and-after design, then looks for the before and after. There
is no before: the series starts two years ahead of the treatment and the movement is concentrated
there.

**The damage.** Moderate to severe. The chapter is already hedging ("narrower than a natural
experiment"), which is honest, but the hedge concedes the wrong thing. The problem is not that this
falls short of a natural experiment. The problem is that the trend the chapter reports does not line up
with the intervention it is being read against.

### F4 — [FIX IF TIME] The billboard hedge is the wrong shape, and there is better evidence sitting unused

**What the chapter claims.** "C. K. Anderson (2009, 2011) named this the billboard effect and measured
the lift ... though later work has not consistently reproduced it (Beritelli & Schegg, 2016)."

**What fails.** "Reproduced" implies a replication attempt, and Beritelli and Schegg did not run one.
Anderson (2009) alternated four JHM properties on and off Expedia and measured the change in the
hotels' own-channel reservations; Anderson (2011) used IHG's reservation data and found around 75% of
brand-site bookers had touched an OTA first. Beritelli and Schegg (2016) surveyed European hotels —
predominantly small independents — and asked whether *total* online bookings track channel *type* or
channel *count*; they found count, and did not confirm a first-tier-channel premium. Different
dependent variable, different population, different design. They are not a replication pair, and
saying so costs the chapter nothing.

Two things the hedge omits, both of which would strengthen it:

- **Anderson and Han (2017)**, *The Billboard Effect: Still Alive and Well*, Cornell Hospitality Report
  17(11): the effect persists on clickstream data, attenuated; around 65% of direct bookers had visited
  an OTA at some point.
- **European Commission (2022)**, the market study on hotel accommodation distribution in the EU:
  **63% of independent hotels** in its six study countries consider that using an OTA increases the
  volume of bookings on their *direct* channels, and 80% consider it increases their total bookings.

That last figure is worth more to this chapter than Anderson is. The chapter needs the aggregating
function to be genuinely integrating for the hotel that could not otherwise be found. A Commission
survey in which nearly two-thirds of independents say the listing feeds their own channel is testimony
from the dependent party, which is exactly the standpoint §5.2 says the diagnostic is run from.

**The damage.** Moderate. The current hedge reads as a scholar being careful; it is actually a scholar
citing one side of a non-comparison and leaving the strongest available support on the table.

### F5 — [FIX NOW] The commission figure is real, mis-scoped, and a decade old in a 2026 volume

**What the chapter claims.** "its commission, which the European Competition Network's monitoring
exercise put at 10% to above 20% of each booking (European Competition Network, 2017)."

**What fails.** Three things.

1. **Mis-scoped.** ECN §4.4 says: "The basic commission rates of the three major OTAs range from ten
   percent to above twenty percent." *Basic*, and *three major OTAs*. The very next paragraph adds that
   OTAs "also charge hotels additional commission in return for ... optional additional services, such
   as better visibility on the OTA's website or membership of a preferred partner program." The
   chapter's "of each booking" reads as the total take. It is the floor.
2. **Stale.** The ECN range describes July 2015 – June 2016. A volume appearing in 2026 or 2027 will be
   printing a commission figure from data eleven years old, in the sector's most-quoted sentence.
3. **A current source exists.** European Commission (2022), *Market study on the distribution of hotel
   accommodation in the EU* — fieldwork in 2021, six member states, independent hotels as the main
   sample — reports basic commission in the 10–20% band for both OTAs and independent hotels, effective
   rates higher, chains' effective rates 0–10 percentage points above basic, and a "stable or slightly
   decreasing" trend across 2017–2021. The published figures are redacted to bands, which is a nuisance;
   the trend statement is not, and it is exactly what a 2026 volume should be citing.

**The damage.** Minor to the argument, real to the chapter's authority in my field. A hospitality
reader who sees ECN 2017 quoted as the current commission rate will assume the author last read the
sector's literature in 2018.

### F6 — [FIX NOW] "Spent a decade trying to leave" is a narrative the data do not carry

**What the chapter claims.** "an actor treating the agency as one leaveable thing would have read the
direction of travel backwards, and a hotel that spent the decade trying to leave mistook the necessary
function for the contingent one."

**What fails.** I cannot verify the antecedent, and everything I can measure runs against it.

Hotels did not spend the decade *trying to leave*. They spent it building capability to work the
channel mix, and where they built a direct channel it grew:

- Real-time booking on the hotel's own website: 6.9% → 12.1% unweighted, 7.4% → 12.3% weighted.
- Channel-manager adoption 39% → 62%; hotels managing rates and availability manually 56% → 27%
  (Schegg, 2024, Exec. Summary VI).
- Meta-search awareness roughly 50% → 80%, with 46% of hotels using the channel; Google Hotel Ads
  reaching 80% usage share among users (Exec. Summary VII).

Read that list again with §4 in hand. A channel manager is not an exit instrument. It is tooling for
rendering one's inventory into the terms several algorithmic intermediaries read, revised as those
systems change — the translational and temporal capacities of algorithmacy, adopted by 62% of European
hotels. The sector did not misread the diagnostic. It ran the correct strategy, invested in the
competence the chapter says the necessary case requires, and the chapter's own case study currently
scolds it for the opposite mistake.

The one genuine "trying to leave" episode of the decade was a **branded-chain** campaign — the 2016
book-direct push — not an independent-hotel one, and the chapter's protagonist is an independent hotel.

**Why it fails for me.** The sentence is the chapter's rhetorical peak and it is a slur on the sector,
delivered without a citation, in a section otherwise scrupulous about sourcing. It is also the sentence
§10 repeats.

**The damage.** Severe as reputation, moderate as argument. Cut it and §5.5 loses nothing but a flourish.

### F7 — [TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD] There is a quasi-experimental literature on precisely this question, and the chapter has not met it

**What the chapter claims.** That the parity decade "lets the two readings be compared against an
outcome," and that the erosion the single-intermediary reading predicts did not occur.

**What fails.** Three peer-reviewed papers exploit exactly the regulatory variation the chapter narrates,
with control groups and transaction data, and none appears in the reference list:

- **Hunold, Kesler, Laitenberger & Schlütter (2018)**, *International Journal of Industrial
  Organization* 61, 542–571. Meta-search price data on roughly 30,000 hotels. The German abolition at
  end-2015 encouraged hotels to use more OTAs *and* their own direct channels, with a sharper
  direct-channel price decrease in Germany than in countries that retained narrow parity.
- **Ennis, Ivaldi & Lagos (2023)**, *Journal of Law and Economics* 66(2), 309–331. Hotel-chain
  transaction data across the EU narrowing and the French/German bans: "direct sales by hotels to
  customers became relatively cheaper than OTA sales for midlevel and luxury hotels," with the narrower
  intervention more effective than complete elimination.
- **Klopack & Pierri (2026)**, *International Journal of Industrial Organization*. Transacted prices and
  occupancy from European hotels: narrowing cut prices ~1.5% and raised occupancy ~1 point; full bans
  cut prices 0–4% with no occupancy effect. Mechanism: both inter-OTA competition and OTA-versus-direct
  competition.

The parity remedies *worked* — on price, which is the margin they were designed for. They did not shift
channel dependence. That is not a defeat for the chapter; it is the split verdict stated in the
currency an economist recognizes. A contingent gate opened and the price effect followed; the necessary
function was untouched and the share did not move.

**Why it fails for me.** The chapter stakes its empirical claim on a comparison and then does not cite
the three papers that ran the comparison properly. In fifteen days the author cannot integrate this
literature. But two sentences acknowledging it convert the section from *uncontrolled trend offered as
a test* into *uncontrolled trend consistent with a controlled literature*, which is a different and much
safer object.

**The damage.** Fatal at a strategy or economics journal; survivable in an edited volume with the
two-sentence patch. This is the finding the January 2027 *Organization Theory* submission must carry
forward in full.

### F8 — [FIX IF TIME] The stronger comparison is available and unused

There *is* a control-group comparison in the public record, from a regulator, on the chapter's exact
question. European Commission (2022) surveyed six member states of which two — Austria and Belgium —
had prohibited parity clauses outright. Its finding:

> "It does not appear that the prohibition of OTA parity clauses in Austria and Belgium is associated
> with a greater or lesser use of OTAs by hotels. The level of use of OTAs is similar in both countries
> (81% and 85%) and the average level of use of OTAs across all six study countries is 80%."

And on share: Austrian hotels reported 35% of sales through OTAs, Belgian hotels 55%, against a
six-country mean of 44%. Where the gate came fully open, dependence on the aggregating function did not
move in either direction.

This is a proper cross-jurisdictional comparison, from the enforcement authority, on independent hotels
— the chapter's protagonist — and it does the work the Schegg time series is currently being asked to
do and cannot. It should be §5.5's lead evidence, with Schegg demoted to a corroborating trend.

**Damage of leaving it out:** the chapter argues its best empirical point with its second-best
instrument.

### The rest of the chapter, briefly

I have limited standing outside §5.5 and will not manufacture findings. Three notes where my sector
gives me purchase:

**§5.4 — the portfolio view is right and my sector confirms it.** The marketplace example (necessary

## 4. Part 2 — style and slop audit

The prose is good and I have few complaints. Four places where style bent a fact.

**(a) The load-bearing "while."**
> "the agencies' share of European hotel overnight stays rose from 19.7% to 29.6% **while** direct
> bookings fell from 57.6% to 50.9%"

*Failure type:* a coordinating conjunction doing the work of a causal or complementary claim the data
do not support. The elegance of the parallel construction is what smuggles it in.
*Rewrite:* see Part 3.

**(b) The two-sentence hammer.**
> "Dependence did not fall as the gate came open. It deepened."

*Failure type:* the short-sentence landing as a substitute for the qualification that should precede
it. It is well-made and it is exactly where a reader stops thinking. If the preceding sentence has to
carry a decomposition, this cannot be the shape that follows it.
*Rewrite:* "The share the agencies carried rose across the decade, and no part of that rise reversed
when the gate opened."

**(c) A hedge that concedes the wrong thing.**
> "so this is narrower than a natural experiment and the judgment sets up a test a later study can run"

*Failure type:* self-narrating rigor. Naming the shortfall in the abstract ("narrower than a natural
experiment") substitutes for naming the specific one — no control group, and a trend concentrated
before the treatment. The sentence sounds more careful than it is.
*Rewrite:* in Part 3.

**(d) The flourish that outran the evidence.**
> "a hotel that spent the decade trying to leave mistook the necessary function for the contingent one"

*Failure type:* an unsourced empirical claim in the position of a rhetorical cadence. The cadence is why
it was not checked.
*Rewrite:* delete; the following sentence in Part 3 replaces it.

One genuine praise, since the brief asks me not to invent problems: the paragraph beginning "The
counterfactual restores the capability of direct dealing, not a prior relationship" (§5.2) is the
cleanest exposition of a hard idea I have read in this literature, and the traveler pair that follows it
is the reason I would cite this chapter at all.

## 5. Part 3 — paste-ready revisions

### 5A. Replacement for §5.5, paragraphs 1 and 4–6 (the evidence run)

This keeps the chapter's register, its verdict, and its structure. Every figure is verified against the
sources named in §3 above. It runs about 120 words longer than what it replaces.

**Paragraph 1 — replace as follows:**

> An independent hotel reaches many of its guests through an online travel agency, and the agency does
> two very different things for its commission, which the European Commission's (2022) market study
> places in a basic band of 10% to 20% of the booking, with effective rates higher for hotels that buy
> visibility on top. It aggregates demand, putting the hotel in front of travelers who would never have
> found it. C. K. Anderson (2009, 2011) named this the billboard effect and measured the lift in a
> hotel's own direct reservations that follows from being listed on an agency at all; Anderson and Han
> (2017) find the effect attenuated but intact on later clickstream data, while Beritelli and Schegg
> (2016), surveying European hotels, attribute online booking volume to the number of channels a hotel
> uses rather than to the presence of any first-tier one. The two studies measure different quantities
> on different populations and do not settle each other. The dependent parties supply a third reading:
> 63% of the independent hotels the Commission surveyed consider that listing on an agency raises the
> volume of bookings on their own direct channels. And the agency has held rate parity clauses
> forbidding the hotel to price lower on its own site.

**Paragraphs 4–6 — replace the evidence run as follows:**

> The two readings predict different things about that decade. Treat the agency as one substitutable
> intermediary, and removing the clause that blocked price competition should erode its position:
> hotels can undercut it on their own sites, so bookings should move to the direct channel. Treat it as
> a portfolio, and only a contingent function was removed, so the necessary function should hold and
> dependence should not fall.
>
> The evidence comes in two grades, and the stronger grade compares jurisdictions. Austria and Belgium
> prohibited parity clauses outright while the other four countries in the European Commission's (2022)
> market study did not, and the Commission found no association between the prohibition and hotels' use
> of agencies: 81% of Austrian and 85% of Belgian hotels used one, against a six-country average of
> 80%. Nor did the share of sales the agencies carried follow the prohibition, running at 35% in
> Austria and 55% in Belgium around a six-country mean of 44%. Where the gate came fully open,
> dependence on the aggregating function did not move.
>
> The weaker grade is the European trend, and it needs decomposing before it can be read. Between 2013
> and 2023 the mean share of overnight stays European hotels report booking through an agency rose from
> 19.7% to 29.6% while the direct share fell from 57.6% to 50.9% (Schegg, 2024), a survey of
> hotelier-reported channel mix rather than a sector aggregate, and one whose country composition shifts
> across waves. Those two movements are not each other's complement. Over the same decade the hotel's
> own real-time booking engine rose from 7.4% to 12.3%; in the unweighted sample, where Schegg reports
> the components, telephone, fax and walk-in bookings fell by nine points between them while tour
> operators, travel agencies and bed wholesalers gave up four more. The European Competition Network
> found the same decomposition over the enforcement period itself, reporting that the offline share of
> hotel room sales fell nine points "mainly to the benefit of the OTAs (eight percentage point
> increase), rather than hotels' direct online channel (increase of one percentage point)" (European
> Competition Network, 2017, para. 37). The share the agencies carried rose across the decade, and no
> part of that rise reversed when the gate opened.
>
> What the decomposition rules out is the exit reading, not the hotels' effort. Independent hotels did
> build the direct channel: the booking engine on the hotel's own site was the fastest-growing channel
> in Europe over the decade, channel-manager use rose from 39% to 62% of properties, and the share of
> hotels managing rates and availability by hand fell from 56% to 27% (Schegg, 2024). What that channel
> took share from was the hotel's own telephone and front desk. Nor did the parity remedies fail on the
> margin they were designed for; Ennis et al. (2023) and Klopack and Pierri (2026) find that the
> interventions lowered direct-channel prices, and in the narrow case raised occupancy, without shifting
> the agencies' share. A remedy that moves price and leaves dependence where it was is what a split
> verdict predicts and what a single-intermediary reading does not.
>
> Three limits bound the claim. The 2024 judgment postdates the last year of these data. The rise in
> the agencies' share owes something to mobile search and to consolidation among the agencies, and most
> of the fall in the direct share falls in the first wave, 2013 to 2015, alongside rather than after the
> abandonment of wide parity. And the function-split predicts the absence of an erosion rather than the
> increase that occurred, so what the decade supplies is consistency across two grades of evidence
> rather than a test. It is enough to sort the two readings. An actor treating the agency as one
> leaveable thing would have read the direction of travel backwards, and the capability European hotels
> actually built over the decade — tooling that renders their inventory into the terms several
> algorithmic intermediaries read, and revises it as those systems change — is the capability the
> necessary verdict calls for, not the exit the contingent one would have licensed.

## 6. Verdict

**Minor revisions**, on the strict condition that F1, F2, F5 and F6 are fixed. Without them I would
recommend major revisions, because §5.5 as printed would not survive a hospitality referee and the
chapter has staked two other sections on it.

**The single most important fix.** Stop treating "direct bookings fell from 57.6% to 50.9%" as the
complement of the OTA rise. It is not, the components run in opposite directions, and the chapter's own
cited authority — the ECN — published the correct decomposition in 2017. Everything else in this review
is downstream of that one word, *while*.

**The chapter's biggest genuine strength.** The traveler pair in §5.2. Distribution economics has spent
twenty years arguing about whether OTAs are parasites or partners and has never had a clean way to say
that the answer depends on which booking you are asking about. "A traveler looking for a hotel chain she
has used before can reach it directly ... A traveler who would never have learned that an independent
hotel exists cannot" resolves it in two sentences, and the necessary/contingent split makes the
resolution portable to sectors I do not work in. Anderson's billboard effect turns out to be a
measurement of the necessary residue. That is a real contribution and it is worth more than the chapter
currently claims for it.

**The one thing only the author can supply.** A decision about what §5.5 is *for*. Right now it is doing
three jobs — illustrating the diagnostic, adjudicating between two theories, and supplying the chapter's
empirical warrant — and it can carry the first two. If the author wants the third, the section needs the
Commission's Austria/Belgium comparison as its lead evidence and an explicit two-sentence acknowledgment
of the Hunold/Ennis/Klopack literature. Nobody else can make that call, because it depends on how much
of the empirical load the anonymized companion is taking.

**Do not demote §5.5.** I was asked to say if it should go. It should not. The case is correctly chosen,
the verdict is correct, and the section is the only place in the chapter where the diagnostic touches
ground. Demoting it would leave a purely conceptual chapter in a volume that already has several. Fix
the evidence run and it becomes the chapter's strongest twelve hundred words.

## 7. Citation behavior

**Would I cite this chapter? Yes — for one thing, precisely.**

I would cite it in the argument I have been losing for a decade. My field frames hotel–OTA dependence as
a bargaining problem with a strategic answer: raise your direct share, diversify your channel mix,
negotiate your commission down. Twenty years of data say the direct share does not move against the
aggregation, and every paper concludes that hotels should try harder. This chapter tells me why the
advice fails — the aggregating function does integrating work that no direct tie reproduces for the
guest who did not know the hotel existed, so it is not a bargaining problem, and the strategy is
standing rather than share. That reframing is citable, it is not in my literature, and I would use it in
the theory section of any paper on channel strategy for independents.

**Against what.** Against the "direct booking imperative" literature and its consultancy shadow, which
sells the independent hotel an exit strategy priced as marketing spend. Against the strand of parity
scholarship that reads the enforcement decade as a policy failure — it was not; it worked on price and
was never capable of touching aggregation, and the chapter's split verdict explains why in a way that
Ennis et al. and Klopack and Pierri report but do not theorize. And against the reflex, in my own field
and in the DMA commentary around Booking.com's designation, of treating "reduce OTA dependence" as a
self-evidently sound objective.

**What would have to change for me to cite §5.5 itself, rather than §5.2.** As printed, I would cite the
theory and route around the evidence, which is a bad outcome for the author because §5.5 is what makes
the theory concrete. With the F1 decomposition fixed and the Commission's Austria/Belgium comparison in
place, I would cite §5.5 directly as the best short statement of what the parity decade shows, and I
would use it in a referee report the next time someone sends me a paper reading Schegg's direct-booking
line as evidence of failed direct strategy. That is a genuinely useful citation and it is four
paragraphs away.

**Would I assign it?** Yes, in a graduate distribution or platform-strategy seminar, paired with the
Schegg study itself and the ECN report — and I would assign it *because* of the trap. Give students the
chapter, then the underlying data, and ask them to find the gap between "direct bookings fell" and what
the components show. It teaches channel decomposition and it teaches source discipline in the same
ninety minutes. That is a compliment with a sting in it: right now the chapter is more useful to me as
an exercise than as a citation, and the fixes in Part 3 reverse that.

I would not assign it to undergraduates. §§1–4 assume a reader who already knows why Habermas is in the
room.
