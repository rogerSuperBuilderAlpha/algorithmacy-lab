## 1. Who I am

I teach science and technology studies at a university in South Asia and hold a fellowship at a European institute, which means I spend part of each year being told that my field site is a case and the rest of it being told that it is a limitation. I work on data colonialism after Couldry and Mejias, on the epistemic politics Ricaurte and Milan and Treré opened up, and on platform labour in Lagos, Manila, and Bengaluru — couriers, microtaskers, and the people who sell on marketplaces they will never be able to phone. Payal Arora's argument that the next billion users are not a deficit version of the first billion is one I have taught for years and still find people resisting. The volume editor's call reached me through a colleague who asked whether anyone on the panel would notice that a book about sovereignty had been commissioned in a language where the word already belongs to someone. I came to this chapter believing three things: that *sovereignty* is not an unclaimed term, that competence frameworks reliably convert structural exclusion into personal shortfall, and that the European regulatory apparatus is a magnificent local achievement that its authors keep mistaking for a general theory.

I will say at the outset what I am not going to write. The lazy version of this review lists the absent citations, observes that the chapter is provincial, and stops. That review would be true and useless, and it would also be unfair to a manuscript that concedes the distributional problem in §4, names the political economy of opacity rather than treating opacity as accident, and refuses to romanticize state-controlled platforms in §8. The chapter is not naive. It is *situated and unaware that it is situated*, which is a different and more repairable fault, and my job is to say exactly where the situatedness becomes a defect in the argument rather than a feature of the sample.

### F1 — *Sovereignty* is used for eleven thousand words without meeting the literature that has held it since 1998 [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter does.** §1 defends the naming decision carefully and at length: Bodin's supremacy, perpetuity, and indivisibility met head-on; Habermas's desubstantialized popular sovereignty enlisted to show that a shared and partial standing is still sovereignty; Repetto (2025) on 240 works, quoted to the effect that recognizing multiple coexisting digital sovereigns "neither stretch[es] the concept of sovereignty nor turn[s] digital sovereignty into something different from sovereignty tout court." §6 then situates the construct against Floridi, Pohle and Thiel, Roberts et al., Couture and Toupin, Bannerman, Pasquale, and Cohen.

**What fails.** Couture and Toupin (2019) — cited in §6 for exactly the right reason, that "the move is not always centralizing" and that grassroots and emancipatory variants exist — organize their survey around five perspectives on digital sovereignty, one of which is Indigenous digital sovereignty. The chapter cites the survey and drops that strand. Hummel et al. (2021), cited twice, is a review of *data* sovereignty that also covers it (VERIFY the exact coverage). So the omission is not a failure to find the literature. The chapter walked past it twice, in its own footnotes.

And the omission is not decorative, because indigenous data sovereignty is a rival theory of the same object that reaches the opposite conclusion on the chapter's central mechanism. The First Nations principles of OCAP — ownership, control, access, possession — date from 1998 and assert standing over data collected about a people by institutions that people cannot exit. Te Mana Raraunga's charter (2016) asserts Māori rights and interests in data held by the New Zealand state. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Carroll et al., 2020) put *Authority to control* at the centre and ground it in collective self-determination under UNDRIP. Every one of those is a claim to a share in the governance of an arrangement the claimant cannot leave — which is the chapter's own definition of coordinative sovereignty, arrived at two to three decades earlier, by people who built working institutions around it.

Where they collide with the chapter is the precondition. §6 states it plainly: "Algorithmacy is the precondition. An actor cannot contest a mediator's arbitrary power without modeling how the mediator sorts and optimizes." The indigenous data sovereignty tradition refuses precisely that ordering, and refuses it for a reason: a competence condition on standing is the historical mechanism by which standing was withheld. Literacy tests, capacity assessments, and competency findings are not analogies here; they are the instruments. The chapter anticipates the objection in a single clause — "The precondition governs exercise, not who is formally endowed with standing. An institution can grant standing before the competence is widely held" — and that clause restates the structure rather than answering it. Grant the right, withhold the capacity, then explain the outcome by reference to the capacity. That is what a treaty right has been for a century and a half.

**Why it fails for me.** I would have to defend this chapter in a room where somebody has done the OCAP training, and I could not. Not because the chapter is wrong — I think it is largely right, and I think its account of standing within an unexitable coordination is *better specified* than most of what the indigenous data sovereignty literature has published, because it distinguishes the case where exit is unavailable from the case where exit is merely expensive. That is a genuine contribution to a debate the chapter does not know it is in. The failure is that a reader from that tradition encounters a chapter about sovereignty and competence that never names them, and concludes the authors do not know they exist. Not knowing is worse than disagreeing.

**Damage.** High, and it is venue-specific damage. The volume is titled *Digital Sovereignty*. A chapter whose whole contribution is a claim about what the word can be made to do is the chapter in that volume most exposed on this. And the fix is *cheap* — one paragraph in §6, two sentences in §1, four references — which makes leaving it out harder to explain, not easier.

**Fix:** paste-ready in R1 and R2 below. [FIX NOW]

### F2 — Contingency is indexed to a jurisdiction, and the diagnostic does not carry the index [FIX NOW]

This is my central analytic finding and I think it is the one worth the author's Saturday.

**What the chapter claims.** §5.2 builds the bypass test and demonstrates node/edge divergence on the App Store payment gate: "Node says necessary and edge says contingent, on the same function, in the same firm. The Digital Markets Act's anti-steering obligations are the edge test run by a regulator." §5.3 then makes the strategic conversion: "Whether a coordinated actor can leave becomes a designable fact, set by whether the mediator is contingent, and contingency is a policy and strategy variable." §5.2 makes the novelty claim on the same basis: the neighbouring tests do not return "a strategy to the party inside the coordination," and the bypass test does.

**What fails.** Apple's alternative business terms, the ones the anti-steering decision produced, apply to apps distributed in the European Union (VERIFY against Apple's developer documentation and the Commission's Case DMA.100109 summary the chapter already cites). A developer in Nairobi or Jakarta selling into her home market gets none of it. So take the chapter's own demonstration case and run it twice in August 2026: for a Berlin developer, the payment gate is contingent, the constraint has been lifted, and the strategy is exit. For a Nairobi developer, the same function of the same firm in the same month is *contingent in structure and necessary in effect*, because the rule holding the tie shut is a private rule made in California, adjudicated where she is not, and unreachable by any government with authority over her. The chapter has the machinery for this and stops one index short. It already says the classification "depends on which coordination is being asked about, since the diagnostic sorts functions for a given dependent party" — actor-indexed, function-indexed, and not jurisdiction-indexed.

The second half is sharper. Look at what the diagnostic returns on a contingent verdict. §5.4: "Against a contingent gate the move is toward exit: interoperability, portability, an alternative channel." §8: "support interoperability, build or back an alternative, break the lock-in." Every one of those is a regulatory achievement, not a firm action. Interoperability is mandated; portability is legislated; an alternative channel at comparable cost is what an anti-steering remedy creates. Which means the strategy the diagnostic returns to a dependent party outside a jurisdiction that will run the edge test is *no strategy at all* — and that contradicts the chapter's own novelty claim against essential facilities. The chapter's advantage over the doctrine was supposed to be that the dependent party runs the test herself instead of waiting for a tribunal. She can indeed run it herself. She cannot act on the answer without the tribunal. In Brussels that gap is invisible because the Commission stands in it. Outside Brussels it is the entire problem.

**Why it fails for me.** I have watched exactly this play out with sellers I interview. They can tell you with precision that a gate is arbitrary and liftable — they do not need a framework for that. What they do not have is anyone with reach over the firm. A diagnostic that tells them the constraint is liftable, when nobody in their legal order can lift it, is a diagnostic that has correctly named their situation and given them nothing. Worse, the chapter's own warning cuts the other way for them: §8 says "Treating a necessary mediator as contingent wastes effort on an exit that cannot be had." For that seller the exit cannot be had, and the chapter's classification tells her to pursue it.

**The fix is not a fourth category.** The chapter already has four and reviewer 04 is right to ask whether it applies them consistently. What it needs is a *modifier on the counterfactual*: contingency is relative to a rule-maker with reach over the mediator, and where no such rule-maker exists the contingent mediator is unexitable in practice and the actor's live repertoire is the voice repertoire. That sentence costs the chapter nothing and buys it a great deal — it converts a scope limitation into a specification, and it generates the best empirical prediction in the whole manuscript, which I hand to §9 in R6: *the same function of the same platform should elicit exit-seeking behaviour from dependent firms inside DMA jurisdiction and voice-building behaviour from dependent firms outside it.* That is a testable, publishable, genuinely novel prediction that only this framework generates, and the author currently owns it without knowing.

**Damage.** High for the chapter's generality claim, moderate for its European reader, and enormous as a missed opportunity. **Fix:** R3 and R6. [FIX NOW for the modifier; the empirical design is CARRY FORWARD to the *Organization Theory* paper.]

### F3 — Necessity is also indexed to what public infrastructure someone has built, and §5.4's own example shows it [FIX NOW for the hedge · TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD for the treatment]

**What the chapter claims.** §5.4: "A payment network authorizes transactions, a determination that depends jointly on the merchant's request and the issuer's approval and that neither could produce alone, which is necessary integrating work."

**What fails.** Brazil's Pix, built by the central bank and launched in 2020, and India's UPI, built by a public-sector-backed body and launched in 2016, reproduced that integrating work outside the card networks and moved a very large share of retail payment onto rails the card networks do not own (VERIFY the current shares; the direction is not in doubt). Neither is the edge operation and neither is ordinary node substitution — no private rival duplicated the facility, and no rule forbidding direct contact was repealed. A state built a substitute for the integrating function. That is a third operation, and the chapter has no name for it.

The consequence for the framework is real, not pedantic. §5.2 defines a necessary mediator as one whose "position rests on integrating work that the direct tie cannot reproduce," and the definition reads as though it names a property of the coordination. Pix shows it naming a property of *the infrastructure that happens to exist*. Payment authorization was necessary integrating work in Brazil until a public institution decided it was a public utility, and then it was not. If necessity can be dissolved by construction rather than by deregulation, then §5.3's "only voice remains" is too strong: against a necessary mediator, the third path is to build the integration publicly, and voice's job may be to demand that construction rather than to negotiate terms.

**Why it fails for me, specifically.** Because this is the chapter's own thesis, demonstrated at national scale, in the two places the chapter does not look. The strongest available evidence that coordinative standing can be built against a mediator nobody could leave comes from Brasília and Mumbai. India's Open Network for Digital Commerce is the same move aimed at the marketplace layer rather than the payment layer — a state-sponsored interoperability protocol that unbundles buyer-side from seller-side applications precisely in order to make demand aggregation contingent (VERIFY: I can point to Islam et al., 2024, in *Open Information Science* and to the Aapti Institute handbook, but I have not been able to load the De Gruyter record to confirm the author list, so the author must source this rather than take it from me). Whether ONDC has worked is genuinely contested and I would not have the chapter claim that it has. That it constitutes a deliberate, state-run attempt to move a marketplace function from necessary to contingent is not contested, and it is the single best live test of this chapter's framework anywhere in the world.

**Damage.** Low for the chapter as it stands, because the payment example survives with one hedging clause. Very high as forgone contribution: this is a paper, and it is the author's to write.

**Fix:** R5 for the hedge now; the rest belongs in the January submission. [FIX NOW / TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]

### F4 — The competence gradient is theorized as class and not as language, feedback, or recourse [FIX IF TIME]

**Credit first, because it is owed.** §4's political-economy paragraph is better than most of what the algorithmic-literacy field produces. It names the cost structure ("time, attention, and often paid analysts and tooling"), cites Cotter and Reisdorf (2020) for the empirical gradient, adds Petre et al. (2019) for the mechanism that holds it in place — the gaming accusation falling hardest on producers who are not already established — and then refuses the comfortable ending: "The platform, for its part, does more than neglect to make its rules legible, since opacity preserves the rent." The paragraph closes on the chapter's best and bleakest sentence: "Coordinative sovereignty, which algorithmacy makes possible, therefore concentrates where algorithmacy is already held." I have no complaint about any of that. It is the deficit model's obituary, written by the person proposing the competence construct.

**What fails.** The gradient the paragraph draws is socioeconomic and intra-national. Three further axes carry mechanisms the chapter's own vignette depends on, and it names none of them.

*Language.* The seller in §4 infers the ranking rule from evidence, and the evidence includes seller guidelines, policy-change notices, dispute forms, and support correspondence. Where those exist in English first and in a seller's language late, badly, or never, the inferential capacity is gated before it begins. This is not an access complaint. It is a claim about the chapter's construct: the *translational* capacity, defined as rendering one's conduct into the terms the system reads, is trivially harder when the terms are published in a second language and the machine-translated version of the policy differs from the enforced one.

*Feedback.* The vignette turns on a single sentence: "Watching which listings fell and which held, she sees that the fall tracks the items where buyers had opened disputes." That presumes a seller-analytics dashboard, retained historical data, enough volume for the pattern to separate from noise, and connectivity stable enough to check daily. The platform distributes the evidence from which a working theory can be built, and it distributes it by market tier. A construct whose first component is inferential should say that the platform controls the inference's inputs.

*Recourse.* The temporal capacity — holding a strategy across a system that changes without notice — is cheap where a mistaken deactivation gets appealed and expensive where it does not. Anwar and Graham (2021) document what the absence looks like for African gig workers; the finding generalizes to sellers.

**Why this matters beyond §4.** The chapter states the concentration finding and then does not carry it forward. §7's instruments and §8's decision procedure both proceed as though the concentration can be relieved by a regulator, and the concentration the chapter has just described is worst exactly where the regulator is absent. One sentence at the end of §8 closing that loop would be worth more than another instrument in the table.

**Damage.** Moderate. The chapter is honest here; it is honest about half the gradient. **Fix:** R4. [FIX IF TIME]

### F5 — §8's non-Western material is entirely cautionary, and the chapter's own citations contain the corrective [FIX NOW, cheap]

**What the chapter does.** §8's closing paragraph sets out to reframe the openness/control standoff. Its negative examples: WeChat "woven into Chinese public administration to the point of hosting police modules" (Bernot, 2025), and Russia's sovereign internet regime (Stadnik, 2021). Its positive examples: CoopCycle's member cooperatives in France, federated social networks, and the New York Drivers Cooperative. That is the paragraph's whole geography — two authoritarian states as warnings, three Euro-American cases as demonstrations.

**What fails, on the chapter's own terms.** Two things, and neither requires me to import a standpoint.

First, §7 cites Lei (2021) — *Delivering Solidarity: Platform Architecture and Collective Contention in China's Platform Economy*, in *American Sociological Review* — for the proposition that platform architecture shapes where grievances pool and whether contention can organize. That citation is three pages away from a paragraph asserting that WeChat's users "hold no standing within a system the state governs securely," and it documents Chinese platform workers organizing contention. The chapter has the evidence that complicates its own flattest sentence and does not use it. Pulling one clause forward costs nothing and makes the sentence defensible.

Second, and larger: the paragraph claims to reframe openness versus control, and the case that actually breaks the binary is missing. The interesting state is not the one that builds a closed national platform; it is the one that builds an *open interoperable protocol* — UPI, ONDC, Pix, the Beckn stack. That is control at the infrastructure layer deployed to create exit at the coordination layer, which is neither pole of the standoff the paragraph stages. The chapter's thesis predicts something specific and non-obvious about those cases, and skipping them leaves the reframing resting on Mastodon.

**Why it fails for me.** Because the composition is the argument. A reader in Bengaluru reaches §8 and finds that the two places outside the Atlantic that the chapter names are the two places it names as failures, while every institutional experiment worth learning from happens in Paris, New York, and the fediverse. That reader will not write a rebuttal. She will simply stop treating the chapter as being about her.

**Damage.** Moderate for the argument, high for the readership. **Fix:** R7 for the Lei clause [FIX NOW]; one sentence on protocol-building states [FIX IF TIME].

### F8 — §7's table has no instrument that works without a regulator [FIX IF TIME]

Seven rows. Interoperability and portability, antitrust and anti-steering remedies, contestability of individual decisions, collective bargaining, data trusts and data intermediaries, cooperative ownership, oversight boards. Five of the seven presuppose a legal order with reach over the mediator; cooperative ownership presupposes capital; oversight boards presuppose a platform that constitutes one.

## 5. Part 3 — paste-ready revisions

Written in the chapter's register: impersonal, active, named agents, no first person.

### R1 — new paragraph for §6, after the Repetto sentence and before the Bannerman paragraph [F1]

> One family of sovereignty claims has held the word longest and sits closest to the construct advanced here. Indigenous data sovereignty asserts a people's authority over data collected about it by institutions it did not choose and cannot leave: the First Nations principles of OCAP — ownership, control, access, possession — have governed First Nations health data in Canada since 1998 (First Nations Information Governance Centre, 2023), Te Mana Raraunga's charter asserts Māori rights and interests in data held by the New Zealand state (Te Mana Raraunga, 2016), and the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance place authority to control alongside collective benefit, responsibility, and ethics (Carroll et al., 2020). Each claims a share in the governance of an arrangement its claimants cannot exit, which is the relation coordinative sovereignty names. Two differences matter. That tradition grounds standing in collective self-determination rather than in a mediator's structure, so it claims standing whether or not a bypass exists; and it declines to condition standing on a competence, because competence conditions are how standing has historically been withheld. The construct developed here takes the second point as a constraint rather than a rebuttal: algorithmacy conditions the exercise of standing and never its allocation, and an institution that grants standing without funding the competence has granted a form. Kukutai and Taylor (2016) collect the agenda; Couture and Toupin (2019) place the indigenous strand among the field's five.

### R2 — replacement for the closing sentence of §1's Repetto paragraph [F1, F7]

> Two limits on the argument follow from where its evidence comes from. The instruments examined below are European, the cases are European and North American, and the diagnostic in section 5 assumes throughout that some authority can be moved to lift a constraint. What the framework predicts where no such authority exists is taken up in section 5.3.

### R3 — new paragraph for §5.3, after "Retention suppresses exit and activates nothing." [F2]

> Contingency is relative to a rule-maker with reach over the mediator, and the relativity has consequences the counterfactual alone does not show. The Digital Markets Act's anti-steering obligations run to applications distributed in the European Union, so the payment gate of section 5.2 is contingent for a developer inside that jurisdiction and unaltered for one outside it — the same function of the same firm, classified alike by the counterfactual and lived differently. Where the mediator is a foreign firm, the constraint a private rule, and the state that would lift it the weaker party, a verdict of contingency identifies a gate correctly and returns a strategy the dependent party cannot execute, because interoperability, portability, and an alternative channel at comparable cost are all achievements of law rather than moves available to a firm. The diagnostic then yields the voice repertoire on a contingent mediator, and the practical instruction is to treat an unliftable constraint as one: contest the terms, since the tie will not open. Naming the rule-maker is therefore part of running the test, and a classification that omits it will misdirect exactly the actors with least room to waste effort.

### R4 — insertion into §4, after "algorithmic knowledge varies with socioeconomic advantage instead of spreading evenly across those subject to it (Cotter & Reisdorf, 2020)" [F4]

> Three further conditions distribute the competence, and each attaches to one of its parts. The inferential part runs on evidence the platform supplies, so a seller with historical performance data, sufficient volume for a pattern to separate from noise, and reliable connectivity can form a working theory that a seller without them cannot. The translational part runs on published terms, so where policies, dispute forms, and enforcement notices appear in one language first and in a seller's language late or not at all, the terms the system reads are terms she reaches second-hand. The temporal part runs on the cost of error, which is low where a mistaken suspension can be appealed and high where no channel of appeal reaches the decision (Anwar & Graham, 2021). Language, evidence, and recourse are conditions of the competence rather than supplements to it, and each is distributed by the platform and by the jurisdiction rather than by the actor.

### R5 — replacement for §5.4's payment sentence [F3]

> A payment network authorizes transactions, a determination that depends jointly on the merchant's request and the issuer's approval and that neither could produce alone, which is necessary integrating work — necessary, that is, relative to the rails that exist, since a public authority that builds an equivalent rail dissolves the necessity without repealing any constraint or substituting a private rival.

### R6 — fifth research direction for §9 [F2, F9]

> A fifth direction tests the jurisdictional index directly. Because a platform's compliance obligations are territorial while its functions are not, the same function of the same firm can be contingent for dependent actors inside a regulator's reach and unexitable for those outside it, which yields a prediction about behavior: dependent firms inside the jurisdiction should pursue exit against that function, and dependent firms outside it should build voice against the same function, with the classification held constant. A matched design across jurisdictions would separate the framework's structural claim from the regulatory environment that has so far carried its cases, and would establish whether the diagnostic guides action where no authority will run the edge test.

### R7 — replacement for §8's WeChat sentence [F5]

> WeChat is woven into Chinese public administration to the point of hosting police modules (Bernot, 2025), and its architecture affords its users little standing over the terms on which it mediates them, though the same architecture shapes where grievances pool and whether contention can organize at all, and Chinese platform workers have organized within it (Lei, 2021).

Colonial land alienation ran through that gap, where a legally valid instrument bound a party who could perceive that something bound her and could not work within it, and the gap is the strongest evidence for the pairing this chapter describes: a standing that its holder lacks the competence to exercise is a form.

## 6. Part 6 — Verdict

**Major revisions.** I want to be precise about the label, because it will read as harsher than I mean. The work I am asking for totals roughly seven hundred words of insertion, one table row, one modifier on the central counterfactual, and six references — all of it doable by 30 August, most of it drafted above. What makes it major rather than minor is not the hours. It is that F1 and F2 together mean the chapter's title claim is currently unearned outside one legal order, and the repair has to appear in §1, §5, §6, §8, and §10 rather than in a footnote. A chapter that specifies its scope and meets the rival tradition is a stronger chapter making a smaller claim; a chapter that keeps the universal claim and neither will get taken apart by the first reader from either literature who opens it.

**The single most important fix:** R3 — index contingency to a rule-maker with reach over the mediator. It repairs the generality problem, it converts a scope limitation into a specification, and it hands the author the best empirical prediction the framework has produced.

**The chapter's biggest genuine strength:** the honesty of §4's closing move. Most competence constructs promise diffusion and stop. This one states that "coordinative sovereignty, which algorithmacy makes possible, therefore concentrates where algorithmacy is already held," documents the mechanism holding the gradient in place, and then in §9 refuses the comfortable historical analogy — Paglayan on schooling as a response to unrest rather than an extension of the franchise, and the platform-expertise industry teaching compliance rather than contestation. A framework that names the conditions under which it fails is a framework worth testing. That paragraph is why this review is long rather than short.

**The one thing only the author can supply:** a decision about whether the necessary/contingent distinction is a claim about coordinations or a claim about coordinations-under-an-institutional-order. F2 and F3 press the same joint from two sides — one showing that contingency depends on who can lift a rule, the other showing that necessity depends on what rails someone has built. If the answer is the first, the chapter is a European theory of platform dependence and should say so proudly. If the second, the framework is more interesting than the chapter currently claims and the companion and the January submission have a great deal of work ahead of them. Nobody outside the author team can make that call.

## 7. Part 7 — Citation behavior

**Would I cite it? Yes, and against a specific target.** I would cite §5.2 and §5.4 against the reflex that dominates digital-sovereignty policy talk in the countries I work in, which is that the answer to dependence on a foreign platform is a domestic one — a national champion, a data-localization mandate, a state-built alternative. The chapter gives me a clean argument that ownership answers the wrong question for an actor coordinating through a mediator it cannot leave, and the portfolio move in §5.4 is the sharpest version of that argument I have read: a ministry that treats its dependence on a marketplace as one thing to be replaced will spend a decade replacing the function that was never the problem. I would use it in exactly that argument, in exactly those rooms.

There is an irony I will own. The passage I would most likely quote is the one I have spent this review attacking — §8's line that a closed national platform "reproduces the co-optation it was meant to escape, now under a domestic flag." I object to its composition and I agree with its content, and it says the thing my field needs said in a register my field's own literature cannot easily reach, because we are institutionally reluctant to criticize a Southern state's platform project. A management chapter can say it without the argument being about the author's loyalties.

**What I would not cite it for.** Sovereignty. Not because the naming defence fails — §1's defence against Bodin is well made — but because citing this chapter as an authority on what sovereignty can be made to mean, while it does not meet OCAP, Te Mana Raraunga, or CARE, would make me responsible for the omission in front of readers who would notice immediately. I would have to add the corrective in a footnote every time, and past two or three uses I would cite Kukutai and Taylor instead and mention this chapter as a management-side parallel.

**Would I assign it? Yes, with a caveat the authors will not enjoy.** It goes into my graduate seminar on platform governance, paired with Couldry and Mejias and with Kukutai and Taylor, and the seminar question is: *this framework was built where the regulator exists — what survives the trip?* That is a genuine use and a productive week, and it is not the use the authors intend, because the chapter functions there as the object of critique as much as its instrument. If R1 and R3 land, that changes. With scope declared and contingency indexed to a rule-maker, I would assign it as a primary framework rather than a specimen, and I would use it in my own work on ONDC — where, as F3 argues, it is the only framework I know of that predicts the right thing.
