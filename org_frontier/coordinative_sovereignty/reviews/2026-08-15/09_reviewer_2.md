# Review 09 — Reviewer 2 (anonymous, hostile, generalist)

**Recommendation: major revision.** The chapter has one genuinely good idea, one genuine corollary, and
about eight thousand words of scaffolding around them. The good idea survives. It survives despite the
chapter, which twice states its own central definition in the vocabulary section 5.2 warns against, and
which runs its flagship empirical case to a conclusion that contradicts the decision procedure section 8
builds on top of it.

## 1. Who I am

I review across fields for three journals and an edited-volume series, usually the manuscript nobody else
would take. I have no stake in digital sovereignty, no stake in republicanism, and no interest in whether
management studies gets a new construct this year. What I have is a rule: a chapter earns a new word by
running its own test on a case where the test could embarrass it. I came to this manuscript expecting the
usual trade — a coined term, a four-cell taxonomy, a literature review shaped to make the term look
inevitable, and a future-research section standing in for evidence. I got about two-thirds of that, plus a
counterfactual operation that is actually new and actually does work. The counterfactual is why I am
writing major revision instead of reject. Everything below is an argument that the chapter is currently
protecting the counterfactual from itself.

Impersonal academic register, no first person in the manuscript, APA 7, masked self-citation. Confirmed and

### F1. The chapter defines its central construct in the vocabulary it has just forbidden. [FIX NOW]

§5.2 closes with a warning, and it is the sharpest sentence in the chapter:

> "Reading irreducibility as necessity is how a contingent gate passes for an unavoidable one."

The Key Terms entry for *Irreducibility* repeats it: "Irreducibility describes a coordination as it stands
and does not by itself establish that a mediator is necessary."

Then §6 opens the definition of the chapter's title construct:

> "Coordinative sovereignty is the autonomy proper to being **irreducibly coordinated**."

And the Key Terms entry repeats the error verbatim: "**Coordinative sovereignty:** The autonomy proper to
being irreducibly coordinated." And §6 again, thirty lines later: "the question of standing within an
**irreducible coordination** outlives the question of who holds the title."

The word "irreducible" appears five times in this chapter. Twice it is the §5.1 property that the chapter
insists does not entail unexitability. Three times it is the definiens of coordinative sovereignty, where
the chapter needs the §5.2 property that does. The construct is therefore defined on the wrong side of the
distinction the chapter exists to draw.

There is a second-order problem that makes this worse rather than merely careless. §5.2's four-way taxonomy
already assigns "reducible" a technical meaning — a mediator "that was never in the coordination's core."
So "irreducibly coordinated" reads, on the chapter's own terms, as *coordinated through a mediator that is
not reducible*, which includes contingent mediators. On the literal reading, coordinative sovereignty is
the autonomy proper to being coordinated through a contingent gate — exactly the case §5.3 says exit
answers. The definition, read strictly, covers the cases it was built to exclude.

**Damage:** maximal. A hostile reader who reads §5.2 and §6 in that order concludes the author has not held
the two tests apart in their own head, and every subsequent claim of a distinction becomes suspect. This is
a fifteen-minute fix and it is the fix I would make first.

### F2. §5.5 and §8 give opposite verdicts on the same hotels, and §5.4 gives a third. [FIX NOW]

This is the chapter's best empirical passage and its worst internal contradiction.

**Step one — §5.4 classifies the structure.** On a marketplace:

> "It matches demand to supply, real integrating work that buyers can bypass for goods they already know,
> which is **partial**."

**Step two — §5.5 meets the identical structure and labels it differently.** The OTA's demand aggregation
introduces travelers who would never have found the hotel (integrating work) and is bypassable for
travelers who already know it (a bypassable part). Same shape, adjacent subsection. §5.5's verdict:

> "Demand aggregation survives the bypass for that traveler, and it is **necessary**."

Two labels for one structure, four hundred words apart. And the chapter has a category for this structure —
it wrote the category in §5.4 and then did not use it on the case that most needs it.

**Step three — §8 asserts a failure the data in §5.5 do not show.**

> "Treating a necessary mediator as contingent wastes effort on an exit that cannot be had, and **the hotels
> of section 5.5 show what that waste looks like at sector scale**."

But run §8's own procedure on what the hotels did. They attacked the parity clause — which §5.5 classifies
as contingent and against which §8 prescribes "the move is toward exit: support interoperability, build or
back an alternative, break the lock-in." They pursued it through "contract law and competition enforcement,"
which is what §5.5 recommends. And they won: wide parity abandoned in 2015, national legislation, narrow
clauses enforced by 2021, C-264/23 in 2024. **The hotels did precisely what the chapter's decision procedure
tells a firm facing a contingent function to do.** §5.5 even concedes that the share increase "owes
something to mobile search and to consolidation among the agencies."

So the sector-scale waste of §8 is, on the chapter's own account, a sector that correctly identified the
contingent function, correctly attacked it, correctly won, and saw dependence rise for reasons the chapter
attributes elsewhere. The moral §5.5 draws — "a hotel that spent the decade trying to leave mistook the
necessary function for the contingent one" — requires a hotel that attacked demand aggregation. The chapter
supplies no such hotel. It supplies a sector that attacked parity clauses.

The honest reading is the *partial* reading, and it is better for the chapter than the one printed: the
hotels ran the split correctly, contested the bypassable part, and the necessary part held — which is
exactly what a portfolio view predicts and what a single-intermediary view does not. §5.5 already contains
that sentence ("only a contingent function was removed, so the necessary function should hold"). It then
overreaches into a diagnosis of sector-wide error that its own evidence does not carry, and §8 repeats the
overreach as an example.

**Damage:** severe, and it lands on the one place the chapter has data. A reviewer who catches it concludes
the diagnostic is being run backward from a conclusion the author already had. Reviewer 08 will press the
measurement side of §5.5; this is the logical side, and it is independent of whether the Schegg series is
sound.

### F3. The independent software vendor of §1 is never run through §5 or §8, and when you run it, it lands in the exit branch. [FIX NOW]

§1's opening exhibit:

> "An independent software vendor with impeccable data-protection practices still depends, for its entire
> access to customers, on an application store whose ranking and review decisions it can neither usefully
> see nor predict nor contest."

That firm is the chapter's emblem of an actor who complies fully and remains helpless. It is the case that
motivates coordinative sovereignty. It never returns. §8 mentions "an application developer or a
marketplace seller" and classifies nothing.

Run the chapter's procedure on it. The chapter has already classified one of the store's functions itself:

> "Restore the developer-to-user tie for payment, through sideloading or a link to an external purchase,
> and the gate's force disappears, because it never rested on integrating work. Node says necessary and edge
> says **contingent**."

Now do the other two. *App review*: its force comes from the absence of a direct install tie; restore
sideloading and the gate's force disappears; **contingent** by the same reasoning, and the chapter has
already conceded the premise in the payment case. *Ranking and discovery*: necessary for the user who would
never have found the app, bypassable for the user who already knows it — **partial**, by §5.4's marketplace
rule.

So the chapter's motivating case classifies as contingent, contingent, partial. Zero necessary functions.
§8 therefore instructs it: "the move is toward exit: support interoperability, build or back an alternative,
break the lock-in." Not voice. Not standing. Not coordinative sovereignty.

The chapter opens with an actor for whom, by its own test, the older sovereignty of exit is the right
answer, and then spends nine sections arguing that exit is the wrong answer.

I do not think the ISV is a bad example. I think it is a *partial* case that the chapter uses rhetorically
as a necessary one, because "for its entire access to customers" pre-decides the verdict in §1 before any
test exists. That phrase is doing the work the diagnostic is supposed to do.

**Damage:** severe. The first case a hostile reader will run the procedure on is the one on page one, and
it comes back against the chapter. Reviewer 10 has been asked to do exactly this with a forty-person member
firm; he will find the same thing from the practitioner end.

### F4. The four-way taxonomy is introduced twice, used once, and then abandoned. [FIX NOW]

"Partial" as a taxonomy term appears in §5.2 (definition), §5.4 (twice), and Key Terms. "Reducible" appears
in §5.4 and Key Terms. After line 548 — that is, after §5.4 ends — **neither word appears again anywhere in
the chapter.** Not in §5.5's verdict, not in §7's table, not in §8's decision procedure, not in §9, not in
§10, not in the abstract.

Concretely:

- §7's institutional table has **two columns**: "Against a contingent mediator" and "Against a necessary
  mediator." A reader with a partial verdict — which, by §5.4's own account of a real marketplace, is the
  modal verdict — finds no column.
- §8's decision procedure has **two branches**: "Where the firm depends on a contingent mediator … Where it
  depends on a necessary mediator." No branch for partial, none for reducible.
- §10 restates the binary: "where the mediator is contingent the older sovereignty of exit still works …
  Where the mediator is necessary, only voice remains."
- The abstract states the binary: "A counterfactual sorts mediators an actor can leave from those it can
  only govern."

§5.4 gives partial one sentence of guidance — "contest the bypassable part and accept the integrating one"
— and cites Baldwin (2008) for where to cut. That is the whole institutional and managerial content of half
the taxonomy. Reducible gets nothing at all after its definition; it is a category with no consequence,
included, so far as I can tell, to make the table of possibilities look complete.

Either the four-way is load-bearing, in which case §7 needs two more columns (or two more rows of prose)
and §8 needs two more branches, or it is a presentational flourish inside §5 and should be labelled as a
refinement rather than as the taxonomy. The current arrangement gets the credit for a four-way distinction
while operating a two-way one.

**Damage:** high, and it compounds F2 and F3, both of which are cases where the missing category was the
right answer.

### F5. For the actor §8 addresses, the exit branch is made of voice actions. [FIX IF TIME]

§8's contingent branch: "support interoperability, build or back an alternative, break the lock-in." §5.4's:
"interoperability, portability, an alternative channel."

Now ask who does those things. The chapter answers in §5.2: "The Digital Markets Act's anti-steering
obligations are the edge test run **by a regulator**." §7 answers again: "mandated interoperability"; "an
antitrust remedy"; "the breaking of an exclusive." §5.3 is explicit that the constraint holding a contingent
mediator in place is lifted by "Deregulation, mandated interoperability, an antitrust remedy."

None of those is an action available to a dependent firm. They are outcomes a dependent firm can *lobby
for*, *litigate for*, or *organize toward* — which is to say, they are exercises of voice, addressed to a
regulator rather than to the mediator. The chapter's central dichotomy partitions the *world*, cleanly. It
does not partition the *action space of the actor the chapter is written for*, and §8 is written as though
it does.

This is not fatal and I do not think it is unfixable — the honest version is that the diagnostic sorts
mediators into those where voice should be aimed at the regulator (contingent) and those where it must be
aimed at the mediator's governance (necessary). That is still a useful distinction and it is more defensible
than the one printed. But it costs the chapter the clean Hirschman mapping, and the clean Hirschman mapping
is load-bearing for §5.3, §7, §8, and §10.

There is a smaller version of the same problem in §5.3: "contingency is a policy and strategy variable,"
and in §5.2, via Gu and Zhu (2021), "Necessity is also not fixed." If both sides of the distinction are
designable, then §5's opening claim — "the property can be checked" — is checking a fact about the current
policy environment, not a structural property of the coordination. The chapter should say so rather than
leave the two claims in adjacent paragraphs.

### F8. The independent-variation claim rests on one case and one that does not yet exist. [FIX IF TIME]

§8's contribution — the reason a measurement literature should change — is that digital and coordinative
sovereignty "vary independently across firms." §1 asserts it: "The two vary independently." The evidence is
two cells of a 2×2:

- High DS / low CS: the app developer and marketplace seller. Real.
- Low DS / high CS: the platform worker under the platform-work directive. And the chapter concedes:
  "No member state had fully transposed the directive as this chapter was written, so the standing it
  describes is a legislative commitment, not yet a present fact."

One observed cell and one anticipated cell is not independent variation; it is a conjecture with an
illustration and a promise. The concession is honest and it should stay, but the claim above it needs
downgrading to match — "the typology predicts independent variation, and the second pairing is arriving in
law before it is observable in data" is defensible. "The two vary independently" is not, on this evidence.

### F9. §9 is four instruments and one test, and the test is third in a serial chain. [FIX IF TIME]

The chapter says it itself: "Four directions follow, **in rough order of dependence**." Read the chain.

Direction 1 builds an algorithmacy scale, and concedes its central problem without solving it: "An
algorithmacy scale would have to show that it captures what those do not." That is a restatement of the
discriminant-validity threat from DeVito and Dogruel, not an approach to it — the one concrete suggestion,
a behavioral criterion of prediction accuracy, is a good idea and gets a single sentence with no site, no
system, and no scoring rule.

Direction 2 builds a coordinative-sovereignty scale, whose discriminant target is psychological empowerment.

Direction 3 is the only direction that tests the chapter's actual claim — classify mediators, then see
whether the response fits the type. It presupposes 1 and 2.

Direction 4 presupposes the companion paper.

So nothing in the empirical program can be run until two new validated multidimensional scales exist, and
the falsifiable proposition is third in the queue. That is not a program; it is a queue with the test at the
back. I am not asking for data in fifteen days. I am asking the chapter to name **one study a reader could
run next year with existing instruments** — direction 3 can be run with a coded classification and any
credible measure of firm response, without waiting for either scale — and to lead §9 with it.

There is a real payoff sentence buried in direction 3: "misallocation becomes a measurable predictor of poor
outcomes." That is the chapter's testable claim. It is in the third paragraph of the ninth section.

The chapter's structure is three sensibilities, three sovereignties, one diagnostic, one definition, one

## 4. Part 2 — style and slop audit

Register-aware. The chapter is a strong piece of prose; these are the places where the optimization shows.

**(a) The two-beat aphoristic closer, at metronome frequency.** Section- and paragraph-ends land the same
way throughout:

> "Dependence did not fall as the gate came open. It deepened." (§5.5)
> "They constitute it." (§3.1)
> "Disclosure makes an object legible. What the coordinated actor needs is standing." (§3.2)
> "Retention suppresses exit and activates nothing." (§5.3)
> "One firm, four kinds of mediator at once." (§5.4)
> "None of it will show up on a compliance audit." (§8)
> "She can perceive that something binds her, and she cannot work within it." (§4)
> "Neither reading alone nor control alone secures it." (§10)

Individually each is good. Eight of them at the same interval reads as a tic. **Fix:** let three of the eight
end on the long sentence instead. §3.1's "They constitute it" should go anyway (F7).

**(b) The counting machine.** Pre-announced enumeration opens six passages: "Three things have to be kept
apart to see what is actually new"; "Five features of platform coordination block the literate model";
"Two questions do the work, and they are not the same question"; "Two further cases complete the picture";
"Four strands of political theory converge on the construct"; "Two changes in kind mark the sequence." This
is the self-narrating-rigor family the house style bans, wearing a numeral. **Fix:** keep it where the count
is load-bearing (the five features, the two questions) and drop the announcement elsewhere. "Two further
cases complete the picture" can simply be "A mediator that was never in the coordination's core is
reducible…"

**(c) The "grip" refrain.** §1: "Some mediators lose their grip entirely. Others lose nothing." §5.2: "Some
mediators keep their grip… Others lose their grip the moment the tie is open." Same metaphor, same
paired-sentence shape, four hundred lines apart. §1 is also pre-running §5.2's whole payload, which is
defensible as forecast but reads as the chapter telling its best joke twice. **Fix:** change §1's to plain
statement — "Some mediators still do their work. Others have nothing left to do."

**(d) The "X, not Y" frame, at high density.** "a fact without a consequence"; "changes the owner, not the
leaveability"; "alters conduct, not dependence"; "a qualification, not a counterexample"; "not collective
ownership of the platform but collective-choice arrangements"; "the capability of direct dealing, not a
prior relationship"; "functions, not firms"; "a legislative commitment, not yet a present fact"; "not the
algorithm's but the firm's"; "compulsion was the mechanism and order the motive." Ten in eleven thousand
words. The reader learns the shape and starts predicting the second half of the sentence. Convert three to
plain predication.

**(e) What is clean, and I checked.** Agentless passive: two instances of "has been" in the whole body
(§3, both with the agent structurally absent and both fine). That is a genuinely good result and better
than most published chapters — say so to whoever did the v2 pass. Verbatim refrains beyond (c): none found.
Filler transitions: none found. Empty closers: none; §10 ends on a claim.

**(f) v2-specific.** The sentence-merge left ragged mid-paragraph line wraps in roughly seven places (the
"leaves untouched the / layer it cannot" break in §2.3 is the most visible; also §3.2, §5.3, §6, §7).
Harmless once typeset, but if any reviewer reads the raw file it looks like an unfinished edit. One reflow
pass.

## 6. Verdict

**Major revision.** Not because the argument is wrong — the core of it is right and the bypass test is a
real contribution — but because four of the chapter's own internal cross-checks currently fail, and three
of them (F1, F2, F3) are visible to any reader who takes the vocabulary at face value. All three are fixable
in a day.

**The single most important fix:** F2/R2–R3. The hotels are the chapter's only evidence, and the chapter
currently reads them as a sector-scale failure when its own function-split reads them as a sector-scale
confirmation. Get that right and §5.5 becomes the strongest passage in the volume. Leave it and the first
strategy scholar who reads it will conclude the diagnostic was fitted to a conclusion.

**The chapter's biggest genuine strength:** the node/edge distinction in §5.2, and specifically the
demonstration that a node test and an edge test return opposite verdicts on the same function of the same
firm. "Node says necessary and edge says contingent, on the same function, in the same firm" is a sentence
worth the chapter. The ancillary-restraints parallel (C-264/23) showing that a court already runs the
operation on clauses is the best supporting move in the piece. Nothing in brokerage, transaction cost
economics, or essential facilities does this, and the chapter is right that none of the three is run *by*
the dependent party.

**What only the author can supply:** a case where the diagnostic returns a verdict the author did not want.
Every case in §5 is chosen to produce a clean answer — the franchised dealer, the clearinghouse, the
interpreter, the App Store gate, the payment network, the four-function marketplace. The one hard case is
raised and then dissolved: "for a function whose whole business is introducing strangers it is the entire
question," answered by relativizing to traveler types, which makes every introduction function necessary
for some counterparts and contingent for others by construction. There is no threshold, "comparable cost" is
never operationalized, and §5.1 exports the difficulty to a companion paper the reader cannot see — "the
companion paper develops measures that grade the borderline cases." Discovery is not a borderline case. It
is the modal function of every platform in the chapter. **One worked case where the answer is genuinely
contested, and where the author says which way it goes and why, would do more for the construct than
sections 2 and 6 combined.**

**And to answer the question I came with:** what does this chapter give that a good essay on the bypass
test would not? Two things, both downstream of the test. §7's finding that the *same instrument changes
character* depending on the mediator type — interoperability as exit-restoring against a contingent gate
and as regulated access against a necessary one — is a genuine result and it is not obvious. §8's
two-construct typology is a real correction to how a literature measures. That is the chapter. §2 and §6
are legitimation: the three-era arc makes algorithmacy feel inevitable, and the four-strand convergence in
§6 makes coordinative sovereignty feel licensed. Neither is load-bearing for the test, §7, or §8. In an
edited volume that framing is a reasonable price of admission, and the author should be clear-eyed that
they are paying it.

## 7. Citation behavior

**Would I cite it? Yes — once, and for one thing.** I would cite it for the node/edge distinction, in an
argument about why essential-facilities reasoning and make-or-buy reasoning systematically misclassify
platform gates: both hold the network of who may deal with whom fixed, and the gate's force lives precisely
in that network. That is a claim I could not previously make with a citation. I would cite it **against**
any paper that treats platform dependence as a single risk to be diversified — the portfolio-of-mediators
point in §5.4 is the cleanest statement of that objection I have read, and "One firm, four kinds of mediator
at once" is quotable.

**What I would not cite:** the term *coordinative sovereignty*, and not because it is a bad term. I would not
cite it because I cannot yet tell a reader what a firm that has it looks like as against a firm that does
not — §9 concedes there is no instrument, and the two illustrative cases in §8 are one firm and one
directive that has not been transposed. A construct I cannot measure and cannot see varying is a construct I
cite as a framing, and framings age out. I would also not cite §2; I have no use for a three-era arc, and
the historiography is not this chapter's to defend.

**What would change that:** one study, of the sort §9's third direction describes, showing that firms whose
voice/exit allocation matches the diagnostic's classification outperform firms whose allocation does not.
That is the sentence that turns this from a framework into something I have to cite. It does not need the
companion's formalism and it does not need a new scale.

**Would I assign it?** Yes, §5 alone, in a doctoral seminar on platform governance, paired with Cutolo and
Kenney (2021) and an essential-facilities case. And I would assign it partly to see how long it takes the
room to find the §5.5/§8 contradiction, which is the best teaching object in the chapter — a well-built
diagnostic and an author reaching past what it returned. If the revisions land, I would assign §5 and §7
and let the students argue about whether §2 earns its keep.
