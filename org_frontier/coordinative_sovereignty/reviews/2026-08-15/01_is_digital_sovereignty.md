# Review 01 — Marit Løvaas

**Reviewer:** Marit Løvaas, Professor of Information Systems
**Manuscript:** *Algorithmacy and Sovereignty: Toward a Coordinative Sovereignty for the Platform Era* (`chapter_v2.md`, 2026-08-14)
**Venue:** IGI Global, *Organizational Implications of Digital Sovereignty in the Age of AI*
**Date:** 2026-08-15
**Recommendation:** Minor revisions — at the strong end of minor. Four of my findings are one-paragraph fixes and the deadline permits all of them.

---

## 1. Who I am

I hold a chair in information systems at a Nordic business school, and my work for the last eight years has been on whether digital sovereignty can be measured at the level of a company rather than a state. I have written on GAIA-X's governance drift, on what sovereign-cloud procurement actually buys a public authority, and on the capability structure of European data spaces; with two colleagues I built and validated an instrument for organizational digital autonomy that a ministry now uses in its supplier-risk screening, and I sit on a national advisory board where the standing question arrives every month in the form of "should we mandate this in procurement." The volume's editor sent me this chapter because I am the reader it is aimed at. I opened it believing three things. Digital sovereignty is a capability construct with a regulatory component and an infrastructural one, and the second is not a footnote to the first. Most conceptual papers on digital sovereignty do not survive contact with an instrument. And the field is drowning in new sovereignty coinages, so a new one has to earn its place by predicting something the existing ones cannot register. The chapter converted me on the third point and irritated me on the second, which is roughly the outcome an author should want from me.

## 2. Step 0 — register and bar

The register is an impersonal academic chapter with named agents and active voice, and the manuscript holds it. I ran the scan I always run: `has been / have been / had been` returns exactly one hit in the body, in "has been the broker's, the dealer's, and the auctioneer's position for centuries," where the construction is correct and the agent is not being hidden. There is no first person. Agentless attribution of claims to the literature — the habit that makes most digital-sovereignty writing unreadable — is absent; Floridi, Repetto, Fratini, Cutolo and Kenney all appear as people who argue things. I will not spend findings on the masked self-citations or on the block quotations, which anonymization requires.

What I will judge hard: whether the second axis is real and measurable, whether §8's claim about how the field operationalizes digital sovereignty is true of the field I work in, whether §9's program contains the study that would test the chapter's own central claim, and whether anything here is usable by a person working procurement and sovereign cloud. What I will let pass: the political-theory apparatus in §6, which is not my competence and which the panel has better readers for; the formal machinery deferred to the companion, which the chapter is right to defer; and the OTA numbers, which belong to a reviewer who knows that literature.

## 3. Part 1 — the read

### Finding 1. §8 asserts independent variation and §9 never proposes to measure it. [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter claims.** §8 opens by separating "two phenomena that vary independently across firms," and the whole organizational payoff rests on that separation: a firm can be "high on the first and low on the second, impeccably compliant and strategically captured, and the two predict different outcomes." §9 then lays out four research directions, and the second builds a coordinative-sovereignty instrument, whose discriminant-validity target is Spreitzer's (1995) psychological empowerment.

**What fails.** Independent variation between two constructs is a correlation, and a correlation is estimated, not stipulated. The chapter names the strongest possible empirical claim in §8 and then designs a program in §9 that cannot return it. Direction two validates coordinative sovereignty against an individual-level construct from organizational behaviour and never proposes to administer a digital-sovereignty measure to the same firms. Direction three classifies mediators and measures response fit — useful, and about coordinative sovereignty alone. Direction four checks reported standing against modelled standing — again coordinative sovereignty alone. The chapter's own two-construct claim is nowhere in its four directions.

**Why it fails for me.** This is exactly the paper I would be asked to referee for an IS journal, and it is the point at which I would send it back. I have run the instrument that measures the first construct. If the author is right, my instrument and his should correlate weakly and predict different criteria, and that is a study I could run in a year on a panel of four hundred European SMEs. He has told me the result before designing the test, and then designed a different test. The Spreitzer choice compounds it: psychological empowerment is a felt state inside an organization one can leave, measured on individuals, while the chapter has just spent §6 arguing the bearer can be a firm. It is the wrong discriminant target for the organizational form of the construct, and the right one is sitting in §8's own opening sentence.

**Damage.** Severe for my readership and cheap to repair. As it stands a measurement-minded reader concludes the typology is a distinction the author does not intend to test. One paragraph closes it. See Revision B.

### Finding 2. §8's opening citations do not support the claim they carry, and two of them are about something else. [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter claims.** "Current research typically operationalizes organizational digital sovereignty as a single capability built from regulatory and infrastructural components (Fratini et al., 2024; Hummel et al., 2021; Schmuntzsch et al., 2025)."

**What fails.** I checked all three.

Fratini et al. (2024) is a systematic conceptual analysis of 271 peer-reviewed articles that yields four *models* — rights-based, market-oriented, centralisation, state-based — and evaluates them against the criterion of comprehensive regulation plus responsiveness to innovation. Its actors are states and its audience is policymakers. It operationalizes nothing, and it is not about organizations. (Verified against the Oxford Research Archive record and the Springer listing for *Digital Society*, 3(3), Art. 59.)

Hummel et al. (2021) reviewed 341 publications and mapped the frequency and connotations of *data* sovereignty, digital sovereignty and cyber sovereignty, proposing "a conceptual grid to systematize different dimensions and connotations." Its object is data sovereignty, its output is a grid of meanings, and it likewise operationalizes nothing at the organizational level. (Verified against the *Big Data & Society* record.)

Schmuntzsch, Shajek and Hartmann (2025) is the closest of the three, being an edited volume explicitly on companies and organizations. Its chapters, on the evidence of its table of contents, run to open-source strategy, edge computing, data trustees and usage control, trustworthiness in digitalization, and inter-organizational systems in care pathways. Whatever else that volume is, it is overwhelmingly *industrial*-strand work — the strand §2.3 declared out of scope four sections earlier.

So the chapter builds its straw man out of the material it excluded, and cites two sources that do not do the thing it accuses the field of doing.

**Why it fails for me.** I have no stake in defending a field-wide operationalization that does not exist. The honest version of the claim is stronger than the one printed: organizational digital sovereignty has no settled operationalization at all, the components that circulate are regulatory capacity and infrastructural control, and *neither component registers what an actor can require of a mediator it depends on*. That version I would cite. The version on the page invites the reply that the author has not read the measurement literature, and a reviewer less friendly than me will make it.

**Damage.** High, because it lands in the first sentence of the section written for my half of the readership, and because a misattributed opening claim colours everything after it. See Revision A.

### Finding 3. The diagnostic does not reach the industrial strand, and the chapter never says so. [FIX NOW for the scope statement; TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD for the full treatment]

**What the chapter claims.** §2.3 names the industrial strand in a single clause — "sovereign cloud, domestic capacity, data localization, procurement" — says the literacy frame "reaches it only partly," and announces that what follows engages the regulatory strand. I grepped the manuscript: *sovereign cloud*, *procurement*, *localization* and *jurisdiction and ownership* occur once each, all in that one sentence, and never again. GAIA-X, European data spaces, hyperscaler concentration, the CLOUD Act problem, and open-source substitution appear nowhere.

**What fails.** The exclusion is defensible as a scoping move and indefensible as a silence, because the reason for it is structurally interesting and the chapter does not notice. Run the chapter's own diagnostic on a cloud dependency and it returns nothing, because a firm buying compute and storage stands in a *dyad*: the supplier does not read the firm and a counterpart at once, and commits no determination about their relationship. The bypass test is defined over triads. Therefore the largest dependencies in the European digital-sovereignty debate — infrastructure supply, chips, hosting — fall outside the diagnostic's domain by construction, not by choice of emphasis.

**Why it fails for me.** Stated, this is a strength. It shows that the two sovereignties differ in their *domain of application* and not merely in their values, which is the sharpest possible answer to the question I came with: is coordinative sovereignty a second axis or platform dependence wearing a badge. Unstated, it reads as an author who did the easy half. A procurement officer who reaches §8 for guidance finds a decision procedure whose first question — is this mediator necessary or contingent — does not parse for the dependency she was worried about, and no sentence tells her why.

**Damage.** High for usability, moderate for credibility, and half of it is fixable in an afternoon. See Revision C. The full treatment — what coordinative sovereignty implies for procurement clauses, for data-space participation agreements, for sovereign-cloud tender design — is a second paper and I would read it.

### Finding 4. A volume "in the age of AI" whose chapter never runs its diagnostic on an AI dependency. [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter claims.** The title of the volume promises AI. The chapter's AI content is: the AI Act mentioned three times as a compliance instrument, an AI-literacy obligation in §9, and "sovereignty in the age of artificial intelligence" in the last paragraph. No foundation model, no model-as-a-service, no inference dependency.

**What fails.** The chapter has the best available example and does not use it. An organization that serves its customers through a bought general-purpose model holds both dependencies at once, and the diagnostic separates them cleanly. The compute and the weights are a supply relation, answerable by second-sourcing and by the industrial instruments. The model's refusals, safety filters and undisclosed version changes are a mediation: they read the deployer's instruction and the customer's request together and commit an output neither wrote, and the tie between deployer and customer — which is already open — does not reproduce them. Necessary mediator, on a coordination that half the volume's readers are building right now, and the answer is voice.

**Why it fails for me.** Two reasons, one honest and one political. Honest: it is the strongest demonstration in the chapter that the diagnostic is not a platform-economics result in sovereignty clothing, because it separates a supply dependency from a coordinative one inside a single vendor relationship — which is precisely what a firm cannot do with make-or-buy or with multihoming. Political: the editor commissioned an AI volume, and a chapter that mentions AI four times will be read as retrofitted. I would not want that said about a chapter I think is right.

**Damage.** Moderate on the argument, high on fit. One paragraph in §5.4 buys both. See Revision D.

### Finding 5. §6 separates competence from standing; §8 fuses them again. [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter claims.** §6's closing paragraph is careful: algorithmacy "governs exercise, not who is formally endowed with standing," an institution "can grant standing before the competence is widely held," and the two "have to be built together." §8 then writes: "The exposure is an algorithmacy gap, and the diagnostic gives managers a decision procedure for it," and two paragraphs later, "a firm's standing within its platform dependencies is a capability problem."

**What fails.** Standing is not a capability. §6 says so. A firm can hold world-class inferential, translational and temporal competence and hold no standing whatever, because standing is conferred by the terms of the coordination and by the institutions that hold those terms — which is the entire argument of §7. Calling the exposure "an algorithmacy gap" makes the remedy a training budget, and the chapter spends §7 arguing that training budgets are not the remedy.

**Why it fails for me.** This is the sentence a consultancy will quote. It converts a structural argument into a capability-maturity pitch, which is exactly the reduction the chapter exists to resist, and it does so in the section written for managers. It also weakens Finding 1's repair, because if standing is a capability then coordinative sovereignty is a capability construct like digital sovereignty and the two-construct claim gets thinner.

**Damage.** Moderate, sentence-level, and the fix improves the paragraph. See Revision E.

### Finding 6. One realized cell and one promissory cell. [FIX IF TIME]

§8 populates the high-DS/low-CS cell with the ISV and the marketplace seller, which is convincing and current. It populates the reverse with the platform worker under Directive (EU) 2024/2831 — and then concedes, correctly, that "no member state had fully transposed the directive as this chapter was written, so the standing it describes is a legislative commitment, not yet a present fact." The concession is honest and it costs the cell. The independent-variation claim therefore rests on one observed pairing and one anticipated one.

A second realized case for the reverse cell exists and sits in the chapter already: CoopCycle's member cooperatives, in §8's own closing paragraph, decide pay schemes and allocation rules on one-member-one-vote terms while owning almost none of the infrastructure they run on. That is high standing beside near-zero infrastructural control, observed, today. It is currently doing service in an argument about openness and control. Moving one sentence of it up into the independent-variation paragraph would populate the cell with a fact.

### Finding 7. §7's data-intermediary paragraph understates what the Omnibus does to it. [FIX IF TIME]

The chapter writes that the Data Governance Act "created a registered category of data intermediary, and the November 2025 Digital Omnibus proposal would repeal that Act and move the data-intermediation rules into the Data Act." Accurate. What it omits is the part that matters for the argument: under COM(2025) 837 the registration that constituted the category becomes *voluntary*, where the DGA made it mandatory at member-state level. A registered category whose registration is optional confers considerably less than the chapter's "the statutory vehicle is in motion while the category persists" implies. This is the one place where a data-spaces reader will catch the chapter being too kind to its own instrument, and one clause fixes it. (Verified against the Commission's proposal and secondary analyses; the author should confirm the article number in the consolidated Data Act text before printing.)

### Finding 8. Small precision items. [FIX IF TIME]

- §7 characterizes the Commission's DMA review as judging it "too early to extend the same obligation to social networking, on mixed evidence of demand." I read the report (COM(2026) 178 final, 28.4.2026). The chapter's first half is exactly right and the source is stronger than the chapter's paraphrase: the report states that alternative messaging services can now interoperate with WhatsApp and Facebook Messenger, "which has already led to new providers, including a European SME entering the market with innovative new functions." The second half softens in the chapter's own favour: the report says the external study "showed mixed evidence" *and* that "demand for such horizontal interoperability from both end and business users was limited." "Limited" is not "mixed." A chapter arguing that interoperability is a voice instrument against necessary mediators should not round a finding of limited demand into ambiguity.
- §2.3's "the literacy frame reaches it only partly" does not say which part. One clause would: the industrial strand's ownership and jurisdiction questions have no reading operation at their centre, so they are governed by title and by contract rather than by inspection.
- Repetto (2025) carries the same point in §1 and again in §6, the second time weakly ("counts accounts that recognize multiple coexisting digital sovereigns as innovations of the concept of sovereignty itself"). The §1 use, with the quotation, is the good one. Cut or repurpose the §6 sentence.
- §3.2 attributes to Fratini et al. (2024) the finding that "existing models sort sovereignty by the actor claiming it and the domain claimed." Fratini's four models sort by *normative orientation* — rights-based, market-oriented, centralisation, state-based. The actor-based coding is Repetto's. Swap the citation or restate the claim.

### Where the chapter is clean for me

§5.4's portfolio argument is the best thing in the manuscript and I have nothing to add to it. §3.3's competitive-bottleneck treatment is correct and correctly attributed to Armstrong. §5.3 handles Hirschman with more care than the platform literature usually manages, and the observation that retention "suppresses exit and activates nothing" is a real contribution to a distinction people quote loosely. §7's opening move — that the same instrument changes character depending on the mediator it meets — is the paragraph I would put in front of my advisory board.

## 4. Part 2 — style and slop audit

The prose is in good order and the v2 rewrite reads as a person writing. Passive-agent laundering is absent, the transitions are tail-to-head rather than filler, and the paragraph shapes vary. Three things stood out.

**Verbatim refrain.** "The standing gap named above" appears twice, in §6 and §8, and "the standing gap Ananny and Crawford name" sets it up in §3.2. Three sightings of one phrase in a fifteen-thousand-word chapter is a tic, and the third is the one that reads as a tag rather than a reference.
> *Rewrite (§8):* "Platform coordination breaks that assumption, because a legible object placed before an actor with no standing to act on it changes nothing."

**The verbless summary closer.** §8 closes three consecutive paragraphs on nominal fragments: "One firm, four kinds of mediator at once" (§5.4), "Dependence did not fall as the gate came open. It deepened" (§5.5), "High digital sovereignty beside low coordinative sovereignty, and an instrument that built a little of the second now proposed for withdrawal." Any one of them earns its place. Together they are a shape, and the third is the weakest, because it summarizes a paragraph the reader has just finished.
> *Rewrite:* "The Regulation attached a channel to a disclosure, and the simplification package would take it away."

**One clotted sentence.** §5.3: "Bypassability is the availability of exit, and necessity does not produce voice: it forecloses exit and leaves voice as the residual lever, one available against contingent third parties too and the only one left where the third party is necessary." Four clauses carrying three distinct claims, and the "one available against contingent third parties too" clause arrives as an afterthought about a case the sentence is not about.
> *Rewrite:* "Bypassability is the availability of exit. Necessity does not produce voice; it forecloses exit and leaves voice as the residue. Voice is available against a contingent mediator as well, and against a necessary one it is all there is."

I found no antithesis-machine constructions, no self-narrating rigor, and no metronomic short openers worth reporting.

## 5. Part 3 — paste-ready revisions

**Revision A — §8, replacing the first sentence.**

> Organizational digital sovereignty has no settled operationalization. The field's reviews sort its models rather than measure them: Fratini et al. (2024) code 271 articles into four models pursued largely by states, and Hummel et al. (2021) read 341 publications into a conceptual grid of the dimensions and connotations data sovereignty carries. Where the construct is applied to companies it is assembled from a regulatory component and an infrastructural one, compliance capacity beside control of infrastructure, data, and code (Schmuntzsch et al., 2025). Neither component registers what an actor can require of a mediator it depends on. The typology here separates two phenomena that vary independently across firms.

**Revision B — §9, a fifth direction, after the fourth.**

> The fifth tests the typology's central claim rather than assuming it. Section 8 holds that digital sovereignty and coordinative sovereignty vary independently across firms, and independence is an estimable quantity. Administering an organizational digital-sovereignty measure and a coordinative-sovereignty measure to the same firms returns a correlation, and the typology predicts a modest one: firms high on compliance capacity and infrastructural control should be spread across the range of standing within the coordinations they depend on. The two should also predict different criteria, regulatory-audit outcomes for the first and recovery from an unannounced change in a ranking or a platform policy for the second. A high correlation would collapse the typology into the single capability the field has assumed, which is the outcome that would refute the chapter.

**Revision C — §5, after the two questions are introduced (end of §5.1).**

> The diagnostic is defined over triadic coordinations, and not every dependency an organization carries is one. A firm buying compute, storage, or connectivity stands in a dyad: the supplier does not read the firm and a counterpart at once and commits no determination about the relationship between them. Concentration, switching costs, and jurisdiction can make such a dependency severe, and the instruments that answer it are the industrial ones named in section 2.3, portability and procurement and domestic capacity. The bypass test bears only where a supplier also stands between the firm and the parties it must reach, which is why the two dependencies call for different remedies and should not be pooled into a single sovereignty score.

**Revision D — §5.4, after the marketplace example.**

> A single vendor can hold both dependencies at once, which the current wave of model services shows plainly. An organization serving its customers through a bought general-purpose model depends on the provider for compute and weights, a supply relation answered by second-sourcing and by the industrial instruments. It depends on the same provider in a second way. The model's refusals, filters, and undisclosed version changes read the organization's instruction and its customer's request together and commit an output neither of them wrote, and the tie between organization and customer, which was never closed, does not reproduce them. In that function the provider is necessary and the response is voice: contestable determinations, terms the deployer has standing to hold, and channels through which deployers press collectively on model policy. Second-sourcing answers the first dependency and leaves the second untouched, which is the error the portfolio view exists to prevent.

**Revision E — §8, replacing "The exposure is an algorithmacy gap, and the diagnostic gives managers a decision procedure for it."**

> The exposure has two parts, and treating them as one wastes the remedy. The first is a competence gap, which a firm can close on its own account by building algorithmacy. The second is a standing gap, which no competence closes, because standing is conferred by the terms of the coordination and by the institutions that hold those terms in place. The diagnostic gives managers a decision procedure for the second and tells them what the first should be spent on.

**Revision F — §2.3, replacing "and the literacy frame reaches it only partly."**

> and the literacy frame reaches it only partly, because the questions at its centre — who holds title, whose law applies, who may be compelled to hand over what — are settled by ownership and jurisdiction rather than by inspecting anything.

## 6. Verdict

**Minor revisions.**

**The single most important fix.** Add the fifth research direction (Revision B). §8 makes an empirical claim about a correlation between two measures, and §9 must contain the study that estimates it. Everything else on my list is repair; this one is the difference between a typology and a distinction.

**The chapter's biggest genuine strength.** §5.4 and §7's opening, taken together. The claim that a platform is a portfolio of mediators and that the same instrument is an exit instrument against one function and a voice instrument against another is a real result, it is correct, and it does work no existing construct in my field does. I have watched a ministry spend two years on an interoperability mandate aimed at a function nobody wanted to leave. This chapter explains why that happens and how to avoid it, and that is worth the price of the sovereignty coinage.

**The one thing only the author can supply.** Whether the two constructs come apart in data. I can supply an instrument for the first; the second exists only in this chapter and its companion. Until someone measures both on the same firms, §8 is a hypothesis stated as a finding, and the author is the only person positioned to say what the expected correlation is and what would count as refutation. He should say it in the chapter, in a number, even a loose one.

## 7. Citation behavior

**Would I cite it? Yes, and I can name the argument.**

I have a paper in progress on why organizational digital-sovereignty scores fail to predict which firms absorb a platform shock. My finding is that firms scoring in the top quartile — sovereign cloud, in-house code, exemplary GDPR posture — get hurt just as badly by a marketplace ranking change as firms in the bottom quartile. I currently explain that with hand-waving about "different risk surfaces." This chapter gives me the explanation and the vocabulary for it: my instrument measures control over owned resources, and what predicts shock absorption is standing within a coordination the firm does not own. I will cite §8's two-construct separation as the conceptual source of my discriminant hypothesis, and §5.4 for why the exposure is function-specific rather than platform-specific. That is a real citation in a real argument, not a courtesy.

**Against what?** Against two targets. First, against the consultancy maturity models now circulating in European procurement, which score a supplier portfolio on ownership and transparency and return a single index. This chapter is the best short argument I know that such an index is measuring one thing and calling it sovereignty. Second, against my own side of the field — against the assumption, which I have made in print, that infrastructural control is the limiting constraint on organizational autonomy. §8's platform-work case, where infrastructure control is zero and standing is being legislated upward, is a counterexample I cannot answer, and I would cite it against myself.

**What I would not cite.** §2.3 and §8's opening sentence, in their current form, for the reasons in Findings 2 and 3. If a doctoral student cited §8's opening to me as evidence of how the field operationalizes digital sovereignty, I would send them to Fratini and Hummel to read what those papers actually do. With Revision A in place I would cite the sentence happily.

**What would have to change for me to cite it as a measurement paper rather than a conceptual one?** The fifth direction, and a stated expectation about the correlation. As it stands I cite it as theory and build the instrument myself.

**Would I assign it?** Yes, in my master's course on digital sovereignty and platform governance, in week nine, where students have already read Floridi (2020), Pohle and Thiel (2020), Couture and Toupin (2019) and Fratini et al. (2024) and are starting to think the word means everything. I would assign §1, §5 and §8 — about six thousand words — and set the seminar question as: *the chapter says these two sovereignties vary independently; design the study that would show it, and say what result would refute the chapter*. That question works as a seminar exercise precisely because the chapter does not answer it, which is a peculiar way for a chapter to be useful and a reason to fix Finding 1 anyway.

**One further note on citation, which the author will not like.** The formal apparatus lives in the companion, and the companion is the paper I will cite for the diagnostic once it appears under a name. This chapter's durable citation is the typology in §8, not the bypass test in §5. That is an argument for making §8 the strongest section in the chapter, and at present §5 is stronger.
