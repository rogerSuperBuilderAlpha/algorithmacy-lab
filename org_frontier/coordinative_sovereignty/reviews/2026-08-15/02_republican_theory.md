## 1. Who I am

I teach political theory and I have spent the last eight years writing a book about domination in economic
life — the argument that the neo-Roman conception of freedom does real work on labour markets and consumer
markets that neither the liberal nor the Marxian account does, and that it does that work only if we are
willing to name agents. Pettit and Skinner are my furniture; Cicerchia, Markell, Elizabeth Anderson,
Nedelsky, and the Mackenzie–Stoljar volume are the people I argue with in print. A citation alert put this
chapter in front of me because §6 cites four things I have written about, and I opened it expecting to be
irritated, because I have watched "sovereignty" get borrowed for a decade by people who want the word's
grandeur without its obligations. My prior, stated plainly: non-domination plus democratic inclusion already
names everything §6 describes, and *sovereignty* imports a picture of mastery that Patchen Markell spent a
book taking apart. The chapter meets me in §1 with Habermas's desubstantialized popular sovereignty, and I
grant that the move is competent. Surviving the concept-stretching charge is not the same as earning the
word, and the chapter has not yet noticed the difference.

I should say at the top that I found something here I did not expect: the necessary/contingent distinction
is a genuine contribution to my own literature, and I say so in Part 7. The theory section is where the
chapter is weakest, and the theory section is the part I can fix.

## 3. Part 1 — the read

Ranked by damage.

### F1 · §6 — Pettit (2016) does not license the bearer move. [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter claims.** "Republican theory has already licensed the move, extending non-domination to
corporate bodies while treating a corporate body's freedom as valuable for the sake of its members (Pettit,
2016)."

**What fails.** I read the paper. Pettit's corporate freedom-bearer is a *people* — "the body of individuals
over whom a state exercises coercive, territorial rule and in whose name it claims to speak and act" (EUI
p. 5) — and his two ideals are that states not dominate their own peoples and that peoples, as corporate
bodies, not be dominated by other agencies. Three things follow that the chapter's sentence gets wrong.

First, on the disvalue question Pettit says the opposite of what the chapter needs. At EUI p. 16: "whether or
not we think there is disvalue in the domination as such of a corporate body — for the record, I do not think
there is — we must recognize that there is disvalue in the domination of a free people." He denies that a
corporate body's domination is bad *as such*. The chapter cites him for a licence he explicitly withholds.

Second, the derivative value runs through a specific mechanism, and the mechanism does not transfer.
Pettit's argument (EUI p. 16) is that where the state is subject to another's arbitrary will, "citizens do
not effectively control the state," so the external power gets channelled into their lives. Corporate freedom
matters because the corporate body is one its members *control*. That is what makes it a people rather than
a firm. An ISV's forty employees do not control the ISV in anything like the way citizens control a
democratic state — and the chapter itself supplies the authority for saying so, twice, in E. Anderson (2017),
whose entire thesis is that the firm is a private government its members do not control. Anderson is cited
in §1 and again at the end of §6, in support of a construct whose bearer clause her book refutes.

Third, in Pettit's ledger the multinational corporation appears on the *dominator* side. The abstract asks
for a world in which no people is dominated by other agencies, "not by other states and not, for example, by
any international agency or multi-national corporation." The chapter takes an article that names firms as
paradigm dominators of corporate freedom-bearers and reads it as authorising firms to be freedom-bearers.

**Why it fails for me.** This is the sentence that installs the firm as a bearer of a domination-derived
construct, and it is the only authority offered. If it goes, the chapter's protagonists — the ISV, the
marketplace seller, the independent hotel, the SME — are standing on nothing but a stipulation.

**Damage.** High. Any republican reader stops at that sentence. The IS and management readers, who will not
check, will take it as settled and cite the chapter for it.

**What to do.** The fix is not to abandon the firm; it is to earn it on the correct condition. Pettit's
structure is available: corporate freedom is valuable *when and because* the corporate body is controlled by
its members. State it as a condition rather than a licence, concede that most firms fail it, and say what
follows — that a firm's coordinative sovereignty is worth having only to the extent its members hold it.
Paste-ready text in Part 3, R1.

### F2 · §6 — the four strands do not converge; two of them are attacks on the first. [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter claims.** "Four strands of political theory converge on the construct," followed by
republicanism, relational autonomy, Markell, and Ostrom/Schlager.

**What fails.** Markell (2008) is titled "The Insufficiency of Non-Domination" and it argues that Pettit's
principle "cannot serve as a single over-arching political ideal, because it responds to only one of two
important dimensions of concern about human agency." His positive term is *usurpation* — domination answers
to concerns about **control**, usurpation to concerns about **involvement**, and he wants both. The chapter's
"Markell (2008) adds a correction to non-domination, which protects without including" is in the right
neighbourhood and never names the concept, never signals that the paper is a sustained critique of the first
strand, and reports a dispute as an addition. Relational autonomy is in a related quarrel: Nedelsky and the
Mackenzie–Stoljar contributors are arguing against exactly the boundary-securing picture of the self that
republican non-domination shares with liberalism. Three of the four strands are in live disagreement, and
§6 stages them as four suppliers delivering four parts to one workshop.

The prose gives the assembly away. Four consecutive paragraphs open on the same frame: "From republican
theory it takes…", "Relational autonomy supplies the next piece", "Markell (2008) adds a correction",
"Ostrom (1990) supplies the institutional form." That is a catalogue, and a reader who knows the literature
reads the catalogue as a substitute for the argument that would have to reconcile the items.

**Why it fails for me.** Convergence is a strong evidential claim — four independent traditions arriving at
the same place is a reason to believe the place is real. Assembly is a weak one. The chapter claims the
former and performs the latter, and it does so in the one section where I am the audience.

**Damage.** High. §6 is the chapter's theoretical core and this is what a political theorist sees first.

**What to do.** Say that the strands disagree and that the disagreement is the point: the construct sits
where the dispute between control and involvement lands when the dominating party is an unexitable mediator.
That is a *stronger* claim than convergence and it costs one paragraph. R5.

### F3 · §6 — Cicerchia is doing the wrong job, and the right answer is already in a paper the chapter cites. [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter claims.** "A standing objection to that application remains: a ranking system is not an
agent, so its power may not be arbitrary in the sense republican theory requires. Cicerchia (2022) supplies
the answer, arguing that non-domination as usually stated has insufficient scope for structural domination
while an adequate account of structural domination still requires that agency be involved — and the interest
in a platform mediator is the firm's, and the firm is an agent."

**What I verified.** The gloss of Cicerchia's abstract is accurate. She holds that critics are right that
neo-republican non-domination "has an insufficient scope," and that an alternative account of structural
domination "should still support the neo-republican idea that agency must be involved." No misquotation
here, and I want that on the record.

**What fails.** The *use* inverts her. Cicerchia's paper is an argument that the neo-republican picture is too
narrow for capitalism's labour markets, where domination arises from competitive structure and class
position and no one intends it. The chapter takes the second half of her two-part claim — agency must be
involved — and uses it to walk back to the narrow agential picture: find the firm, point at it, objection
answered. The first half of her claim, the half that cuts against the chapter, disappears. And the
disappearance matters, because §3.3 has already made a structural argument. Armstrong's competitive
bottleneck is a *market-structure* claim: the platform extracts because of how single-homing and
multi-homing distribute across the two sides, not because anyone decided it should. So the chapter faces a
dilemma it never states. If the domination is agential — the firm tunes the system in its own interest, as
§3.3 also says — then plain Pettit suffices and Cicerchia is decoration. If it is structural, in the sense
§3.3's economics implies, then Cicerchia's *first* half bites and the chapter's answer is not available.

Worse, the answer the chapter wants is sitting in a paper it already cites. Pettit (2016), footnote 4 at EUI
p. 6: "Domination, as it is defined here, can exist only between agents, individual or corporate. But to
live under structures that facilitate domination by other agents — private or public domination, to
anticipate a distinction made later — is to be subject to structural domination (Pettit 2012, 2014b).
Relieving agential domination will often argue in the first place for combatting such structural
domination." That is precisely the platform case: the algorithmic system is a structure that facilitates the
firm's domination, and attacking the structure is how you relieve the agential domination. One footnote,
already in the bibliography, does the job Cicerchia is being made to do badly.

And Muldoon and Raekstad, cited in the preceding sentence, already make the move: their abstract says
certain algorithms "facilitate new relationships of domination by sustaining a socio-technical system in
which the owners and managers of a company dominate workers." The objection the chapter raises against them
has been answered by them.

**Damage.** High but cheap to repair. R2.

### F4 · §6 — Schlager and Ostrom misdescribed, and the error is load-bearing. [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter claims.** "Schlager and Ostrom (1992) separate the bundle of rights that make up
property, distinguishing the claimant and proprietor positions, which carry rights of management and
exclusion, from ownership proper, which carries the right of alienation."

**What fails, factually.** Claimants do not hold exclusion. I checked Table 1, "Bundles of Rights Associated
with Positions," reproduced in Ostrom's *Private and Common Property Rights* (2000, p. 340, sourced there to
E. Ostrom and Schlager 1996, p. 133). Authorized user: access, withdrawal, management. Claimant: access,
withdrawal, management. **Proprietor: access, withdrawal, management, exclusion.** Owner: all four plus
alienation. Ostrom's own text on the facing page: "'Proprietors' hold the same rights as claimants with the
addition of the right to determine who may access and harvest from a resource." Exclusion is the whole
difference between the two positions the chapter runs together.

**What fails, substantively — and this is the part that matters.** Exclusion is exactly the right the
platform-dependent actor does not have and cannot get. That is the definition of its situation: the platform
can exclude it, and it can exclude no one from anything. The Maine lobster gangs are proprietors in
Schlager and Ostrom's sense because they can and do enforce territorial boundaries against outsiders; their
de facto standing over grounds they do not own rests on a capacity to exclude, exercised without title. Take
exclusion away and they are claimants — and Schlager and Ostrom's whole finding is that the positions with
enforcement capacity are the ones that sustain governance.

So the chapter's conclusion — "not collective ownership of the platform but collective-choice arrangements
over the coordination's rules" — describes the *claimant* position, which is the weak one, while borrowing
the rhetorical force of *proprietorship*, which is the strong one, and it gets there by an error that
deletes the distinction. On Schlager and Ostrom's own schema, a marketplace seller is an authorized user.

There is a further problem the chapter half-sees. It concedes that a platform is "not an unowned pasture but
enclosed," then treats that as an adjustment rather than a difference in kind. Commons proprietorship is a
right held *over a resource* by a group that can police it, tolerated or recognised by an external authority.
What is the resource on a platform? Not the ranking system, which the seller cannot withdraw units from.
The analogy is doing more work than it can bear, and it is doing that work in a single paragraph.

**Damage.** High, and the factual half is unarguable. **R3** turns the error into an argument, which is the
best available outcome in fifteen days.

### F5 · §6, §7, §9 — the robustness condition is missing, and it is the difference between voice and permission. [FIX NOW]

**What the chapter claims.** §6: coordinative sovereignty's "currency is effective voice — terms that hold
because the coordinated actors have the standing to make them hold." §7: the common measure is "how much
effective voice does the institution confer." §7's table: oversight boards deliver "voice, bounded by the
constituting platform." §9's instrument: the standing dimensions are "freedom from the mediator's arbitrary
power, effective voice over the terms of mediation, and a share in the coordination's governance."

**What fails.** Republican freedom is modally demanding, and the chapter never says so. Pettit states the
condition in the very paper the chapter cites for the bearer move (EUI p. 3): republican theory takes the
yardstick of political progress to be "modally demanding benefits like freedom as non-domination," where
"modally demanding benefits require that they be robustly thus and so: that the actual benefit involved …
should continue to be available even under counterfactual circumstances where others turn indifferent or
hostile." Voice that a platform confers and can withdraw is not standing. It is the benevolent master's
open door — the paradigm case of domination surviving the absence of interference, which is the argument
§6's own first paragraph makes and then forgets.

The chapter's table concedes the point about oversight boards and then counts them anyway. A data trust
whose fiduciary can be defunded, a bargaining unit the platform can dissolve by reclassifying its members,
an appeal channel the platform can retire in a terms-of-service update — every row needs a second question,
and the chapter asks only the first.

**Why it fails for me.** Without an entrenchment criterion, §7 cannot distinguish a data trust from a
corporate ombudsman, §8's decision procedure cannot tell a manager which voice investment is worth making,
and §9's instrument will measure how heard an actor *feels*, which is a satisfaction measure. Adaptive
preference does the rest of the damage: a captive population that has resigned itself will report high
effective voice, and §5.3 already knows this ("a captive population may resign itself sooner than
complain") without carrying the knowledge into §9.

**Damage.** Highest of any single finding, because it runs through three sections and out into the
measurement program. It is also the cheapest structural fix in the chapter: one clause in the definition,
one column heading in the table, one item in §9. **R4.** This is my single most important fix.

### F6 · §1 vs §5.3, §7, §8 — the chapter uses Anderson to deny that exit answers domination, then builds a diagnostic that says exit answers domination. [FIX IF TIME]

§1 quotes E. Anderson (2017) to say the right to quit "has never been sufficient, because the worker who
quits arrives at another employer with the same powers." That is the republican commonplace and I endorse
it. §5.3 then defines the payoff: "Bypassability is the availability of exit, and necessity does not produce
voice." Careful, and correct. The care does not survive.

§7's table rates "Contestability of individual decisions" and "Collective bargaining" as **marginal** against
a contingent mediator. §8 instructs: "Where the firm depends on a contingent mediator, the move is toward
exit: support interoperability, build or back an alternative, break the lock-in." §10 repeats it: "where the
mediator is contingent the older sovereignty of exit still works."

For a republican, exit's availability changes the *leverage* voice can draw on. It does not make voice
marginal and it does not by itself secure freedom, because the actor who can leave is still, while it stays,
subject to a power it cannot contest — and it will stay, because switching is costly, which is what §5.3's
"involuntary retention" paragraph is about. Hirschman knew this and the chapter quotes him knowing it:
"exit does not reliably strengthen voice and can substitute for it." Then §7 marks contestability marginal
in the exact column where Hirschman's warning applies.

The repair does not require rebuilding the diagnostic, which I think is sound. It requires changing what the
diagnostic sorts: not exit-versus-voice, but which supports make voice effective. Against a contingent
mediator, exit is the *backing* that gives voice its force. Against a necessary one, the backing has to come
from institutions. That is one sentence in §5.3, two cell edits in §7's table, and one sentence in §8, and it
makes the chapter consistent with its own opening.

### F7 · §1 — right Berlin, wrong deployment, no locator. [FIX IF TIME]

"Berlin (1969) named the general shape of that error: a person does not become free to walk by ceasing to
want to walk."

The passage the chapter is reaching for is real and I verified it: "If I find that I am able to do little or
nothing of what I wish, I need only contract or extinguish my wishes, and I am made free," from the section
Berlin calls **The Retreat to the Inner Citadel**. The chapter neither names the argument nor gives a page,
and the paraphrase it substitutes is not Berlin's example.

Two problems beyond the locator. Berlin aims that argument at *positive* liberty — at the self-mastery and
self-realization traditions — and the construct the chapter is building is a positive-liberty construct: a
share in governing the arrangement that governs you. Deploying Berlin as its herald, unremarked, is an irony
a political theorist catches within a page. And Berlin's negative liberty would not classify the necessary
mediator's constraint as unfreedom at all, since for Berlin unfreedom is deliberate interference by others
and not the absence of a capacity.

The fix is short and it earns a citation that is currently doing nothing. Skinner (1998) sits in a
parenthesis in §6 and never speaks. Use him: the neo-Roman conception is a third term that Berlin's dichotomy
does not contain, which is why the chapter can take Berlin's warning about the inner citadel without taking
his framework. R6.

### F8 · §9 — the eyeball test, uncited, and the corporate analogue the chapter needs is in the same paper. [FIX NOW, trivial]

"Republican theory offers a usable test item in the form of the question whether an actor can look the
mediator in the eye and speak plainly, without needing to ingratiate or to avoid provoking a power that
could retaliate."

That is Pettit's **eyeball test**, taken without attribution. In the chapter's own Pettit (2016), EUI p. 13,
the formulation runs: interference must be reduced so "that the person can look others in the eye without
reason for fear or deference." Cite it.

The gift is on p. 15. §9 says the instrument "needs a form at each level, and their correspondence is itself
a question." Pettit supplies the corporate-level form: "The test I suggest here is a straight-talk test.
Different peoples enjoy freedom as non-domination in relation to others — and in relation to non-state
bodies — to the extent that their representatives can talk straight in dealing with one another. This test
is an analogue of the eyeball test." Individual bearer, eyeball test. Organizational bearer, straight-talk
test. The two-level instrument problem §9 poses has a two-level answer in the literature already, and the
chapter walks past it. R7.

### F9 · §2.1 and §10 — the chapter commits in §2.1 the concept-stretching it defends against in §1. [FIX NOW, cheap]

§2.1: "Within its range that sovereignty was real." §10: "Oracy held the oral order to account, literacy
held the literate state to account." §1 has just met Bodin head-on and defended the term by narrowing its
extension. Then §2 projects it backward onto pre-literate orders that had no such concept and could not have
had one — sovereignty is a sixteenth-century construction and Bodin, the chapter's own anchor, is where it is
constructed. A reader who accepted the §1 defence watches it undone one page later.

Cost of the fix: two words. Write "that authority was real" in §2.1, and in §10 "oracy sustained
accountability within the oral order." The arc survives intact; it stops claiming that oral societies had
sovereigns in the sense §1 spent four paragraphs securing.

### F10 · §1 and throughout — voice without a demos. [TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]

This is my real objection and I do not think fifteen days can answer it.

Habermas (1996) desubstantializes the *seat* of popular sovereignty. He does not detach it from a public of
citizens giving itself law. The passage at pp. 486–487 sits in the appendix essay "Popular Sovereignty as
Procedure," and its subject is legally institutionalized opinion- and will-formation in a constitutional
state: sovereignty withdraws into democratic procedures and the communicative presuppositions of their
exercise. The subjectlessness is the point *about the seat*. The demos is not dissolved; it is what the
procedures are procedures of.

Coordinative sovereignty has no demos. Its bearer is whoever occupies a relation, and the occupants of that
relation — an ISV, a hotel, a courier — are a class defined by a shared exposure, not a body that
constitutes itself, deliberates, or authors a rule. And the construct is wholly *constituted*: its holders
contest terms inside a coordination they never authored, and nothing in §6 or §7 gives them a constituent
moment. The tradition from Bodin through Rousseau to the constituent-power literature treats that moment as
what distinguishes sovereignty from every other kind of standing.

Which returns me to my prior. Strip the word and what remains is: non-domination (strand one) plus
involvement (Markell) under conditions of unexitability. That is a real and useful configuration and it is
not sovereignty. My honest reading is that "sovereignty" is here because the volume's call is about
sovereignty. I do not think that is fatal — the chapter is a chapter in that volume — but the OT submission
will meet a reader who says it out loud, and the answer has to be an argument about constituent power, not
a further citation of Habermas's appendix. Carry forward.

### F11 · §6 — Markell's book on sovereignty-as-mastery is the missing citation. [TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]

The chapter cites Markell (2008) and not *Bound by Recognition* (2003), which is the sustained argument
against the picture of agency as sovereign mastery. A chapter whose title word is *sovereignty*, citing
Markell for something else, invites the question of whether the author knows the book. Markell's engagement
with the *dominium*/*imperium* distinction in the 2008 article is likewise directly relevant to a chapter
about private power exercising public-like authority, and it goes unused. Both are more than a fifteen-day
fix; they are how the OT version opens.

### F12 · §6, §8 — a firm's coordinative sovereignty can be bought by dominating the people inside it. [TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]

Republican freedom does not aggregate. An ISV that wins standing over App Store ranking may fund it by
squeezing its own developers; a hotel that wins standing against an OTA may pass the cost to housekeeping
staff. §8's capability agenda — train, budget, and audit algorithmacy the way compliance is audited —
plausibly encourages the transfer, because the firms that can afford the competence are the ones §4 already
identifies as advantaged. §6's bearer clause gestures at this ("where the two diverge, the members' standing
governs") and then discharges it in half a sentence. The chapter has no account of what happens when
coordinative sovereignty at one level is purchased at another, and Nkemelu on this panel will press the same
point from the labour side. Carry forward; it is a section of the OT paper, not a paragraph here.

### F13 · §1, §5.2, §5.3, §9 — adaptive preference appears four times, unnamed. [FIX IF TIME]

Berlin's inner citadel in §1. Hahl et al.'s brokered parties who do not perceive the gap between them and so
do not try to close it, §5.2. "A captive population may resign itself sooner than complain," §5.3.
"Self-reported competence invites over-confidence," §9. Four appearances of one phenomenon, and no name for

reason for the behavioural anchor §9 already proposes and currently justifies only by over-confidence.

### F14 · Key Terms — the non-domination entry switches Pettits without saying so. [FIX IF TIME]

"**Non-domination:** The republican conception of freedom as the absence of another's arbitrary power…. A
coordinated actor is dominated to the degree the mediator's power over it is unconstrained by rules the
actor can contest."

The first sentence is Pettit (1997), arbitrariness. The second is the later formulation, where the test is
whether interference is subject to the interferee's control and contestation is the mechanism. The chapter
cites only 1997. The shift is not a defect — the later formulation is *better* for this argument, since a
ranking system that no one controls is a cleaner case of uncontrolled interference than of arbitrary will —
but it should be made rather than slid into. Add Pettit (2012), *On the People's Terms*, and say which
formulation §6 runs on.

## 4. Part 2 — style and slop audit

Register-aware. I am not complaining about impersonality or about the block quotations.

**(a) The catalogue frame in §6.** Four paragraph openers on one template: "From republican theory it
takes…" / "Relational autonomy supplies the next piece." / "Markell (2008) adds a correction…" / "Ostrom
(1990) supplies the institutional form." Failure type: metronomic openers doing the work of an argument. The
shape *is* the finding in F2 — a reader hears assembly before they can name why. Rewrite in R5.

**(b) §6: "Where the two diverge, the members' standing governs — a position taken here, and one a reader may
decline while keeping the rest."** Failure type: self-narrating rigor, exactly the tic the house style
proscribes, and here it is also an evasion. A reader cannot decline it while keeping the rest, because if
members' standing governs then the firm's coordinative sovereignty is derivative and "the construct is one"
is false. Rewrite: *If the two diverge, the members' standing governs, and a firm's coordinative sovereignty
is worth defending to the extent that it secures theirs.*

**(c) §6: "Where the mediator is necessary and only voice remains, autonomy has to be reconceived."**
Failure type: overclaim plus an agentless modal, and it is refuted by the paragraph three lines below, which
cites four traditions that already conceived it. Rewrite: *Where the mediator is necessary and only voice
remains, the received idea of sovereignty as independence has nothing to offer, and republican theory has
been working the alternative for thirty years without naming its platform case.*

**(d) §6: "Coordinative sovereignty is more than worker rights or consumer protection, though it bears on
both."** Failure type: the antithesis engine with a concessive tail. The real distinction arrives in the next
sentence and is good ("Rights and protections are guarantees against a mediator's conduct; coordinative
sovereignty is a share in its governance"). Cut the opener and lead with the distinction.

**(e) §6 closer: "The mediator cannot be wished away, and freedom lives in the terms of the mediation."** and
**§10 closer: "Neither reading alone nor control alone secures it."** Failure type: empty aphoristic closers.
The first is a restatement of the section title; the second is a symmetry. §10 has a better last line already
sitting two sentences earlier — the hotels who "came out of it more dependent than they went in."

**(f) §6: "Markell (2008) adds a correction to non-domination, which protects without including."** Failure
type: a slogan standing in for a concept. *Including* is not Markell's word; *involvement* is, and
*usurpation* is the term for its absence. Handled in R5.

**(g) Credit where due.** §5.3's "Hirschman's loyalty kept the voice-prone from leaving and thereby activated
voice. Retention suppresses exit and activates nothing." That is a real distinction, compressed, landing on a
short sentence after a longer one, and it is the kind of sentence I would read aloud in a seminar. §6's
"worker ownership can change who sets a necessary mediator's rules and cannot make it leaveable" is the
second. Whatever else the v2 rewrite did, it did not flatten these.

## 5. Part 3 — paste-ready revisions

All written in the chapter's register: third person, named agents, APA 7.

**R1 — replaces the bearer paragraph in §6** ("Who bears this standing…" through "…as section 9 takes up.")

> Who bears this standing, a person or a firm? The construct is defined by a relation, so whoever occupies
> the position of being coordinated through a mediation it cannot leave is a candidate bearer. Republican
> theory sets the condition on which a corporate bearer counts. Pettit (2016) extends freedom as
> non-domination to corporate bodies and denies that the domination of a corporate body carries disvalue in
> itself; what carries the disvalue is the effect on members, since a body subject to another's arbitrary
> will cannot be controlled by the people in whose name it acts, and the external power is channelled into
> their lives. Corporate standing therefore matters in proportion to the members' control over the body that
> holds it. Most firms satisfy that condition weakly, and E. Anderson (2017) explains why: the firm governs
> its members rather than answering to them. A firm's coordinative sovereignty is accordingly worth defending
> to the degree that it secures the standing of the people who make it up, and where the two diverge the
> members' standing governs. The construct is one and the bearer is layered, which is why section 9 asks for
> an instrument at each level and treats their correspondence as an open question.

**R2 — replaces the Cicerchia sentences in §6**

> A standing objection meets that application: a ranking system is not an agent, so its power may not be
> arbitrary in the sense republican theory requires. Pettit (2016) answers it directly, holding that
> domination obtains between agents while a structure that facilitates one agent's domination of another is
> itself a form of structural domination, and that relieving the agential form ordinarily requires attacking
> the structural one. The platform case has both. The interest the system carries is the firm's, and the firm
> is an agent, which is where section 3 located it; the system is the structure through which that interest
> reaches the coordinated actor. Cicerchia (2022) marks the limit of the agential account, arguing that
> non-domination as usually stated has insufficient scope for the structural domination a labour market
> produces without anyone intending it, and the competitive bottleneck of section 3.3 is a structural
> condition of exactly that kind. The two levels are not alternatives, and an account of the platform
> mediator needs both.

*(Note to the author: if you keep Cicerchia, this is the only use of her that does not invert her argument.
The safer alternative is to cut her from §6 and cite Pettit's footnote alone; but her first half is doing
real work on §3.3's economics and the chapter is stronger for keeping it.)*

**R3 — replaces the Schlager and Ostrom passage in §6**

> The commons literature has worked out what remains available under exactly that condition. Schlager and
> Ostrom (1992) decompose property into access, withdrawal, management, exclusion, and alienation, and array
> the positions that hold different bundles: an authorized user holds access and withdrawal, a claimant adds
> management, a proprietor adds exclusion, and an owner adds alienation. Their Maine lobster fishers hold de
> facto proprietorship over grounds the state owns, governing access and effort without holding title,
> because they can enforce a boundary against outsiders. The schema locates the platform-dependent actor
> precisely, and the location is uncomfortable: it holds access and withdrawal and nothing above them, while
> the proprietor's right of exclusion sits entirely with the mediator, which excludes at will and cannot be
> excluded from anything. The available claim is therefore the management right — collective-choice
> arrangements over the coordination's rules — and the commons record shows what that right requires to
> hold. Ostrom (1990) makes external recognition a design principle in its own right, since a group's rules
> survive only where an outside authority declines to override them. Section 7 reads the European
> instruments in that light: they are the external recognition without which a collective-choice claim over a
> platform's rules is a request.

*(This corrects the exclusion error, converts it into an argument, and gives §7 a reason to exist that it
currently lacks. Ostrom's seventh design principle, minimal recognition of the right to organize, is
verified.)*

**R4 — the robustness condition, three insertions**

*(i) §6, appended to the definition paragraph:*

> Voice of that kind has to be robust to count. Republican freedom is modally demanding: a benefit secures
> freedom only where it survives the counterfactual in which the other party turns indifferent or hostile
> (Pettit, 2016). Standing a mediator confers and can withdraw at will fails that test however generous it
> is while it lasts, which is why an entrenched channel with narrow powers can be worth more than a broad
> one held at the platform's pleasure.

*(ii) §7, replacing the sentence beginning "Across these forms the common measure is…":*

> Two questions sort these forms rather than one. How much effective voice does the institution confer, and
> how far is that voice entrenched against the mediator's unilateral withdrawal? An oversight board
> constituted by the platform scores well on the first and poorly on the second; a statutory contestation
> right scores the other way. Cumulative standing is what a stack of institutions confers on both measures at
> once, and the diagnostic tells a policymaker where to put each one.

*(iii) §9, appended to the second research direction:*

> The instrument also needs an entrenchment item, since a channel the mediator can close is not standing.
> Asking how a respondent's voice would fare if the mediator wished to be rid of it separates secured
> standing from tolerated access, and it guards against the resignation that a captive population reports as
> satisfaction.

**R5 — replaces the strand-frame opening of §6**

> Political theory does not converge on this construct so much as leave a space for it. Republican theory
> supplies the first term, freedom as non-domination, the absence of another's arbitrary power and a stronger
> condition than the absence of interference (Pettit, 1997; Skinner, 1998); an actor can be left alone and
> still unfree if it lives at the mercy of a power that could interfere at will on terms it cannot contest,
> which is the condition of the platform-dependent actor. Markell (2008) argues that the term is
> insufficient on its own, since domination answers to a concern about control and leaves a second concern
> untouched, the concern with involvement, whose violation he calls usurpation: an actor secured against
> arbitrary power may still be displaced from any part in the determinations that govern it. Relational
> autonomy presses a related objection from another direction, holding that autonomy is constituted through
> the relationships that structure a person rather than secured by drawing a boundary around one (Mackenzie
> & Stoljar, 2000; Nedelsky, 2011), so that a relationship can enlarge autonomy or shrink it and the shape
> is what matters. Coordinative sovereignty is what the dispute yields when the dominating party is a
> mediator the actor cannot leave: control and involvement come apart there more sharply than in the cases
> the dispute was argued over, because protection against a mediator's worst conduct is exactly what a
> platform can grant while conceding nothing about who authors the coordination.

**R6 — replaces the Berlin sentence in §1**

> Berlin (1969, pp. 135–141) named the general shape of that error in what he called the retreat to the
> inner citadel: "If I find that I am able to do little or nothing of what I wish, I need only contract or
> extinguish my wishes, and I am made free." He aimed the charge at the positive conception of liberty, and
> the neo-Roman conception this chapter draws on is neither of the two he distinguished (Skinner, 1998). It
> takes the warning without the framework. An actor that withdraws its demands from the terms of a mediation
> it cannot escape has not secured its autonomy; it has abandoned the field on which its autonomy would be
> exercised.

*(**Page numbers left as a placeholder — the author must confirm the pagination in the edition cited.** The
quotation itself I verified.)*

**R7 — replaces the test-item sentence in §9**

> Republican theory supplies a usable item at each level. Pettit's eyeball test asks whether a person can
> look another in the eye without reason for fear or deference (Pettit, 2016), which for an individual bearer
> becomes the question whether an actor can deal with the mediator plainly without needing to ingratiate or
> to avoid provoking a power that could retaliate. Pettit offers a corporate analogue in the straight-talk
> test, which asks whether a body's representatives can talk straight in dealing with another body, and that
> is the organizational form of the item. The two tests give the instrument its two levels, and whether they
> move together in the same firm is the correspondence question.

## 6. Part 4 — verdict

**Major revisions.** That word overstates the labour and understates the problem, so let me be precise: four
of the five [FIX NOW] findings are sentence- or paragraph-level and every one has paste-ready text above, so
the chapter is reachable by 30 August. It is *major* because §6 as written will not survive a political
theorist reading it with the sources open, and §6 is the section the title promises.

**The single most important fix:** the robustness condition (F5, R4). Without it, "effective voice" is
compatible with everything the chapter is against, and the fix propagates correctly into §7's table and §9's
instrument. Three insertions, under two hundred words.

**The chapter's biggest genuine strength:** the necessary/contingent distinction and the edge-versus-node
formulation of the bypass test. I do not have this in my literature and I want it. Republican theory has
been arguing about whether exit answers domination for twenty years without a way to say *which* dominating
relations exit could touch even in principle, and §5.2 gives me one. The franchised-dealer case is clean; the
App Store case is cleaner. Whatever happens to the sovereignty framing, that section should be preserved
intact.

**The one thing only the author can supply:** an argument that the word *sovereignty* survives the absence
of a demos. Not another citation of Habermas's appendix — an argument about constituent power, or an explicit
concession that the term is being used for a standing that is wholly constituted and why that is
nevertheless the right word. Everything else on my list, another reviewer could have written. That one is the
author's to answer or to concede, and I would respect the concession more than a further defence.

## 7. Part 5 — citation behavior

**Would I cite this chapter? Yes — for one thing, and not the thing it is named after.**

The book I am finishing has a chapter arguing against what I call the exit consensus: the position, shared
across labour law, competition policy, and a good deal of liberal political theory, that domination in
economic life is answered by making the dominated party able to leave. My case against it has been the
Anderson case — that the departing worker arrives at another employer with the same powers — and the
weakness of that case is that it is an empirical generalization dressed as an argument. Someone can always
reply that a *sufficiently* competitive market would answer it. Section 5.2 gives me a structural reply. Some
mediators occupy their position because a rule forbids the parties to reach each other; others occupy it
because of integrating work no direct tie reproduces. Against the first the exit consensus is right and its
remedy is available. Against the second the consensus is not merely empirically disappointed; it is
incoherent, because the exit it prescribes is the dissolution of the coordination the parties need. That is
the argument I have wanted and could not construct, and I would cite this chapter for it by section.

**Against what, specifically.** Two targets. First, the exit consensus as above — I would use the App Store
node/edge divergence as the worked case, because it shows that a mediator can be non-substitutable and
bypassable at once, which is the possibility the consensus cannot represent. Second, and this will make the
authors uncomfortable, I would cite §6 *against* the labour-republican literature that treats worker
ownership as the terminus of the argument. The sentence "worker ownership can change who sets a necessary
mediator's rules and cannot make it leaveable" is the best sentence in the chapter and it names a limit that
Muldoon and Raekstad and the platform-cooperativism literature have not confronted. I would quote it
verbatim.

**What I would not cite it for.** *Coordinative sovereignty*, as a term. I would cite the mechanism and name
it something else in my own text — most likely "standing under unexitable mediation," which is uglier and
carries no debts. For the reasons in F10: the construct has no demos and no constituent moment, and I do not
want to spend my own credibility defending someone else's borrowed word. I would say so in a footnote,
courteously, and the footnote would be the kind that gets quoted back at the authors.

**What would change that.** One of two things. Either an argument that a class of similarly exposed actors
becomes a demos through the institutions §7 describes — that a bargaining unit or a data trust is a
constituent act and not merely a channel, which is a real argument and I think a defensible one — or an
explicit decision to treat "sovereignty" as a family-resemblance term with the Habermas appendix as the
warrant and no claim to constituent power. The second is honest and cheap and available by 30 August. The
first is a paper.

**Would I assign it?** Yes, and specifically. In my graduate seminar on domination and markets I would assign
§3 and §5 in the week on structural versus agential domination, paired with Cicerchia (2022) and Muldoon and
Raekstad (2023), and the seminar question would be whether the platform mediator is a case of one, the other,
or both — which is F3 turned into a two-hour discussion. I would assign §6 in a different week and for a
different purpose: as an exercise in reading a convergence claim against its own sources. Students would be
sent to Markell's title and asked whether a paper called "The Insufficiency of Non-Domination" can be a
strand converging with non-domination. That is a pedagogically excellent use of a section and not one the
authors will enjoy hearing about, which is why the fixes in Part 3 are worth making before it becomes the
section's reputation.

I would not assign §7 or §8; those are a policy brief and a managerial memo respectively, and my students
are not the audience.
