## 1. Who I am

I practise competition and platform law in Brussels, mostly on the defending side of gatekeeper
compliance files and, increasingly, on the complaining side for business users who cannot get an
answer out of a compliance dialogue they are not party to. I teach a seminar on platform regulation
at a law faculty two days a month and I write case notes, which means I have spent the past four
years watching interdisciplinary work cite the DMA as though it were a slogan. Someone forwarded me
this chapter because the abstract promised a test that sorts intermediaries a firm can leave from
intermediaries it can only govern, and that is a question I get asked by clients in almost those
words. I came to it expecting the usual: instruments named correctly and understood loosely, a
recital mistaken for an obligation, a pending case cited as though decided. What I found is better
than that — the author has read the instruments and the numbers on the page are, with the exceptions
catalogued below, right. The chapter's problem is not that it invents law. It is that it states law
at the level of generality at which the interesting exceptions live, and that in three places the
record has moved underneath it since drafting.

Two things I believe before reading anything. First, that the interesting question about European
platform regulation is never what an instrument says but who has standing to make it bite, which is
this chapter's question, so I am predisposed to want it to work. Second, that a legal claim printed
in a book without a date attached is a claim that will be wrong within eighteen months, and that
authors who write "the Commission has proposed" are writing a sentence with a shelf life. Section 8
does this repeatedly.

---

## 2. Step 0 — register and bar

Impersonal academic chapter, no first person, named agents, APA 7. Confirmed. The masked
"Author, 2026" self-citations and the block quotations are anonymization artefacts and I do not
count them. The chapter is not a law review article and I will not judge it as one: it does not owe
me pinpoint citations to every recital, it does not owe me the national implementing measures, and
it is entitled to compress doctrine that is not doing analytical work.

What I judge hard: any sentence that states what an instrument does, what a court held, or what a
number measures. Those either match the record or they do not, and a chapter that is going to be
read by policy people — §7 and §8 are addressed to exactly them — carries a higher duty on those
sentences than on its theory. I also judge hard the *direction* of a legal claim: a right that runs
to natural persons is not a right that runs to firms, and a chapter whose whole argument turns on the
difference between a dependent person and a dependent firm cannot be casual about which is which.

What I let pass: the theoretical apparatus, the Selznick and Hirschman and Pettit machinery, the
node/edge distinction as a formal matter, the OTA share series (reviewer 08's home turf), and the
question whether "sovereignty" is the right word. Also the absence of the companion paper's formal
results, per the brief.

**CONTRADICTED**

1. **§5.5 — "not ancillary restraints and so fall within the prohibition on anticompetitive
   agreements."** The consequence does not follow and the Court did not say it. Failing the
   ancillary-restraints gateway means only that the clause must be assessed on its own terms under
   Article 101(1); it may still escape as non-restrictive by object or effect, and it may still be
   exempted under Article 101(3). The Court says so expressly at para. 55: the objective-necessity
   condition "does not require a balancing of the procompetitive and anticompetitive effects of an
   agreement. It is only in the context of Article 101(3) TFEU that that balancing may take place."
   The Court's own press release puts the holding as: price parity clauses "cannot, in principle, be
   classified as 'ancillary restraints'." The chapter has converted a lost safe harbour into a
   finding of illegality. Any competition lawyer reading §5.5 stops here. **[FIX NOW]**

2. **§3.2 and §7 — what Article 22 GDPR gives.** Both sections say Article 22 "gives a natural
   person subject to a solely automated decision the right to obtain human intervention and to
   contest the outcome." Article 22(1) does not grant a right to contest; it states a prohibition in
   principle — "the data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely
   on automated processing" — which the Court has confirmed operates as a prohibition rather than a
   right to be invoked (C-634/21 *SCHUFA Holding (Scoring)*, 7 December 2023). The rights to human
   intervention, to express a point of view and to contest live in Article 22(3), and by its own
   terms Article 22(3) applies only "in the cases referred to in points (a) and (c) of paragraph 2"
   — contract and explicit consent. Where the decision is authorised by Union or Member State law
   under Article 22(2)(b), the safeguards are whatever that law supplies. This matters for the
   chapter's own argument: the case-level standing it claims is "already in hard law" is standing
   that attaches to two of three legal bases. **[FIX NOW]** — two clauses.

3. **§5.2 and §7 — essential facilities and the duplication test.** §5.2 says the doctrine "asks
   whether an excluded rival could viably duplicate the facility it seeks access to"; §7 says
   "competition law has long understood [interoperability] as one under the essential-facilities
   logic it applies to infrastructure that cannot be duplicated." That was the law until 25 February
   2025. In Case C-233/23, *Alphabet and Others* (Android Auto), the Court held that the *Bronner*
   indispensability criterion does **not** apply to a refusal of interoperability where the platform
   was developed to be open to third-party complementors, and that such a refusal may be abusive
   even where the platform is not indispensable to the applicant's commercial viability. The
   chapter is describing a doctrine the Court has just narrowed, in the platform setting, in the
   direction the chapter wants. This is the most damaging of the three because it is also the
   biggest missed opportunity: the Court has drawn something very close to the chapter's own
   necessary/contingent line — a facility built for one's own use versus a platform built to be
   coordinated through — and the chapter does not know it. **[FIX NOW]**

4. **§8 — "Enforcement has reached platform conduct without reaching that layer."** On 23 July 2026
   the Commission adopted two DMA non-compliance decisions against Google totalling €890 million:
   €460 million under Article 6(5) for preferential treatment of Google's own shopping, hotel,
   transport and sports results in Search ranking, and €430 million under Article 5(4) for Play
   Store anti-steering. The first of those is enforcement reaching the ranking layer. The sentence
   as written is now false on its face; the defensible version is narrower — enforcement reaches the
   ranking of the gatekeeper's own services against rivals, and still gives the ranked business user
   no standing over the determination that ranked *it*. That narrower claim is the chapter's real
   claim and it survives. **[FIX NOW]**

5. **§5.2 and the App Store demonstration — "the gate's force disappears."** The chapter says that
   restoring the developer-to-user tie for payment, by sideloading or a link out, makes the gate's
   force disappear "because it never rested on integrating work," and calls the DMA's anti-steering
   obligations "the edge test run by a regulator." The regulator ran it. In June 2025 Apple
   restructured its EU terms to comply with the Article 5(4) decision, merging link-outs, web views
   and alternative payment processing into a single entitlement priced at an initial-acquisition fee
   plus a Store Services fee plus a Core Technology Commission on external purchases. The tie was
   restored and the toll moved onto it. This is not a quibble — it is the strongest available
   objection to the bypass test as a *strategy* device, and the chapter would be far more
   interesting for meeting it. **[FIX NOW as a qualification; TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD as the
   theoretical problem]** See §3.4.

6. **§7 — collective bargaining by "platform workers or platform-dependent firms."** The sentence
   puts these two on the same footing. Union competition law puts them on opposite sides of a line.
   The Commission's *Guidelines on the application of Union competition law to collective agreements

### 3.4 §5.2 — the bypass test meets an actual regulator

The App Store demonstration is the chapter's proof that node and edge diverge, and it is a good one.
It is also the place where a lawyer's knowledge does the most damage, because the counterfactual the
chapter runs in the abstract has since been run in the world, and the result is not the one on the
page.

The chapter's claim: delete the payment gate as a node and no other rail practicably reaches those
users, so every node test returns necessary; restore the developer-to-user tie for payment and "the
gate's force disappears, because it never rested on integrating work." Then: "The Digital Markets
Act's anti-steering obligations are the edge test run by a regulator."

Article 5(4) is indeed an edge instrument — it forbids the gatekeeper from preventing business users
from communicating offers and concluding contracts outside the core platform service. The Commission
enforced it against Apple in April 2025. Apple complied in June 2025 by permitting the link-out and
attaching a fee schedule to it: an initial-acquisition fee, a Store Services fee, and a Core
Technology Commission levied on purchases made outside the store. The direct tie exists. The gate's
force did not disappear; it was repriced onto the restored tie.

Why this is more than an update. The chapter's binary asks whether a mediator's position "rested on
the absence of the direct tie." Apple's position rested on the absence of the tie *and* on control of
the installed base, and the second survives the first. A gate that can levy a toll on traffic it no
longer routes is not contingent in the chapter's sense and not necessary either: it is a mediator
whose integrating work is zero and whose extraction is undiminished by the bypass. The four-way
taxonomy has no cell for it, and "partial mediator" does not fit, because partial is defined as real
integrating work alongside a bypassable gate, and there is no integrating work here.

I do not think this can be fixed in fifteen days, and I do not think it should be papered over. The
honest move is a short paragraph conceding that opening the edge does not by itself dissolve the
mediator's extraction where the mediator retains a separate lever — control of the device, of the
account, of the installed base — and flagging the question of what a restored tie is worth when the
mediator can price access to it. That paragraph makes the chapter more credible with the people who
have watched this file, and it hands the companion paper a live question.
**[FIX NOW: the qualification. TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD: the taxonomy gap.]**

Second point on this section. "The neighbouring tests cannot ask the second question, and the reason
is not oversight." I accept the argument for *Bronner*-era essential facilities. After *Android
Auto*, it is less clean. The Court in C-233/23 held that where a dominant undertaking has developed a
platform for third-party use, refusal to make it interoperable can be abusive without proof that the
platform is indispensable — that is, the Court declined to hold the network of who may deal with
whom fixed, and reasoned instead about what the platform was built to do. It did not run the
chapter's edge test, but it stopped running the pure node test, and a reader who knows the case will
feel the chapter's "each holds the network fixed" claim as slightly stale. Adding the case costs one
sentence and buys the chapter a court moving in its direction. **[FIX NOW]**

### 3.6 §5.5 — the OTA case as a lawyer reads it

The tourism economics is reviewer 08's. Three legal points.

The C-264/23 characterisation is wrong (ledger item 1) and this is the sentence that will be quoted
against the chapter, because it sits in the chapter's best empirical section and states a holding
about a very well-known case. Fix it exactly.

The ECN figure needs the word "basic" (ledger item 8), and it needs a date on its face. It is a 2017
report on a 2016 exercise across ten member states, one of which was the United Kingdom. Printing
"which the European Competition Network's monitoring exercise put at" in an undated present tense,
in a volume that will appear in 2027, is the publication-lag problem in miniature. There is a more
recent source: the Commission's *Market study on the distribution of hotel accommodation in the EU*
(DG COMP, 2022), which reports data to 2021, distinguishes basic from effective commission, and
reports that chains' effective rates run approximately [0-10] percentage points above their basic
rates. Its figures are published in redacted bands, so it does not beat the ECN number for
precision, but it does mean the chapter can write "the range has been stable across the ECN's 2016
exercise and the Commission's 2022 market study," which is a claim that ages instead of expiring.
**[FIX IF TIME]** — worth twenty minutes.

Third: the chapter says the 2024 judgment "sets up a test a later study can run." It does more than
that. Booking's DMA designation in May 2024 brought Article 5(3), which prohibits parity obligations
outright for gatekeepers, and the DMA review of April 2026 found the regime fit for purpose without
amendment. So the parity gate is now closed twice over, by judgment and by ex ante obligation, and
the chapter's prediction — that dependence will not fall because only a contingent function was

### 3.7 §7 — the instrument table

The brief asks whether the rows survive a lawyer's reading. As doctrine, no. As strategy, mostly yes.
The problem is the column headings, which promise doctrine.

"Against a contingent mediator / Against a necessary mediator" reads as a claim about what the
instrument *is* in each case. But no instrument in that table changes its legal character with the
mediator's type. Article 22 GDPR applies to a solely automated decision with significant effects
whether or not the controller is bypassable. P2B Article 11 complaint-handling applies to providers
of online intermediation services above the micro-enterprise threshold, full stop. What changes is
the *value to the dependent actor* of exercising the instrument. Rename the columns —
"Value where exit is available / Value where exit is foreclosed" — and every row becomes defensible
and the table stops making a claim it cannot support. That is a one-line fix that removes my main
structural objection. **[FIX NOW]**

Three rows, specifically.

*Contestability of individual decisions — "marginal" against a contingent mediator.* Wrong even as
strategy. Case-level contestation is how a dependent actor generates the evidence that the mediator
is behaving arbitrarily, and that evidence is what makes an exit campaign — a complaint to a
national authority, a P2B representative action, a press file — credible. It is upstream of exit, not
an alternative to it. Rewrite as "generates the record that exit strategies run on."

*Collective bargaining — "marginal" against a contingent mediator.* Same objection, plus ledger item
6: the row silently assumes a bearer who can lawfully bargain, and under EU competition law only
some of the chapter's protagonists can. The row needs a bearer qualifier.

*Antitrust and anti-steering remedies — "alters conduct, not dependence" against a necessary
mediator.* Too flat after *Android Auto*, where an Article 102 remedy compelling interoperability
with a platform the applicant could not duplicate goes directly to dependence. And the Apple
aftermath (ledger item 5) shows the opposite failure on the contingent side: an anti-steering remedy
that opened the bypass and left the extraction intact. Both cells are stated with more confidence
than the record supports. The honest version says the remedy alters the *terms* of dependence and
that whether it alters its *degree* is an empirical question the Apple and Google files are now
answering in real time. **[FIX IF TIME]**

The interoperability paragraph that opens §7 is otherwise the strongest legal writing in the chapter.
"A regulator sorting functions by whether interoperability bites is running the diagnostic under
another name" is a good sentence and the Commission's own reasoning in COM(2026) 178 — that
messaging interoperability produced entrants while social-network interoperability faces both greater
technical complexity and limited demand — is a genuinely apt illustration. Keep it.

### 3.8 §8 — the publication-lag problem

The brief asks which sentences will be wrong when an IGI volume appears. Assume the volume lands in
mid-to-late 2027. Here is the list, worst first.

**1. "No member state had fully transposed the directive as this chapter was written."** Guaranteed
stale. The deadline is 2 December 2026 — three months after the chapter is due and roughly a year
before the volume appears. As of April 2026, four member states had a platform-work presumption of
employment in national law (Italy, Spain, Belgium, Portugal), five were drafting, and the rest had
not started; Italy's rider presumption came into force in May 2026. By publication some states will
have transposed, most will have missed the deadline, and the Commission will likely have opened
infringement proceedings. Any of the three possible sentences ("none has," "all have," "some have")
is a hostage. Write the deadline, not the state of play. Draft supplied in Part 3.

**2. "The Commission has since proposed to repeal it."** Accurate today. As of 15 August 2026 the
Digital Omnibus (COM(2025) 837) has not reached trilogue: the ITRE/IMCO joint report was tabled in
June 2026, the Council was working from a presidency compromise of 10 June after ambassadors failed
to agree on 8 June, and a Parliament impact study on the GDPR, ePrivacy and P2B strands is due in
October 2026 — which is a fair indicator that the P2B repeal is contested rather than settled. Note
also the two-file split: the *AI* omnibus (COM(2025) 836) is done, adopted by Parliament on 16 June
2026 and published as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, while this one is not. A reader in 2027 who knows
"the omnibus passed" may read the chapter's sentence as referring to the wrong file. Date the
sentence and name the file. **[FIX NOW]**

**3. The Article 22 discussion, on two grounds.** Beyond ledger item 2, the Digital Omnibus proposes
to amend Article 22 itself — reframing the right not to be subject to automated decision-making into
an exhaustive list of permitted cases, and relaxing the contractual-necessity test so that a decision
may count as necessary even where a human alternative exists. The EDPB and EDPS objected in their
joint opinion that Article 22 must remain a prohibition in principle. On reporting available to me,
the Article 22 amendments were not present in Member State compromise texts circulating in March
2026, so the provision may survive intact — but a chapter that describes Article 22 as "a floor" and
says its "practice [is] thin" while the legislature is actively considering lowering it should say
so. This is also the chapter's strongest available example of its own thesis: an instrument that
supplies case-level standing is the one under simplification pressure, and the one that supplies
ranking disclosure to business users is proposed for repeal. Two facts, one argument. **[FIX NOW]**

**4. "Enforcement has reached platform conduct without reaching that layer."** Already false (ledger
item 4) and will look worse in 2027 as the Google decisions work through their sixty-day compliance
clock and, presumably, an appeal.

**5. T-438/25 "pending."** Safe — General Court proceedings of this kind run two to four years, so it
will still be pending — but write "pending at the time of writing" so the sentence carries its own
date.

**6. The DMA review.** Ages well. COM(2026) 178 is the statutory Article 53 review, the next one is
due in 2029, and the Commission concluded that the DMA is fit for purpose and needs no legislative
amendment. The chapter can say that with confidence and gain three years of stability. Worth adding
the "fit for purpose, no amendment" finding explicitly: it is the fixed point in §8's otherwise
shifting landscape, and it sets up the chapter's own claim that the gap is not in the DMA's drafting
but in what disclosure-and-conduct regulation can do at all.

The general prescription. Every legal sentence in §7 and §8 should carry one of three markers: a
date ("as of August 2026"), a deadline ("member states must, by 2 December 2026"), or a status word
("proposed," "pending," "in force since"). Sentences in the undated present tense — "the Commission
has since proposed," "no member state had fully transposed," "the European Competition Network's
monitoring exercise put at" — are the ones that rot. There are eleven of them by my count and they
can all be fixed in an hour.

### 3.9 §3.2, §6, §9, §10 — brief

§3.2 is good law apart from ledger item 2. The P2B / DSA / DMA triage in the fifth paragraph is
accurate and unusually well proportioned: ranking parameters to the P2B Regulation, recommender
transparency and researcher access to the DSA, gatekeeper conduct to the DMA. That is the right map.

One addition, and it is the single best thing a lawyer can give this chapter. §3.2's first argument is
that opacity "survives the release of source code, because the behavior of a model over millions of
parameters and cases is not legible the way a statute is legible." The Court of Justice held
essentially this in Case C-203/22, *Dun & Bradstreet Austria* (27 February 2025): "meaningful
information about the logic involved" under Article 15(1)(h) GDPR is not discharged by communicating
the algorithm or by "a detailed description of all the steps in automated decision making," and the
information must be sufficient to let the data subject actually contest the decision; where the
controller claims trade secrecy, it must disclose to the supervisory authority or court, which then
balances. That is the chapter's argument, made by the Court, in a judgment nobody in this literature
cites. It converts a Burrell-plus-Ananny-and-Crawford paragraph into a paragraph with hard-law
backing, and it does the same work for §7's contestability discussion. **[FIX NOW]** — one sentence,
one reference.

§6 is outside my competence and I do not judge it.

§9's four research directions are fine. One legal observation on the second: an instrument for
coordinative sovereignty that asks whether an actor "can look the mediator in the eye and speak
plainly" is going to correlate heavily with whether the actor has been through a dispute, which is a
selection problem, not a construct problem. Not my finding to press.

§10 makes no legal claims and I have nothing on it. "Europe's hotels spent a decade prying open a
gate their dependence did not run through" is a good line and I would quote it.

## 4. Part 2 — style and slop audit, lawyer's angle

Short, because the prose is mostly good and reviewer 06 owns this.

**"European competition law already runs this counterfactual on contract terms."** (§5.2)
*Failure type: overstated frequency.* "Already runs" suggests routine application. The
ancillary-restraints gateway is narrow and rarely satisfied — that is the whole force of para. 53.
Rewrite: "European competition law asks a version of this question about contract terms."

**"The Digital Markets Act's anti-steering obligations are the edge test run by a regulator."**
(§5.2) and **"A regulator sorting functions by whether interoperability bites is running the
diagnostic under another name."** (§7)
*Failure type: verbatim refrain.* The same claim, the same shape, twice, and the second one is
better. Keep §7's. Downgrade §5.2's to something that survives the Apple aftermath: "The Digital
Markets Act's anti-steering obligations run the edge operation, and what happened next is

## 5. Part 3 — paste-ready revisions

All written in the chapter's register: impersonal, named agents, no first person.

**(a) §5.5, the C-264/23 holding.** Replace:

> In September 2024 the Court of Justice of the European Union held that parity clauses, wide and
> narrow alike, are not ancillary restraints and so fall within the prohibition on anticompetitive
> agreements (Case C-264/23), four months after Booking.com was designated a gatekeeper under the
> Digital Markets Act.

with:

> In September 2024 the Court of Justice of the European Union held that parity clauses, wide and
> narrow alike, cannot in principle be classified as ancillary restraints, so that each must be
> assessed on its own terms under the prohibition on anticompetitive agreements rather than
> escaping it with the operation it accompanies (Case C-264/23). The judgment came four months
> after Booking.com was designated a gatekeeper under the Digital Markets Act, whose Article 5(3)
> forbids a gatekeeper to impose parity obligations at all.

**(b) §5.2, the ancillary-restraints analogy, with both limbs and the better paragraph.** Replace the
sentence beginning "Under the ancillary-restraints doctrine a restriction escapes…" with:

> Under the ancillary-restraints doctrine a restriction accompanies its main operation outside the
> prohibition on anticompetitive agreements only where "the implementation of the main operation,
> which is not anti-competitive in nature, would be impossible in the absence of the restriction in
> question" and the restriction is proportionate to that operation's objectives (Case C-264/23,
> paras. 53–54) — the same question, asked of a clause. The Court adds that the comparison need not
> stop at the bare absence of the restriction but may extend to "realistic situations that might
> arise in the absence of that restriction" (para. 54), which is the operation the diagnostic
> performs on a mediator's functions.

**(c) §3.2, Article 22.** Replace:

> Article 22 of the GDPR belongs to a different family: it gives a natural person subject to a
> solely automated decision the right to obtain human intervention and to contest the outcome.

with:

> Article 22 of the GDPR belongs to a different family. It prohibits in principle the subjection of
> a natural person to a decision taken solely by automated processing that produces legal or
> similarly significant effects, and where such a decision is nevertheless permitted on the basis of
> a contract or explicit consent it entitles the person to human intervention, to state a view, and
> to contest the outcome. The Court of Justice has read the prohibition broadly, treating a credit
> score as itself the decision where the lender draws heavily on it (Case C-634/21), and has held
> that the accompanying right of access is not discharged by handing over the algorithm, since what
> the person is owed is enough to contest the decision (Case C-203/22).

Apply the same correction to the §7 sentence beginning "European data-protection law sets a floor."

**(d) §7, interoperability and essential facilities.** Replace "and competition law has long
understood it as one under the essential-facilities logic it applies to infrastructure that cannot be
duplicated" with:

> and competition law has long understood it as one, first through the essential-facilities logic it
> applies to infrastructure that cannot be duplicated, and lately on a broader footing: in Case
> C-233/23 the Court of Justice held that a dominant undertaking's refusal to make a platform
> interoperable with a third party's application may be abusive even where the platform is not
> indispensable to that party, because the platform had been developed not "solely for the needs of
> [the undertaking's] own business but with a view to enabling third-party undertakings to use that
> infrastructure." The distinction the Court draws there — between a facility built for its owner's
> use and a platform built to be coordinated through — tracks the one the diagnostic draws.

**(e) §7, the data-intermediary sentence.** Replace the two sentences from "The European Data
Governance Act has since created…" with:

> The European Data Governance Act created a registered category of data intermediary, obliging such
> providers to notify a national authority before operating. The Digital Omnibus proposal of
> November 2025 would repeal that Act, carry the data-intermediation rules into the Data Act, and
> make the registration voluntary (European Commission, 2025a). The category would survive its
> statutory vehicle and lose the obligation that made it a register, and the evidence on whether the
> mechanism confers standing in practice is not yet in.

**(f) §8, the transposition sentence, written to age.** Replace:

> No member state had fully transposed the directive as this chapter was written, so the standing it
> describes is a legislative commitment, not yet a present fact.

with:

> Transposition falls due on 2 December 2026, and the directive therefore describes standing that
> member states are obliged to create rather than standing platform workers presently hold. How much
> of it survives twenty-seven transpositions is the question, and the directive leaves the
> presumption's trigger — facts indicating direction and control — to national law, collective
> agreements and practice, which is where the variation will come from.

**(g) §8, the enforcement sentence.** Replace from "Enforcement has reached platform conduct…"
through "…have followed against other platforms" with:

> Enforcement has reached the gatekeeper's own conduct without giving the ranked party standing over
> the ranking. The European Commission fined Apple €500 million in April 2025 for restricting how
> developers steer users to offers outside its App Store (European Commission, 2025b), a decision
> Apple has appealed and which was pending at the time of writing (Case T-438/25); it fined Meta
> €200 million the same day, and in July 2026 fined Google €890 million in two decisions, one for
> preferring its own services in search results and one for anti-steering on its application store.
> Each reaches what the platform does to rivals or to the flow of transactions. None gives the
> developer or the merchant a channel through which to contest the determination that ranked it.

**(h) §8, the P2B sentence, narrowed.** Replace "which would remove the only duty in European law to
disclose the main parameters of ranking to the business users being ranked. The Digital Services
Act's recommender transparency runs to a platform's users and does not replace that duty." with:

> which would remove the duty owed to a business user to explain the parameters by which its own
> goods and services are ranked for others. The Digital Services Act's recommender transparency runs
> in the other direction, explaining to a recipient why particular information is being surfaced to
> that recipient, and it does not replace the duty the Regulation owes to the ranked party.

**(i) §5.5, the commission range.** Replace "which the European Competition Network's monitoring
exercise put at 10% to above 20% of each booking (European Competition Network, 2017)" with:

> which the European Competition Network's 2016 monitoring exercise put at ten to above twenty
> percent of each booking in basic commission, before the additional commission agencies charge for
> better visibility and preferred-partner status (European Competition Network, 2017, para. 32).

**(j) §7, the collective-bargaining bearer problem.** Add after "Where platform workers or
platform-dependent firms can organize…":

> The two face different law. The Commission's 2022 guidelines withhold enforcement of the
> prohibition on anticompetitive agreements from collective agreements concluded by solo
> self-employed persons in a weak negotiating position, naming those who work through digital labour
> platforms; a comparable agreement among independent firms about the terms they will accept from a
> marketplace remains a horizontal agreement between undertakings. Voice at the level of the rule is
> lawful for the worker and, for the dependent firm, available only through representation rather
> than bargaining.

**(k) §5.2, the qualification the Apple file requires.** Add after "Node says necessary and edge says
contingent, on the same function, in the same firm.":

> What follows from opening the edge is a further question. Ordered in April 2025 to stop restricting
> how developers steer users outside its store, Apple opened the link and attached a schedule of fees
> to purchases made through it, so that the tie was restored and the charge moved onto the restored
> tie. A gate can rest on the absence of the direct link and on a second lever — control of the
> device, the account, the installed base — and the counterfactual isolates the first without
> touching the second. Contingency establishes that exit is available in principle; what exit is
> worth is set by what the mediator can still charge for it.

## 6. Verdict

**Minor revisions**, on the legal material. Everything in my ledger except items 4, 5 and the
taxonomy problem is a sentence-level fix, and the paste-ready text above covers them. I would not
hold the chapter for the taxonomy gap; I would hold it for items 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6, all of which are
statements about law that a competent reader will catch.

**The single most important fix:** the C-264/23 holding in §5.5. It is the only place where the
chapter states a court's conclusion incorrectly, it sits inside the chapter's best empirical section,
and the case is famous enough that a hostile referee will find it in thirty seconds. Everything else
I have raised is a matter of precision or of the record having moved. That one is an error.

**The chapter's biggest genuine strength**, from where I sit: it identifies, correctly and in the
right vocabulary, the thing that is actually wrong with the European architecture — that the
instruments make the object legible and do not give the affected party a channel — and it does so
without the usual interdisciplinary sloppiness about which instrument does what. The P2B / DSA / DMA
triage in §3.2 is more accurate than most of what I read in law reviews. And the observation in §8,
that the one instrument attaching a complaint channel and representative standing to a ranking
disclosure is the instrument now proposed for repeal, is a genuinely good piece of legal-political
noticing that I have not seen made in print. That paragraph is the chapter's contribution to my
field. It should be developed, not compressed.

**The one thing only the author can supply:** whether the necessary/contingent distinction survives a
mediator that can price the restored tie. The Apple file is the test case and it is in the public
record. Everything else here I could have written; that question needs whoever built the diagnostic
to say what the diagnostic does with it.

## 7. Citation behavior

Yes, and I know the argument I would cite it in.

I write case notes and short pieces on remedy design, and the recurring difficulty is that
competition and platform-regulation remedies are argued in a vocabulary — indispensability,
substitutability, contestability — that has no term for what a business user actually wants, which is
not a different supplier but a hearing. Coordinative sovereignty names that, and the necessary /
contingent split gives me a way to say why an interoperability remedy is the right instrument in one
file and theatre in another. The specific argument: **against the assumption, standard in remedy
design and explicit in the Commission's contestability framing, that opening a market is the
general-purpose fix.** I would cite this chapter for the proposition that where a mediator's position
survives the restoration of direct dealing, a contestability remedy delivers nothing to the dependent
party and the regulator should be building a governance channel instead. That is a live argument in
the DMA review consultations and this chapter states it better than the trade-association
submissions do.

I would also cite §7's observation about the P2B repeal in a piece on the Digital Omnibus. It is a
sharper framing than "deregulation bad": the Regulation is being repealed as redundant because the
DSA and DMA cover ranking, and what they do not cover is the channel the P2B Regulation attached to
the disclosure. That is an argument, and it is one I would want a footnote for.

What would stop me. If the C-264/23 sentence ships as written, I will not cite the chapter for
anything, because a reader who follows my footnote to §5.5 finds a misstated holding and discounts
everything I built on it. That is the whole cost calculation for a practitioner-academic: I am
lending my accuracy to the source. Fix that sentence and the Article 22 sentences and I cite it
without hesitation.

Would I assign it? Yes, in the seminar, in the week on remedies — paired with the DMA review report
and the Android Auto judgment. It is the right length, it is readable by non-lawyers, and it gives
students a question to argue about rather than a doctrine to memorise. I would assign §§3, 5 and 7
and set the Apple compliance saga against §5.2 as the seminar problem: the regulator ran the edge
test, the edge opened, the toll moved, now tell me what the diagnostic says. That is a good two
hours. A chapter that generates a good seminar problem is a chapter worth having.
