# Review — 03. Adaeze Nkemelu, platform-labour sociologist

**Manuscript:** *Algorithmacy and Sovereignty: Toward a Coordinative Sovereignty for the Platform Era*
(`chapter/chapter_v2.md`, 2026-08-14 rewrite)
**Verdict:** minor revisions for the volume; major revisions for the argument the chapter wants to make.

---

## 1. Who I am

I teach sociology of work and I have spent nine years in cars, on motorbikes, and in moderation
floors — couriers in Lagos and Manchester, ride-hail drivers in Nairobi and Lisbon, content
moderators in a Warsaw vendor site that had a client I was not allowed to name. My publications sit
in the Vallas and Schor line: platforms as a distinct organizational form, algorithmic control as
Kellogg's contested terrain, Rahman's invisible cage, Wood's autonomy that operates inside control.
A colleague in a business school sent me this chapter with the line "this is your argument in
management language," and he meant it as a compliment. He was right about the argument. The
translation is what I want to talk about. I came in believing three things that the chapter will
have to move me off: that the analytic center of platform coordination is the person who can be
deactivated for speaking; that voice, on platforms, has almost never been conferred and has almost
always been seized; and that a competence-based account of standing, however carefully hedged,
drifts toward telling the worst-off party that it is short a skill. The chapter's §4 political-
economy paragraph tells me the author saw the third one coming. I will judge whether seeing it was
enough.

---

## 2. Step 0 — register and bar

The register is an impersonal academic chapter with no first person, APA 7, for an IGI volume on
digital sovereignty. I accept all of that and will not complain about the absence of "I," the
masked self-citations, or the block quotations. I will not complain that the chapter is written for
managers; the volume's call is what it is, and a labour argument that refuses to enter management
venues stays in its own seminar. I will not demand the companion's formal apparatus.

What I will judge hard: whether the chapter's protagonists are chosen or defaulted; whether the
worked cases are picked for what they prove or for what they avoid; whether the political-economy
paragraph in §4 does work or buys an indulgence; and whether §7's institutional catalog describes
the world in which those institutions came into existence. Standpoint is not a complaint about who
the author is. It is a claim that the choice of protagonist has consequences inside the argument,
and I intend to show the consequences rather than assert them.

## 3. Part 1 — the read

### 3.1 Exit, voice, and the third power — the mediator's power to remove the actor
**[TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD; one paragraph is [FIX NOW]]**

This is the finding that matters most and the one I cannot ask the author to fix in fifteen days.

§5.3 takes Hirschman's pair and maps it onto the diagnostic: contingent mediator, exit available;
necessary mediator, voice only. The chapter is careful about the asymmetries Hirschman himself
worried over — that exit can substitute for voice, that a captive population resigns itself sooner
than it complains. It never notices that Hirschman's whole apparatus assumes the *member* decides
whether to leave. Exit and voice are both the member's acts. Loyalty is the member's disposition.
Decline is something the organization does to itself.

Platform coordination breaks that assumption at the root, because the mediator holds a power
Hirschman had no occasion to model: it can remove the actor from the coordination. Deactivation is
not exit. It is expulsion, and it is priced into every act of voice a worker considers. Rahman's
invisible cage, which the chapter cites, is a cage precisely because the drivers and freelancers
inside it are managing the risk of removal by a standard they cannot see. Bucher and colleagues'
anticipatory compliance, also cited, is behaviour organized around that risk. Curchod and
colleagues' eBay sellers, whom the chapter narrates at length, contact buyers pre-emptively because
suspension is on the table. Three of the chapter's own sources describe conduct driven by the fear
of expulsion, and the chapter reads all three as evidence of opacity.

The damage runs in two directions. First, §5.3's account of why voice is weak under a necessary
mediator is incomplete, and the missing term is the strongest one: voice is weak not only because
there is no exit to lend the critic leverage, but because the mediator can end the relationship on
its own motion and owes no account of why. Second — and this is the part that should trouble the
author most — §9 already knows this. The proposed test item for coordinative sovereignty asks
whether an actor "can look the mediator in the eye and speak plainly, without needing to ingratiate
or to avoid provoking a power that could retaliate." Retaliation appears in the instrument and
nowhere in the theory. A construct whose measurement anticipates a mechanism its framework does not
model has a hole in the framework, not in the instrument.

For the deadline: one paragraph in §5.3, after the Hirschman discussion, naming expulsion as the
mediator's power and noting that it prices voice. I supply a draft in Part 5. The full treatment —
a three-term structure of exit, voice, and removal, with the empirical literature on deactivation
behind it — is a paper, and it should be the paper.

### 3.2 The bypass test is never run on a labour platform **[FIX IF TIME]**

§5.2 through §5.5 run the counterfactual on a franchised car dealer, an application store's payment
gate, a payment network's anti-steering rule, a large marketplace's four functions, and an online
travel agency. Five worked cases, all of them business-to-business or business-to-consumer, none of
them a labour platform. §3.1 opens the triad with a driver and a rider and then never returns to
them. The one platform whose paradigm case is a worker is the one platform the diagnostic is not
made to sort.

I do not think this is squeamishness. I think it is that the answer is uncomfortable, and I want to
show the author what it is, because the chapter is better if it faces it.

Take ride-hail dispatch and run §5.2 honestly. Restore the direct tie between driver and rider — the
tie the platform's terms forbid, on pain of deactivation — and ask what dispatch still does. For a
rider standing on an unfamiliar street at two in the morning who needs a car in four minutes,
matching is real integrating work that no direct tie between two strangers reproduces. That function
survives the bypass. Payment, dynamic pricing, the rating that decides continued access, and the
deactivation decision do not: they rest on a rule against direct dealing, and where drivers and
riders do exchange numbers and arrange pickups off-app — which they do, at volume, in every city
where I have worked — the platform loses exactly those functions and keeps the cold match. By the
chapter's own four-way taxonomy the verdict is *partial*, with the same shape as the hotel case:
necessary aggregation, contingent gate.

That verdict is a genuine result and the chapter should claim it. Its strategic implication is what
the chapter cannot currently absorb. §5.4 and §8 say that against a contingent gate the move is
toward exit, and §8 warns that "treating a necessary mediator as contingent wastes effort on an exit
that cannot be had." But the driver who acts on a correct contingent verdict — who arranges the
direct tie, who runs the bypass the diagnostic told her was available — commits the offence for
which the platform removes her. The decision procedure returns a strategy that costs a firm a
negotiation and costs a worker her income. That asymmetry is not a detail of implementation. It is
a variable the diagnostic does not carry, and it is the variable that separates the chapter's
protagonists from mine. An ISV that multihomes is diversifying. A courier who multihomes is
sometimes doing the same thing and sometimes breaking an exclusivity term.

Related and smaller, but load-bearing: §3.1 says "a driver is coordinating with a rider she will
never meet." She meets him. He gets into her car and sits behind her for twenty minutes. The
chapter's triad leans on the parties' mutual inaccessibility — the introduction is repeated in §1
("a counterpart it will never meet") and in the Key Terms — and ride-hail is the case that falsifies
it while strengthening the underlying claim. The parties meet bodily, the mediation holds
absolutely, and that is a *better* demonstration that a necessary mediator's grip does not rest on
the parties' ignorance of each other. Fix the sentence; it costs nothing and it buys the point.

### 3.3 §7 catalogues standing that is granted, never standing that is taken **[FIX IF TIME]**

I read §7 twice looking for a worker who does something. There is not one. Seven instruments, four
paragraphs of institutional description, and in fifteen thousand words no strike, no union, no
walkout, no case brought by the people the instruments are for. The word "organize" appears twice,
both times in the conditional — "where platform workers or platform-dependent firms *can* organize"
— and the second appearance is §8 advising a manager to "organize with other dependent firms."

The chapter's own source says the opposite of what §7 assumes. Dasgupta and colleagues' systematic
review, which §5.3 cites in a subordinate clause on the way to somewhere else, is subtitled *the
constitution of a grassroots voice mechanism*. Its finding is that platform-worker voice constitutes
itself outside institutional channels — in messaging groups, in wildcat log-offs, in
self-organized associations that no statute created — and that formal voice arrives, when it
arrives, downstream of the informal kind. Lei's *Delivering solidarity*, the chapter's other worker-
collective-action citation, appears exactly once, to explain why a bargaining unit might fail to
form. The chapter cites the literature on workers acting twice, and uses it once for a "notably"
and once for a discouragement.

The damage is that §7 reads as a menu handed to a reader who is deciding which instrument to
support, and describes none of the instruments as things that exist because somebody fought for
them. That is not only a political omission; it is an analytic one, because it removes the mechanism
by which any of these instruments would arrive in a coordination that does not yet have them. §7's
closing sentence — "the diagnostic is what tells a policymaker where to put each one" — names the
implied agent. A policymaker. The chapter is written for a reader who allocates instruments, not
for one who needs them.

The cheapest repair is also the strongest, because the chapter is missing the single best worked
example of its own contestability row. On 4 April 2023 the Amsterdam Court of Appeal ruled largely
in favour of drivers against Uber and Ola, finding that ride assignment, pricing, rating, fraud-
probability scoring and account deactivation constitute automated decision-making within Article 22,
and rejecting Uber's human-in-the-loop defence on the facts as not much more than a symbolic act.
The cases were brought by drivers through the App Drivers and Couriers Union and Worker Info
Exchange. That is §7's contestability row and §7's data-trust row, run by workers, against a
mediator the chapter would classify as necessary, producing a finding that goes directly to the
chapter's thesis about the standing gap. It belongs in §7 and its absence is conspicuous to anyone
in my field. (Details verified; see Part 8.)

### 3.4 §7's collective-bargaining row omits the barrier and its removal **[FIX IF TIME]**

The chapter lists collective bargaining as voice at the level of the rule and anchors it in the
platform-work directive's Article 25. For a labour reader the conspicuous absence is what stood in
its way for two decades: EU competition law treated self-employed platform workers as undertakings,
so their collective agreements risked being cartels. The Commission's guidelines of 29 September
2022 on the application of Union competition law to collective agreements of solo self-employed
persons removed that obstacle for, among others, persons providing services through a digital labour
platform.

This matters to the chapter's own argument, not only to my sense of the record. §7's organizing
claim is that the *same instrument changes character depending on the mediator it meets*. Here is a
case where one body of law converted another body of law's voice instrument into a prohibited
restraint, and where a soft-law act reversed it specifically for platform work. The chapter's
sorting principle handles instruments against mediators and has no cell for instruments against
instruments. One sentence would both fix the omission and extend the sorting.

### 3.5 The bearer question, seen from the domination literature **[TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]**

§6 makes the construct relational and lets a firm bear it, citing Pettit (2016) for the extension of
non-domination to corporate bodies and adding that "where the two diverge, the members' standing
governs." I have read that clause five times.

The construct is assembled out of literatures that are all about asymmetry between a powerful party
and a vulnerable one: Pettit and Skinner on domination, Muldoon and Raekstad on algorithmic
domination in the gig economy, Cicerchia on structural domination in the labour market, Elizabeth
Anderson on private government. Make the bearer relational, and an independent software vendor and a
courier become co-bearers of the same construct. Then read §4's closing sentence — "coordinative
sovereignty, which algorithmacy makes possible, therefore concentrates where algorithmacy is already
held" — and the construct's distribution tracks structural advantage. A concept built from the
domination literature now measures a quantity of which the dominating party's suppliers hold more
than the dominated. That is not incoherent. It is a concept that cannot, by itself, distinguish
domination from ordinary market disadvantage, and every borrowed normative premise in §6 assumed it
could.

The tie-break clause does not save it. "Members' standing governs" is determinate for a cooperative
and empty for a firm, because a firm's members, on the Pettit reading the chapter invokes, are the
people whose interests the corporate body's freedom serves — for an independent hotel, its owners.
The housekeeper is not a member. Which brings the deeper problem: the triad has no position for her.
In §5.5's hotel case the two coordinated parties are the hotel and the traveller and the mediator is
the agency, and the people who absorb a commission of ten to above twenty percent of every booking
in an operation with thin margins do not appear in the coordination at all. Marketplace sellers'
coordinative sovereignty is exercised, in part, over warehouse and delivery labour. A framework whose
unit is "the coordinated actor" cannot see a party who is inside the coordination and is neither of
the two coordinated parties nor the mediator.

I do not think this is fixable by 30 August and I would not want it fixed badly. What the chapter can
do now is say it: one sentence conceding that the relational bearer makes the construct's
distribution track advantage, and that the chapter takes no position on the coordinated actor's own
downstream dependants. Naming a limit is not the same as having none.

### 3.6 The Matthew-effect sentence is the chapter's best and it is stated and dropped **[FIX IF TIME]**

§4's political-economy paragraph does what the brief says it was written to do, and it does it well.
Cotter and Reisdorf on algorithmic knowledge tracking socioeconomic advantage, Petre and colleagues
on "gaming" as a charge that falls hardest on the unestablished, opacity preserving the rent, and
then the conclusion: the competence concentrates where it is already held. That paragraph is the
answer to the deficit-model objection, and I accept it as an answer *to that objection*.

It is not enough, for a different reason. The chapter states the mechanism and then writes §7 and §8
as though it had not. If coordinative sovereignty concentrates where algorithmacy is already held,
then every instrument in §7 is subject to the same gradient, and the prediction is specific: data
trusts will be joined by the operators with the administrative capacity to join them, bargaining
units will form where workers already have stable schedules and a shared language, contestation
rights will be exercised by whoever can absorb the delay, and oversight boards will hear from the
already-articulate. My fieldwork says exactly this. The couriers who model the dispatcher accurately
are the ones with a second income, a smartphone data plan they do not ration, and the language of
the app's interface as a first language. The gradient does not disappear when the instrument
arrives; it decides who reaches the instrument.

That is one paragraph in §7, and it turns the chapter's best sentence from an aside into a
proposition with testable content. It also converts my standpoint objection into the chapter's own
finding, which is a better outcome for the author than absorbing it as a criticism.

### 3.7 Vallas and Schor supply the triad; the chapter drops what they actually argued **[FIX NOW]**

§3 cites Vallas and Schor for platforms as a distinct governance mechanism and for the *tertius
gaudens*. Their contribution is the image of platforms as **permissive potentates** — actors that
externalize responsibility and control over transactions while retaining concentrated power. That
concept is not decoration; it is the mechanism the chapter needs. The platform's power operates
through the *granting* of discretion: choose your hours, set your price, decline the job. Wood and
colleagues, whom the chapter cites two paragraphs later, found the same thing and the chapter
renders it as "the flexibility such platforms advertise is real, and it operates inside that
control" — a good sentence that names the phenomenon without naming the literature that theorized
it.

This matters beyond attribution. §3's account of co-optation, narrowed along Selznick's axis to
enrollment with the forms of participation removed, is a *withholding* account: the platform gives
nothing, not even ceremony. The permissive-potentate account is the opposite and is better
supported: the platform gives a great deal — discretion, autonomy, the appearance of
entrepreneurship — and that giving is how the control operates. The chapter has picked the weaker of
two available readings of its own source, and Vallas and Schor's is the one that explains why
platform workers so often describe themselves as independent while behaving as though they are
supervised. Two sentences in §3 would fix it and strengthen §3.1.

### 3.8 Wood et al. stripped of its geography **[FIX NOW]**

"A worker chooses her hours and cannot see what her choices are measured against (Wood et al.,
2019)." That study is of remote gig workers in Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, and its
finding about autonomy inside control is inseparable from labour markets where the platform is the
best available employer. The chapter uses it as a generic proposition about workers. I mention it
because it is the chapter's one piece of evidence from outside Europe and North America and it
enters with its provenance removed. Six words fix it.

### 3.9 Content moderators are absent and the construct predicts them **[FIX IF TIME]**

The chapter's third case, on my reading, writes itself. A content moderator coordinates a platform
with its users through a system that queues her work, times her decisions, scores her agreement
against a policy she did not write, and routes her output to an oversight board that adjudicates her
determinations while giving her no standing before it. §7's oversight-board row — "voice, bounded by
the constituting platform" — is literally true of the worker whose labour the board reviews, and the
chapter's Klonick citation does not notice her. The mediator is necessary by any reading of §5.2.
The employer is a vendor in a third country. The construct's bearer question, the expulsion problem,
and the bounded-oversight row all converge on one occupation.

A sentence in §7 would do. The case is a paper.

### 3.10 §5.5's hotel decade has a labour interior **[TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD]**

The chapter's strongest empirical moment measures channel shares and concludes that dependence
deepened. Commission of ten to above twenty percent of every booking comes out of a cost structure,
and in an independent hotel the adjustable line is labour — hours, agency staffing, outsourced
housekeeping. Whether a decade of deepening intermediation shows up in hotel employment terms is a
question the chapter's own data invite and cannot answer. I raise it because it is the shape of the
next study and because it is the point at which "the coordinated actor's" welfare stops being a
single quantity.

### 3.11 Sections where I have no purchase

§2 is not mine and I have no objection to it. §9's measurement program is competent and its
discriminant-validity worry against DeVito is the right worry; my only note is the one in 3.1, that
its retaliation item outruns the theory. §10 is clean.

## 4. Part 2 — style and slop audit

Judged against the house register, not against a generic anti-AI checklist. The prose is mostly
good, and the v2 rewrite reads as a person writing. Four specimens.

**(a) The unsourced vignette.** §4's "The shape is visible in an ordinary week" runs a
three-paragraph account of a seller diagnosing a ranking drop, in ethnographic present tense, with
no source. It sits directly after Curchod and colleagues, so a reader will take it as their data. It
is not. For anyone who does fieldwork, a constructed composite written in the register of an
observation is the one thing you cannot let stand unmarked. This is a one-clause fix and I would
make it a condition of acceptance.

> *Rewrite the opening:* "A composite drawn from that study's dynamics shows the shape in an
> ordinary week."

**(b) The metronomic two-beat closer.** The chapter ends sections and paragraphs on the same
rhythm often enough that I started predicting it: "They constitute it." / "Retention suppresses exit
and activates nothing." / "She has read nothing. There was nothing to read." / "Dependence did not
fall as the gate came open. It deepened." / "None of it will show up on a compliance audit." /
"came out of it more dependent than they went in." Each is good. Six is a tic. Cut two — I would
cut "She has read nothing. There was nothing to read.", which is the weakest, and let the paragraph
end on "abandons a channel that was working."

**(c) Antithesis on a schedule.** "Disclosure makes an object legible. What the coordinated actor
needs is standing." The contrast is earned and the shape is the machine's. It also closes §3.2,
immediately after a paragraph that has already made the point twice.

> *Rewrite:* "Disclosure makes an object legible, and legibility without standing to act on it
> leaves the coordinated actor where §3.2 found her."

**(d) Agentless passive at the chapter's most political sentence.** §8: "Standing over the
coordination is being built while control of the infrastructure stays at zero." Being built by
whom? By legislators, after a decade of litigation, strikes, and reclassification cases brought by
platform workers and their unions. The chapter's house rule bans agentless passives where a named
agent exists, and here the missing agent is precisely the actor §7 never shows acting. Style defect
and substantive omission in one clause.

> *Rewrite:* "Legislators are building standing over the coordination while control of the
> infrastructure stays at zero."

One more of the same family, smaller: §3.3's "the coordinated are enrolled in a logic that need not
serve them" — the platform enrolls them, and the sentence has just finished naming it.

## 5. Part 3 — paste-ready revisions

**(1) §5.3, after the Hirschman paragraph on captive populations. Names expulsion.**

> Hirschman's pair assumes that leaving is the member's decision. A mediator that assigns access
> holds a third power neither exit nor voice describes: it can remove the actor from the
> coordination altogether. Deactivation is not exit, because the coordinated actor does not choose
> it and is owed no account of it, and its availability prices every act of voice that precedes it.
> Rahman's (2021) reactive workers, Bucher et al.'s (2021) anticipatory compliance, and Curchod et
> al.'s (2020) pre-emptive sellers all describe conduct organized around the risk of removal rather
> than around the calculus of leaving. Where the mediator is necessary and can also expel, voice is
> the only remaining power and the mediator sets its cost.

**(2) §5.4 or §8, after the exit prescription. Prices the bypass for a worker.**

> The cost of acting on the diagnostic is not the same for every dependent party. A firm that runs
> the bypass against a contingent gate risks a commercial dispute; a worker who runs the same
> bypass, where the platform's terms forbid direct dealing, risks removal from the coordination. A
> correct classification therefore does not settle what a dependent party should do, and the
> repertoire available to a dependent person is narrower than the repertoire available to a
> dependent firm at the same point in the same coordination.

**(3) §7, after the table and the bearer sentence. Runs the §4 gradient against the instruments.**

> The gradient of section 4 reaches the instruments themselves. Because algorithmacy concentrates
> where it is already held, an instrument that confers standing formally will be exercised first by
> the actors with the capacity to reach it: data trusts by operators who can manage a membership,
> bargaining units where schedules are stable enough for a unit to form, contestation rights by
> parties who can absorb the delay of contesting. Cotter and Reisdorf's (2020) knowledge gap
> therefore predicts a standing gap inside each remedy, and an institutional design that ignores it
> will widen the distribution it was built to correct.

**(4) §7, contestability paragraph. Adds the worked example and the actors who brought it.**

> Workers have already run this instrument against a necessary mediator. In April 2023 the Amsterdam
> Court of Appeal held that ride assignment, pricing, driver rating, fraud-probability scoring, and
> account deactivation on the Uber and Ola platforms constitute automated decision-making within
> Article 22, and rejected the platforms' human-in-the-loop defence on the facts. Drivers brought
> the cases through their union and an associated data-rights organization, which is how a case-level
> right became a collective instrument: the standing was individual and the capacity to exercise it
> was pooled.

**(5) §7, collective-bargaining paragraph. Adds the barrier and its removal.**

> The instrument had first to be released by another body of law. Because competition law treats
> self-employed persons as undertakings, collective agreements among platform workers classified as
> self-employed risked falling under the prohibition on restrictive agreements, until the European
> Commission's 2022 guidelines on collective agreements of solo self-employed persons declared that
> the prohibition does not apply to persons providing services through a digital labour platform.
> Instruments sort against one another as well as against mediators.

## 6. Verdict

**Minor revisions** for the volume as it stands, and I say that without reservation: the chapter is
well made, its central distinction is real, and nothing I have found makes it unpublishable in
August. **Major revisions** for the argument the chapter believes it is making, and those cannot be
done by 30 August.

**The single most important fix**, of the ones that fit the window: name expulsion in §5.3 (Part 5,
item 1) and price the bypass for a worker (item 2). Two paragraphs. They close the gap between the
chapter's theory and its own §9 instrument, and they are the difference between a framework that has
one bearer and a framework that admits it has two with unequal costs.

**The chapter's biggest genuine strength** is §6's structural argument against cooperativism, and I
want to be clear that I mean it. "A driver-owned dispatch platform still coordinates its drivers
through a system no single driver can bypass, so the question of standing within an irreducible
coordination outlives the question of who holds the title." My field has been arguing about
ownership for a decade on the assumption that ownership answers domination. Muldoon and Raekstad
concede that domination can survive a change of owner and then recommend the change of owner anyway.
This chapter says why the concession is fatal and not a caveat, and it says it in one sentence. That
sentence will outlive the rest of the chapter.

**The one thing only the author can supply** is a worked case in which the diagnostic is run on a
coordination whose dependent party is a person rather than a firm, and run to the point where the
answer is awkward. I have sketched ride-hail dispatch above; the author has the apparatus and I do
not. Until that case exists, the honest description of the chapter is that it is a framework for
platform-dependent firms with a claim to cover platform-dependent persons, and the claim rests on a
single clause in §6.

## 7. Citation behavior

**Would I cite it? Yes — three times, and never for anything about workers.**

The first and certain citation is §6's argument against cooperativism, in the paper I am currently
writing on courier cooperatives in two European cities. My co-authors and I have a section arguing
that member ownership has not delivered what its advocates promised, and our current explanation is
managerial: cooperatives professionalize, the board drifts, members disengage. That is Muldoon and
Raekstad's worry and it is weaker than what this chapter offers. The chapter's claim is structural —
ownership changes who sets a necessary mediator's rules and cannot make it leaveable — and it
explains the CoopCycle and Drivers Cooperative pattern better than managerial decay does. I would
cite it in that exact position, against the cooperativist literature, and it would change the
paragraph.

The second is against my own colleagues in labour law. There is a settled reflex in my field that
the platform-work directive's presumption of employment is the main event and that algorithmic
management is a subsidiary chapter of it. The chapter's §8 says, correctly, that standing over the
coordination varies independently of control over the infrastructure — and by extension of
employment status. A reclassified courier with a contract of employment still cannot see the
dispatcher. I have made that argument in seminars and lost it for want of a construct. "Coordinative
sovereignty" gives me the construct and a citable statement of the independence claim, and §8's
observation that Article 25 ties collective bargaining directly to algorithmic-management rights is
the textual hook I would use.

The third is the bypass test itself, cited narrowly and with the caveat in 3.2, in an argument
against the reformist habit of treating every platform dependence as a competition problem awaiting
an interoperability remedy. My field over-prescribes exit because exit is what the available legal
instruments deliver. The node/edge distinction is a clean way to say why that misfires, and I would
cite it as a diagnostic rather than as a theory of platform power.

**What I would not cite it for, and what would have to change.** I would not cite this chapter for
any claim about platform workers, because it makes none that its sources do not make better, and
because a labour reader who followed the citation would find workers appearing as illustrations of
opacity and beneficiaries of a directive. If the author wants that citation, the chapter needs the
ride-hail case worked to its awkward conclusion, expulsion in the theory rather than in the
instrument, and one instance somewhere in fifteen thousand words of workers acting rather than being
acted upon. Nothing in that list requires the author to change a single claim; it requires a
protagonist.

**Would I assign it? Yes, and with a warning attached.** Not to undergraduates — the register would
lose them and the ISV would not hold them. To my doctoral seminar on platform work, in week nine,
paired with Muldoon and Raekstad and with Dasgupta and colleagues, and I would set the question that
way: here is our argument, made rigorously, in a management vocabulary, for a management audience,
with the worker moved from the center to the margin. What survived the translation, what did not,
and would you make the trade? I expect the seminar to split, and I expect the students who work on
firms to say the chapter gained more than it lost. That is a genuinely useful hour and it is not a
flattering reason to be assigned. I would rather assign it for the argument.

   automated decision-making under Article 22, and rejected the human-in-the-loop defence on the
   facts as "not much more than a purely symbolic act." Cases brought with the App Drivers and
   Couriers Union and Worker Info Exchange. Confirmed against the court-case records and several
   independent legal notes. The author must obtain the ECLI numbers and the paragraph locator for
   the "symbolic act" characterization before quoting it; I have not seen the judgment text and Part
   5, item 4 is deliberately written without a quotation for that reason.

   between roughly $181m and $200m against about $12m in opposition. If the author uses this as the
   "platforms contest the instruments" example, cite Ballotpedia or the California Secretary of
