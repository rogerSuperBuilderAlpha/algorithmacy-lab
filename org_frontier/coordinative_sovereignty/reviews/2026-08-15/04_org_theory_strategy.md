# Review 04 — Henrik Sølvberg

**Manuscript:** *Algorithmacy and Sovereignty: Toward a Coordinative Sovereignty for the Platform Era*
(`chapter/chapter_v2.md`, 2026-08-14)
**Venue:** IGI Global, *Organizational Implications of Digital Sovereignty in the Age of AI*
**Date:** 15 August 2026
**Recommendation:** **Major revisions** — deliverable inside fifteen days, because every repair I ask for
draws on sources already in the reference list.

---

## 1. Who I am

I teach strategy and organization theory at a business school where the platform seminar is mine, and I have
spent fifteen years on the boundary of the firm: two papers on concurrent sourcing in component supply, one
on brokerage rents in freight forwarding that took three rounds at *Strategic Management Journal* before
Ryall and Sorenson's endogenous-tie result forced me to rewrite the identification, and a long, unhappy
consulting engagement with a mid-sized logistics software vendor whose entire order book ran through one
marketplace. I review for *SMJ*, *Organization Science*, and *Strategy Science*, and I have rejected three
papers this year for the same offence: a novelty claim staked against a literature the authors had not read.
I came to this chapter expecting a fourth. That expectation was wrong in an interesting way, and the way it
was wrong is the substance of this review. The authors have read the literature. They cite it. Obstfeld,
Ryall and Sorenson, Hahl and colleagues, Gu and Zhu, Sekar and Siddiq, Baldwin, Cutolo and Kenney — all
present, all in the reference list, several quoted accurately. What §5.2 does is name three neighbours
against which the novelty claim survives, and leave out the five against which it does not. That is a
different failure from ignorance, it is more embarrassing to defend at a conference, and it is fixable this
month.

I also came in believing two things the chapter has to overcome. First, that "platform dependence" is a
well-worked construct with an established strategy vocabulary, and that a new label earns its keep only by
predicting something the old one gets wrong. Second, that the interesting unit of analysis in platform
dependence is the function, not the firm — which is precisely §5.4's claim, and the reason I finished the
chapter a partial convert rather than a hostile one.

---

## 2. Step 0 — register and bar

The register is an impersonal academic chapter with no first person, and it holds throughout. I judged
against that bar and not against a journal-article bar. Masked self-citation, block quotation, and the
absence of the companion's formal results are venue facts and I have not counted them as defects. I have
also not asked the chapter to compute anything; §5 promises a plain counterfactual and delivers a plain
counterfactual.

What I judged hard: §5.2's comparison set and the novelty claim resting on it; §5.3's exit/voice mapping
onto platform economics; §5.4's portfolio claim and whether the four-way taxonomy survives into §7–§10;
§5.5's evidentiary status; and every strategy and economics citation, one by one. What I let pass: the
political theory in §6, the media theory in §2, the legal detail in §7 — other reviewers hold those. Where a
finding of mine bears on §6 or §8 I have said so briefly and moved on.

One thing I will say up front, because it should not get lost in what follows: **§5.4 is the best twenty
lines in the chapter and, to my knowledge, genuinely new to the strategy literature.** "A platform is a
portfolio of mediators" is a real correction to how platform dependence gets measured, and the sentence
"Managers who read their platform dependence as a single risk to be diversified miss this" is the sentence I
would put on a slide. Most of my complaints are that the chapter does not believe §5.4 hard enough to let it
govern §5.2, §5.5, §7, and §8.

## 3. Part 1 — the read

Ranked by damage.

### F1. [FIX NOW] The novelty claim in §5.2 is stated against a comparison set that excludes the five literatures that already run the operation — all of which the chapter cites elsewhere

**What the chapter claims.** "The test resembles three others, and the operation differs from each." The
three are brokerage research (Burt, 2004; Obstfeld, 2005), transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1975),
and essential facilities. "Each of these deletes or substitutes the mediator: they operate on a node. The
bypass test leaves the mediator in place and adds a tie: it operates on an edge." Then: "The neighboring
tests cannot ask the second question, and the reason is not oversight. Each holds the network of who may
deal with whom fixed."

**What fails.** That last sentence is false of four of the chapter's own sources, and arguably of a fifth.

*Obstfeld (2005).* The chapter files Obstfeld under "what a broker gains from standing between disconnected
parties." Obstfeld's paper is the standard citation for the opposite orientation. The *tertius iungens* is
the third who **joins** — a strategic behavioural orientation toward "introducing disconnected individuals
or facilitating new coordination between connected individuals," explicitly contrasted by Obstfeld against
the *tertius gaudens* of structural-hole theory. Obstfeld's entire apparatus is tie creation. Citing him as
an instance of a literature that "holds the network of who may deal with whom fixed" inverts his argument.
The chapter knows the distinction, because §3.1 deploys *tertius gaudens* correctly via Vallas and Schor.
That makes the §5.2 lumping worse, not better.

*Ryall and Sorenson (2007).* The chapter uses them for "when a broker is essential to the value a coalition
creates," which is accurate as far as it goes. It stops one result short. Ryall and Sorenson run the model
twice: once with relations exogenous, where a broker can hold an advantage under three conditions, and once
where "actors are allowed to form relations on the basis of their expectations of the future value of those
relations," in which case "profitable positions of intermediation only arise under strict assumptions of
unilateral action." The second run is endogenous tie formation. It is the bypass test, in a biform game,
nineteen years ago, with a sharper answer than the chapter's: when parties can form the direct tie
strategically, brokerage rents mostly vanish. A strategy reviewer who knows this paper will read §5.2 as
either unread or selectively read, and the chapter's own citation is what convicts it.

*Hahl, Kacperczyk and Davis (2016).* The chapter uses them for a perception caveat: brokered parties who do
not see the gap do not try to close it. Their actual framing is that "returns to these brokers can vary
depending on whether alters can credibly threaten to disintermediate the broker and close the structural
hole." Their working paper circulated under the title *Knowledge Asymmetry and Disintermediation in
Brokerage*. Conditioning brokerage returns on the alters' ability to close the tie is the bypass test as a
strategic-organization construct. The chapter has it as a footnote and needs it as a rival.

*Gu and Zhu (2021); Sekar and Siddiq (2026).* There is a modelling and field-experimental literature called,
in so many words, **platform disintermediation**, which asks what a platform's position is worth when the
two sides can reach each other directly. Gu and Zhu run a randomised controlled trial in a freelance
marketplace and find that when trust rises far enough, "the two sides may circumvent the intermediary to
avoid the intermediary's fees." Sekar and Siddiq model exactly the same question and derive pricing
remedies. The chapter cites both. It cites Gu and Zhu in §5.2 itself, three paragraphs after asserting that
no neighbouring test can ask the second question, as a caveat about necessity not being fixed. That is the
neighbouring literature, sitting inside the paragraph that denies it exists.

*Baldwin (2008).* Baldwin models production as a task network whose links are transfers, and asks where the
system should be **cut**. Thin crossing points are locations in the link structure. That is a normative
operation on ties, not on nodes.

**Why it fails for me.** I referee this exact move. An author defines an operation, names three neighbours
chosen so the operation looks new, and asserts that the neighbours "cannot" perform it. The correct claim
here is available and defensible, and the chapter is one paragraph from it. It is not that the operation is
unavailable to the neighbours. It is that the neighbours run it **on the mediator's returns, from outside**,
and the chapter runs it **on the dependent party's option set, from inside**, and converts the answer into a
course of action rather than a prediction about rent. The chapter half-says this already — "none of the
three returns a strategy to the party inside the coordination, and none of the three is run by that party" —
and then buries it under a formal-novelty claim that will not survive a strategy reviewer.

**Damage.** For the IGI volume: moderate, since the volume's readers are not brokerage theorists. For the
*Organization Theory* submission in January: fatal at desk. I would reject on this alone, and I would name
Ryall and Sorenson in the first paragraph of the report.

**Fix.** Retreat from the formal claim to the standpoint claim, and enlarge the comparison set to include
the disintermediation literature. Paste-ready text in Part 3.

### F2. [FIX NOW] §3.3 and §5.3 make opposite claims about what multihoming implies, and the contradiction runs straight through §5.5

**What the chapter claims.** §3.3, correctly following Armstrong (2006): "where one side single-homes … and
the other multi-homes across several, each platform holds a monopoly over access to its own single-homing
users and extracts from the multi-homing side, which must pay every platform to reach the users behind it."
§5.3, citing Eisenmann, Parker and Van Alstyne (2006): "where an actor can hold more than one platform at
once, the bottleneck is contestable."

**What fails.** These are contradictory. In Armstrong's competitive bottleneck, multihoming by the dependent
side is the **condition of extraction**, not the relief from it. Contestability requires the *other* side to
multi-home. The chapter states the correct version in §3.3 and the folk version in §5.3, one section apart,
and then builds §5.5 on a sector where the dependent side multi-homes about as hard as any sector in Europe.
Independent hotels list on Booking and Expedia and HRS simultaneously; travellers, per search, largely do
not. Armstrong's model says extraction persists. It did.

**Why it fails for me.** This is the paragraph where a platform economist stops reading. It is also the
paragraph that makes F3 possible, because a reader who believes §5.3 will not notice that Armstrong already
predicts §5.5's outcome.

**Damage.** High and cheap. The fix is two sentences and it strengthens the chapter, because the corrected
version supplies a second reason why hotels' multihoming did not restore exit.

### F3. [FIX NOW] §5.5 does not discriminate the bypass reading from a third reading the chapter itself supplies two sections earlier

**What the chapter claims.** Two readings are set against the OTA decade. Reading one treats the agency as
"one substitutable intermediary," predicting that removing the parity clause erodes its position. Reading
two treats it as a portfolio, predicting the necessary function holds and dependence does not fall. Share
rose 19.7% to 29.6%, direct fell 57.6% to 50.9%, and the chapter concludes: "It is still enough to sort the
two readings."

**What fails.** Between the two readings sits a third that the chapter has already introduced. Armstrong's
competitive bottleneck predicts persistent extraction from the multihoming side regardless of parity
clauses, because the clause is a price-restraint instrument and the bottleneck is an access instrument.
Remove the clause, leave the single-homing traveller behind the gate, and nothing about the extraction
changes. That reading is not the chapter's construct, it requires no necessary/contingent distinction, it
requires no bypass counterfactual, and it makes the same prediction. Two hypotheses that agree on the only
datum cannot be sorted by that datum.

The chapter's own hedge misses this. It concedes inference strength — "narrower than a natural experiment,"
"owes something to mobile search and to consolidation" — and then recovers with "It is still enough to sort
the two readings." The concession is about how *strong* the test is. The problem is whether it
**discriminates**, and a weak test that discriminates is worth printing where a strong one that does not is
not.

**Is saying so enough?** No. Not because the chapter is insufficiently modest, but because the modesty is
aimed at the wrong joint.

**Why it fails for me.** §5.5 is the chapter's only empirical moment and the one passage I would have quoted.
As written I cannot quote it, because the first question at a seminar is "isn't that just the competitive
bottleneck?" and the chapter has no answer on the page.

**Damage.** High. But there is a repair that costs one paragraph and makes §5.5 better than it is now, and
it comes out of the chapter's own Gu and Zhu citation. See F7 and Part 3.

### F4. [FIX NOW] The four-way taxonomy is three levels of analysis presented as one flat list, which is why the chapter reverts to a binary after §5.4

**What the chapter claims.** §5.2 introduces necessary, contingent, reducible, and partial. §5.4 exhibits
"one firm, four kinds of mediator at once." Key Terms defines all four.

**What happens after.** §5.5, the flagship case, returns what is by §5.2's own definition a **partial**
mediator — real integrating work alongside a bypassable gate — and never uses the word. §7's instrument
table has two columns. §8's decision procedure has two branches. §9's two-step design has two categories,
"exit-seeking against contingent gates, voice-building against necessary ones." §10 is binary. "Reducible"
appears in §5.2 and §5.4 and never again.

**Why it fails for me, and the charitable reading the chapter should adopt.** The reversion is not
sloppiness. It is the taxonomy telling the truth about itself. If the diagnostic classifies *functions*, as
§5.4 insists it does, then at the function level there are exactly two verdicts: the function survives the
restored tie or it does not. "Partial" is not a function-level verdict, it is a **firm-level aggregate** over
functions with mixed verdicts. "Reducible" is not a bypass verdict at all, it is a **§5.1 verdict** — a
mediator that was never binding, so no tie needs restoring. The chapter is running three levels of analysis
and printing them as a flat list of four kinds.

Say that, and the taxonomy is coherent and the binary in §7–§10 is correct rather than a lapse. Leave it as
is, and a reviewer counts four terms introduced and two used.

**Damage.** Moderate for the volume, high for the *OT* version, where a taxonomy that the paper's own later
sections do not use is a standing invitation to reject. The fix is a level-of-analysis sentence in §5.2 and
one word in §5.5. Cheap.

### F5. [FIX NOW] The unit of classification is unstable: firm, function, and function-by-counterparty-segment are used interchangeably

**What the chapter claims.** §5.2: "the diagnostic sorts functions for a given dependent party and does not
sort platforms once and for all." §5.4: "the diagnostic classifies functions, not firms."

**What fails.** §5.4's marketplace example lists four functions and assigns four verdicts, of which one —
"It matches demand to supply, real integrating work that buyers can bypass for goods they already know,
which is partial" — is not a function split at all. It is one function classified two ways by **buyer
segment**. §5.5 does the same thing and it is the load-bearing move of the case: "Restore the capability of
direct dealing for a traveler who already knows the hotel exists… Restore the same capability for a traveler
who would never have learned…" That is demand aggregation, one function, split across traveller types.

The chapter then reports the result as "a split that names two different instruments" — parity clause versus
aggregation — which is a *function* split. Two different splits are being run and reported as one.

**Why this matters operationally.** A hotel manager reading §8 needs to know what to weight. If aggregation
is necessary for first-time discoverers and contingent for repeat guests, the strategy is not "voice against
aggregation." It is: measure the repeat share, push it up, and let the necessary residue shrink. That is a
different Monday from the one §8 hands over, and it is a better one.

**Damage.** Moderate for the volume, high for the measurement program §9 promises, since a construct whose
unit of classification floats between three levels cannot be operationalised. Fix: state the unit as
(function × dependent party × counterparty segment) in one sentence in §5.2, and use it consistently in
§5.5.

### F6. [FIX IF TIME] The node/edge divergence is produced by an asymmetric ceteris paribus, and the honest distinction is constraint provenance

**What the chapter claims.** Franchised dealer: "Delete the dealer, and under franchise law no other party
may deliver a new vehicle in that territory, so the dealer is non-substitutable… Restore the
manufacturer-to-buyer tie, and the dealer's position evaporates." App Store payment gate: node test says
necessary, edge test says contingent, "on the same function, in the same firm."

**What fails.** In both demonstrations the node test runs **with the constraint in force** and the edge test
runs **with the constraint lifted**. Of course they disagree. Run the node test with franchise law repealed
and the dealer is substitutable; run it with sideloading permitted and another payment rail reaches the
users. The divergence is not between operating on a node and operating on an edge. It is between holding a
legal or contractual constraint fixed and relaxing it.

The chapter is closer to seeing this than it lets on: "The bypass test asks which of those constraints is
load-bearing." That sentence is the real content of §5.2, and it is a good one. What it describes is not a
new graph operation. It is a **classification of constraints by provenance** — architectural and economic
constraints that survive any legal change, against legal and contractual constraints that a regulator, a
court, or a renegotiation can lift.

**Why it fails for me.** The provenance framing is stronger, not weaker. It tells the dependent firm which
constraints to litigate and which to plan around. It maps directly onto the DMA line the chapter already
has: "The Digital Markets Act's anti-steering obligations are the edge test run by a regulator." And it
survives F1, because no brokerage or disintermediation paper classifies constraints that way.

**Damage.** Moderate now, larger later. If the companion paper's formal apparatus is built on node/edge as
the load-bearing distinction, this finding is upstream of the formalism and worth resolving before that
paper is fixed.

### F7. [FIX IF TIME] Necessity has no stock/flow distinction, and the chapter's own Gu and Zhu citation shows why it needs one

**What the chapter claims.** Necessity rests on "integrating work that the direct tie cannot reproduce."
Then, as a caveat: Gu and Zhu find that the platform's own trust-building "raises the rate at which parties
transact around it, which gives a mediator a reason to withhold the very integration that would make it
necessary."

**What fails.** Read that caveat against the definition and it produces a contradiction. In Gu and Zhu,
trust-provision *is* integrating work, and performing it well makes the platform **more** bypassable, not
less. So integrating work does not uniformly confer necessity. Two kinds have to be told apart:

- **Re-performed integration**, which must be supplied at every transaction and therefore cannot be carried
  off: clearing and settlement, matching many orders into one price, real-time dispatch against a live pool.
  The chapter's clearinghouse and exchange examples are of this kind.
- **Transferable integration**, which produces a durable asset the parties keep: trust, reputation,
  discovery, an introduction. Once produced, the next transaction can route around it.

Demand aggregation — the chapter's flagship "necessary" function — is the transferable kind. That is exactly
what C. K. Anderson's billboard effect asserts: the introduction is a stock the hotel retains. So §5.5
classifies as necessary a function whose necessity, by the chapter's own Gu and Zhu logic, decays with each
repeat guest. And the OTA's countermeasures — parity clauses, masked guest contact details, the whole
apparatus of preventing transfer — are Gu and Zhu's mechanism operating in the hotel sector, unrecognised by
the chapter as such.

**What this buys.** A discriminating prediction, which is what F3 says §5.5 lacks. Armstrong's competitive
bottleneck predicts uniform persistent extraction across all hotels. The bypass test with a stock/flow
refinement predicts something Armstrong does not: OTA dependence should erode **within the repeat-guest
segment** and persist **within first-time discovery**, so that hotels with high repeat share should show
falling OTA dependence over the decade while hotels dependent on discovery should show rising dependence,
with the sector aggregate moving with the mix. That is a within-sector, cross-sectional prediction, it is
testable against distribution data, and no rival in the chapter makes it.

**Damage.** As written, a live inconsistency between §5.2's definition and §5.2's own caveat. Repaired, it is
the chapter's best theoretical contribution after §5.4. If fifteen days will not carry the empirics — and
they will not — state the prediction and hand it to §9's research program. Draft text in Part 3.

### F8. [FIX IF TIME] The franchised-dealer example violates the portfolio principle the chapter states two subsections later

**What the chapter claims.** "A franchised car dealer relays a vehicle from manufacturer to buyer and
integrates nothing; it occupies the coordination because franchise law forbids the manufacturer to sell
direct."

**What fails.** "Integrates nothing" is false as stated, and an organization theorist will say so before the
end of the sentence. Dealers hold and finance inventory, absorb demand variability at the local level, take
trade-ins whose valuation requires local knowledge, perform warranty work under a manufacturer mandate, and
carry relationship-specific investments that are, historically, part of why dealer-protection statutes exist
at all. A dealer is the chapter's own **partial mediator**: a contingent sales gate bolted to a bundle of
necessary local functions. Using it as the pure case of contingency contradicts §5.4's portfolio principle
and hands a reviewer a free counterexample.

**Why the fix improves the chapter.** Narrow the claim to the dealer's exclusive right of sale, and add the
one case that proves it: Tesla. Where state franchise law permitted direct sale, the sales function
dissolved; where it did not, the dealer survived while performing the same non-sales functions everywhere.
That is the chapter's counterfactual with an observed treatment and observed variation across jurisdictions,
and it is a better demonstration than the App Store because the constraint varies while the technology does
not.

**Damage.** Low as a defect, high as a missed opportunity. This is the free win in the chapter.

### F9. [FIX IF TIME] Cutolo and Kenney are excluded from the comparison set they belong in

**What the chapter claims.** "None of the three returns a strategy to the party inside the coordination, and
none of the three is run by that party."

**What fails.** True of the three named. Not true of the closest neighbour, which the chapter cites three
separate times (§1, §3.2, §8). Cutolo and Kenney (2021) is a paper about power asymmetry between platforms
and ecosystem members that, in the authors' own framing, provides "guidance on how these firms should
compete." It is a strategy-to-the-dependent-party paper drawing on power-dependence theory. Leaving it out
of the comparison set while citing it for the problem statement is the move a referee notices.

**The differentiation is available.** Cutolo and Kenney give the dependent firm a *risk taxonomy* and
generic responses. The chapter gives it a *classification rule* that sorts the firm's dependencies before
choosing a response, and predicts that the wrong response against the wrong mediator type wastes effort. Say
that in two sentences and the chapter is safe. Leave it, and a reviewer supplies it as an objection.

**Damage.** Moderate at the volume, high at *OT*.

### F10. [FIX IF TIME] Baldwin's thin crossing points decorate rather than work

**What the chapter claims.** For a partial verdict, "Modularity research supplies the criterion for where the
two come apart: cut at the thin crossing points, where the interface between functions is narrow enough that
separating them costs little (Baldwin, 2008)."

**Verified and accurate as paraphrase.** Baldwin locates transactions at thin crossing points of the task
network because mundane transaction costs are low there, and those points are where firms and industries
split apart.

**What fails.** The criterion is never applied. §5.4 asserts four function boundaries in the marketplace
example without showing any of them to be thin. §5.5, where a partial verdict is actually reached, does not
invoke Baldwin at all — and the parity-clause/aggregation boundary is the obvious place to test him. Is that
a thin crossing point? Plausibly yes: the clause is a contractual term severable from the listing, and
national enforcement severed it. Say so and Baldwin does work. Leave it, and a modularity reader reads
decoration.

**Damage.** Low, but it is one of only three org-theory citations in §5.4 and it should earn its place.

### F11. [FIX IF TIME] Parmigiani is the right citation attached to the wrong example, and the right example is in §5.5

**What the chapter claims.** "Firms that both make and buy the same input are not hedging between two
options but pursuing a distinct strategy with its own logic (Parmigiani, 2007), and the same holds for an
actor that fights one function of a platform while depending on another."

**What fails.** Parmigiani's phenomenon is defined by the *same good*: firms "simultaneously make and buy
the same good," and her finding is that concurrent sourcing is a distinct choice rather than a point on a
make/buy continuum. Fighting function A while depending on function B is not concurrent sourcing; the inputs
differ, and the analogy breaks on the feature that defines the construct.

**The right example is already in the chapter.** A hotel selling the same room night through the OTA and
through its own site is concurrent sourcing in the distribution register: same good, two channels, held
simultaneously, and — per Parmigiani — a distinct strategy rather than a way station toward one channel or
the other. That reading also strengthens §5.5, because it explains why "trying to leave" was the wrong frame
for what hotels were doing: they were not migrating, they were concurrently sourcing, and the observed share
shift is a mix shift within a stable dual-channel strategy.

**Damage.** Low as an error, moderate as a lost argument.

### F12. [FIX IF TIME] J. D. Thompson is cited for a label whose content contradicts the chapter's core claim

**What the chapter claims.** "The platform occupies the mediating position that J. D. Thompson (1967) named
a mediating technology, linking parties who would otherwise never transact."

**What fails.** Accurate as a label, and then dropped. Thompson's apparatus pairs each technology with an
interdependence type and a coordination mode: the mediating technology carries **pooled** interdependence
and is coordinated by **standardization**, precisely because its clients do not interact with each other.
Reciprocal interdependence belongs to the **intensive** technology and is coordinated by mutual adjustment.
The chapter's central claim — that the platform "binds the two parties into a joint outcome that neither
could produce alone" — asserts reciprocal interdependence. In Thompson's scheme that is not what a mediating
technology carries.

**Why this is worth a sentence rather than a scolding.** The tension is the chapter's actual finding in
Thompson's vocabulary: the algorithmic platform wears the mediating technology's form (many clients,
standardized rules, no client-to-client interaction) while producing intensive-technology interdependence
(a joint determination over a specific pair). Thompson has no cell for that, and saying so places the
chapter inside the classical literature rather than beside it.

**Damage.** Low for the volume. For the January *Organization Theory* submission this is the strongest
available framing and I would build the theory section on it.

### F13. [TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD] The TCE reading stops at 1975, and Williamson's own answer to "voice under bilateral dependency" goes unmet

The chapter reads transaction cost economics as the make-or-buy decision and nothing else. Williamson's
later work is where the chapter's own problem lives: bilateral dependency in which neither party can exit
cheaply, and the response is not a boundary move but **private ordering** — credible commitments, hostages,
governance structures that make continued dealing self-enforcing where exit is unavailable. That is a
sixty-year-old theory of voice under irreducible dependence, and "coordinative sovereignty" has to say what
it adds to it. The plausible answer is asymmetry: Williamson's hostage exchange assumes two parties who can
each post a bond, and the platform-dependent seller has nothing to post. Also that private ordering assumes
a negotiated contract, and §3 establishes that platform enrollment has no negotiation. Both answers are
good. Neither is on the page. This one will not fit in fifteen days; it belongs at the front of the *OT*
paper.

### F14. [TOO LATE — CARRY FORWARD] The diagnostic is never run on a case it might fail, and "comparable cost" has no measurement rule

Every case in §5 comes back with the verdict the argument needs. The dealer is contingent, the clearinghouse
is necessary, the App Store gate is contingent, the OTA splits exactly along the line the chapter drew in
§5.4. A taxonomy that has never been run on a case where the analyst did not already know the answer is a
vocabulary, not a diagnostic.

Underneath that sits a measurement problem the chapter should name: the counterfactual turns on whether the
parties "could reach each other at comparable cost," and no rule is given for computing comparable. Every
contested classification in practice will turn on that phrase. Essential-facilities doctrine has spent
thirty years on the equivalent question and has not settled it. §9 should say so, and say that the
classification rule is the first thing the empirical program has to make inter-rater reliable — which is a
better research direction than three of the four currently listed.

## 4. Part 2 — style and slop audit

The register is intact and the prose is better than most edited-volume chapters. Four flags, all in §5.

**(a) The four-fold appositive metronome, §5.4.** Four clauses in one paragraph closing on the same
construction: "which is necessary integrating work," "which is contingent," "which is necessary," "which is
partial," "which is contingent," "which is reducible." Six, actually. The paragraph reads as a generated
list, which is a shame, because its content is the chapter's best.

*Rewrite:* break the run by varying where the verdict lands.

> A payment network authorizes transactions, and the determination depends jointly on the merchant's request
> and the issuer's approval: neither could produce it alone, which is integrating work no direct tie
> reproduces. The same network's rule against steering the customer to a cheaper method at the till
> integrates nothing. Its force comes from the network's contract with the merchant, and a contract can be
> struck down. A large marketplace fulfills orders through logistics no seller-to-buyer tie reproduces at
> scale. Its matching function is necessary for a buyer who does not know what she wants and bypassable for
> one who does. Its gate over which seller a buyer sees is a toll, and its resale of goods it has bought is
> a plain conduit that a brand able to reach customers directly routes around. One firm, four verdicts.

**(b) "The test resembles three others, and the operation differs from each."** Antithesis machine, and the
sentence that commits F1. Replaced in Part 3.

**(c) Refrain: "the standing gap named above."** Appears verbatim in §6 and §8, plus "the standing gap Ananny
and Crawford name" in §3.2 and "supplying the standing gap" in §8. Four occurrences of one phrase, two of
them identical. Vary the second: "Platform coordination breaks that assumption at the point Ananny and

## 5. Part 3 — paste-ready revisions

### R1 — replaces the §5.2 comparison paragraph beginning "The test resembles three others…"

> Four literatures ask a version of this question, and they ask it from a different position. Brokerage
> research asks what a broker's position yields: Burt (1992, 2004) on the returns to spanning a structural
> hole, Obstfeld (2005) on the broker who instead joins the parties it stands between, and Ryall and
> Sorenson (2007), who formalize when a broker is essential to a coalition's value and show that when actors
> form relations on their own expectations of value, profitable intermediation survives only under strict
> assumptions. Hahl et al. (2016) make the alters' capacity to close the tie the condition on brokerage
> returns. A platform-disintermediation literature runs the counterfactual empirically and analytically:
> Gu and Zhu (2021) find that a marketplace's own trust-building raises the rate at which the two sides
> transact around it, and Sekar and Siddiq (2026) model the platform's pricing response. Transaction cost
> economics asks whether a firm should perform a transaction internally or buy it (Williamson, 1975), and
> competition law's essential-facilities doctrine asks whether an excluded rival could viably duplicate the
> facility it seeks access to.
>
> Each of these asks what the mediator's position is worth, and each asks it from outside the coordination:
> Ryall and Sorenson from the analyst's seat, Gu and Zhu from the platform's, a court from the tribunal's.
> The diagnostic here asks the same counterfactual from inside, on behalf of the party that cannot leave,
> and converts each answer into a course of action rather than a prediction about rent or a finding of
> legality. It also asks it function by function rather than of the mediator as a whole, which is what
> section 5.4 develops. Given a mediator, a dependent party, and a set of rules, the question is which of
> the mediator's functions would survive the removal of the prohibition on direct contact, and what the
> party should therefore do about each.
>
> The operation has a second use the neighboring literatures do not need: it sorts constraints by
> provenance. A node test run under a law returns a different verdict from the same test run with the law
> repealed, and the difference identifies which constraint is load-bearing. A franchised car dealer's
> exclusive right of sale rests on a statute, so deleting the dealer under franchise law returns a mediator
> no other party may replace, while restoring the manufacturer-to-buyer tie dissolves the exclusivity
> entirely; Tesla's direct sales, permitted in some jurisdictions and blocked in others, are that
> counterfactual with the treatment observed. The dealer's other functions — inventory, trade-ins, warranty
> work — survive either way, which makes the dealer a partial mediator and not a pure case of contingency.

### R2 — replaces the multihoming sentence in §5.3

> Platform economics locates the same fact in the homing pattern, and the direction is the opposite of the
> intuitive one. Multihoming by the dependent side does not contest the bottleneck; in Armstrong's (2006)
> competitive bottleneck it is the condition under which the bottleneck extracts, since a platform holding
> the single-homing side can charge every multihoming actor that needs to reach it. Contestability requires
> the *other* side to hold more than one platform at once (Eisenmann et al., 2006). An actor that responds
> to a necessary mediator by adding a second one has multiplied its tolls without opening an exit.

### R3 — replaces the §5.5 paragraph beginning "What the decade covers…"

> Three readings have to be told apart, not two. Treat the agency as one substitutable intermediary and the
> removal of the clause should erode its position. Treat the sector through the competitive bottleneck and
> the outcome follows without any necessary/contingent distinction: travelers hold one search habit, hotels
> list on every agency, and a price restraint is not the instrument that holds that asymmetry in place, so
> removing it changes the price term and not the access term. The portfolio reading agrees with the second
> on this decade. The observed series therefore refutes the first reading and does not separate the other
> two, and the concessions compound it: the 2024 judgment postdates the last year of the data, the increase
> owes something to mobile search and to consolidation among the agencies, and the window spans a pandemic
> that reallocated distribution in both directions.
>
> What separates the portfolio reading from the bottleneck reading is a prediction the bottleneck does not
> make. Demand aggregation is integrating work of a transferable kind: the introduction it performs is a
> stock the hotel keeps, unlike the clearing a settlement system must re-perform at every transaction. Gu
> and Zhu (2021) show the consequence on a marketplace, where the intermediary's own trust-building raises
> the rate at which the two sides deal directly, and the agencies' countermeasures — parity clauses, masked
> guest contact details, restrictions on post-stay solicitation — are that mechanism recognized and resisted.
> The portfolio reading therefore predicts divergence *within* the sector: dependence should fall where
> repeat guests dominate and rise where first-time discovery does, with the aggregate tracking the mix. The
> bottleneck reading predicts uniform persistence. Distribution data disaggregated by repeat share would
> separate them, and the 2024 judgment sets up a second window in which to run the comparison. The decade

### R4 — insert in §5.2, after the four kinds are introduced

## 6. Verdict

**Major revisions**, deliverable by 30 August. Every repair I have asked for uses a source the chapter
already cites, and the four I have marked FIX NOW total perhaps eight hundred words of replacement text,
supplied above.

I want to be exact about the calibration, since two prior panels said minor. For this volume and this
readership, the chapter as it stands would survive; the IGI audience will not have read Ryall and Sorenson.
For the January *Organization Theory* submission the same §5.2 would be desk-rejected, and I would write the
rejection. The gap between those two judgments is F1, and F1 costs one paragraph.

**The single most important fix.** Rewrite the §5.2 comparison paragraph (R1). Retreat from "the neighboring
tests cannot ask this" to "the neighboring tests ask it about the mediator's returns, from outside; this
asks it about the dependent party's options, from inside, function by function." Enlarge the set to include
Obstfeld properly, Ryall and Sorenson's endogenous-tie result, Hahl et al.'s disintermediation condition,
and the platform-disintermediation literature. The novelty that survives is smaller, defensible, and enough.

**The chapter's biggest genuine strength.** §5.4. "A platform is a portfolio of mediators" is a correct and,
so far as I know, unclaimed correction to how platform dependence is measured in strategy research, which
almost universally treats dependence as a firm-level scalar — revenue share through the platform — and
therefore cannot distinguish a firm that is eighty percent dependent on a contingent gate from one eighty
percent dependent on a necessary function. Those two firms face different strategies and the literature
gives them the same number. That is a publishable claim on its own and the chapter should know it is the
best thing in the manuscript.

**The one thing only the author can supply.** A case the diagnostic was run on *before* the answer was
known, ideally one where it returned an uncomfortable verdict. Every case in §5 confirms. If the companion
paper's classifier has been run on a set of coordinations and disagreed with the analyst on any of them,
that disagreement is worth more to this chapter than any additional theory, and one sentence reporting it
would answer F14. Only the authors know whether it exists.

## 7. Citation behavior

**Would I cite it?** Yes, and I can name the paper. I have a project on measurement in platform-dependence
research, arguing that the field's standard operationalization — revenue concentration through a single
platform — is misspecified because it aggregates over functions with different exit properties. I would cite
this chapter for §5.4 and for the necessary/contingent distinction as a *conceptual* basis for
disaggregating that measure, and I would cite §5.5 as an illustration that aggregate dependence and
function-level dependence can move in opposite directions. That is a real citation in a real paper, not a
courtesy.

**Against what?** Against Cutolo and Kenney (2021), who established platform dependence as a strategic
condition and gave it a risk taxonomy but treat the dependence as a property of the relationship rather than
of each function within it. And against the multihoming literature, whose implicit prescription —
diversify — the chapter shows to be inapplicable to a whole class of dependencies. That is a fair fight and
the chapter wins parts of it.

**What I would not cite it for, as written.** §5.2's bypass test as a novel diagnostic operation. Not
because the idea is wrong, but because citing it would expose me. If I write "the bypass test asks a
question brokerage theory and transaction cost economics cannot," a referee who knows Ryall and Sorenson's
second result will ask me why I believe that, and I will have no answer that does not amount to "the chapter
said so." I would instead cite Gu and Zhu for the disintermediation counterfactual and the chapter for the
function-level application of it, which is a demotion the authors will not enjoy and which R1 would prevent.

**What would change that.** R1, or anything equivalent to it. Confront the disintermediation literature by
name, concede that the counterfactual is not new as an operation, and stake the claim on the standpoint and
the unit — asked by the dependent party, about a function, and converted into a strategy. I would cite that
without hesitation, because the claim would be true.

**Would I assign it?** Yes, twice over, and the second use is the one I would tell the authors about.

First, on the reading list for my doctoral platform-strategy seminar, in the week on dependence and value
capture, paired with Cutolo and Kenney (2021) and Gu and Zhu (2021). It is the clearest statement I have
seen of why "can this firm leave?" is the wrong unit of question, and it is written well enough that
students will finish it, which is not true of most of what I assign that week.

Second, in the methods seminar, as a worked example of **how a novelty claim gets constructed by choice of
comparison set**. I would hand students §5.2 and the reference list, ask them to find every cited work that
performs the operation §5.2 says its neighbours cannot perform, and give them twenty minutes. Most will find
three. The exercise teaches something no lecture does, and it is not a hostile use — it is the use a paper
earns when its own bibliography contains the answer to its own novelty claim. If the authors would rather I
assigned the revised §5.2 instead, R1 is above.
