## Part 1 — Theory and structure, §1–§10

### §1 Introduction

The opening compliance-vs-capture gap is well drawn and the thesis is legible: sovereignty has always been paired with a communicative competence, the platform introduces a coordination the prior competences cannot govern, and a new sovereignty is needed. One political-theory flag arrives early and shadows the whole chapter. The chapter treats "sovereignty" as **standing to hold power to account** (see §10: "Sovereignty has always been the standing to hold power to account through the medium in which power was exercised"). That is a revisionist, subject-side definition. The Bodin–Hobbes–Schmitt–Krasner tradition the chapter cites elsewhere (§2, Krasner 1999) defines sovereignty as the *possession and exercise of supreme, final authority* — the un-commanded commander. The chapter is quietly working in the **popular/democratic** register (the people's standing *over* governance) while borrowing the vocabulary of the **authority** register. That slippage is not fatal, but it is unowned, and it is what lets the Bodin error in §5.3 through the door. A referee will demand one sentence, early, that pins the register: coordinative sovereignty uses "sovereignty" in the popular sense of the standing of the governed, not the Bodinian sense of supreme authority.

### §2 Sovereignty and its sensibilities (oracy / literacy / digital)

The oracy–literacy–digital nesting is handled with care, and §2.2's "the same properties that let the state govern let its subjects contest it" is exactly the right move for a democratic-sovereignty argument (Habermas, Anderson, Scott are used accurately). No political-theory wince here. Minor register slip: "Sovereignty is usually sorted by what it governs" (l.58) is agentless passive with the agents (scholars) dropped even though four are cited in the same sentence — flip to "Scholars usually sort sovereignty by…" (see Part 2).

### §3 The platform turn

The Stark & Vanden Broeck co-optation framing and the Selznick (1949) contrast ("the platform-enrolled gain no seat and no share, only a determination") are used accurately and land well. §3's discipline — separating coordination *logic* (old), *topology* (old, Simmel/Burt/Obstfeld), and the *opaque + interested + adaptive mediator* (new) — is the strongest analytic passage in the chapter and pre-empts the "hierarchy in new clothes" objection honestly. §3.3's competitive-bottleneck rent argument (Armstrong & Wright, Rochet & Tirole) is a genuine value-add: it grounds the domination claim in political economy rather than assertion. No accuracy problem. The transparency point ("a transparent bottleneck is still a bottleneck") is correct but here begins a repetition that runs across §3.2, §3.3, §6, §7, and §8 — see Part 2.

### §4 Algorithmacy

Not primarily political theory, but two things bear on the normative argument. First, the seller vignette runs two full paragraphs (the marketplace seller, then "one morning her orders halve") illustrating the *same* three-part competence; one of them is enough (Part 3). Second — and this matters for §6 — §4 quietly shifts the **bearer** of the competence between a *person* (the seller, the gig worker) and a *firm* (the SME, "the actors best placed to model the system are the large ones"). That ambiguity about who holds algorithmacy, a person or an organization, propagates into coordinative sovereignty and is worth resolving (see §6).

### §5 The diagnostic (§5.1–§5.2)

The factorization/bypass machinery is not political theory, and the chapter is appropriately modest that the formal measures live in the companion work. One is/ought caution to carry into §5.3: the diagnostic establishes a *causal-structural* fact (does the mediator survive the restored direct tie), and the chapter then reads a *normative* prescription off it (seek exit vs build voice). Causal necessity is not normative necessity. A natural monopoly is causally necessary and the standard political response is *not* "voice within it" but public ownership or utility regulation. The chapter needs to mark that the bypass test tells you what is *structurally* possible, not what is *politically* warranted — otherwise §5.3's clean binary forecloses a third response the chapter itself endorses in §7 (below).

### §5.3 Exit and voice — the hinge (heaviest weight)

This is where the argument turns and where a political-theory referee will push hardest. Four issues, in ascending severity.

**(a) The Hirschman mapping is only half-earned.** "Exit maps onto bypassability, voice onto necessity" (l.401–402) is neat, and the exit-half is genuinely earned: bypassability *is* the availability of the direct tie, which *is* Hirschman's exit. The voice-half is looser. Necessity does not *produce* voice; it merely *forecloses exit*, leaving voice as the residual. "Voice onto necessity" states an entailment the chapter does not have — voice is available against contingent mediators too, and, more damagingly, necessity is exactly the condition under which Hirschman feared voice would be *least* effective. The chapter's gloss — "Hirschman himself observed that monopoly restricts exit and so elevates voice" (l.400–401) — is the rosy half of Hirschman. His deeper point in the monopoly chapter is the **lazy monopoly**: a monopolist may *welcome* the exit of its most quality-conscious, voice-prone members, so the absence of exit does not reliably strengthen voice and can leave it feeble. A necessary mediator faces *less* competitive pressure to heed voice, not more. The chapter should confront the lazy-monopoly complication head-on: necessity is precisely where voice is hardest to make effective, which *raises* the stakes of the institution-building in §7 rather than lowering them. Presented as-is, the mapping reads as though necessity is good news for voice; Hirschman's own analysis says the opposite.

**(b) Loyalty is dropped, and lock-in is misfiled.** Hirschman's third term, **loyalty**, is the mechanism that holds exit in check and thereby *activates* voice. The chapter drops it, and in doing so mis-describes lock-in. Lock-in (multihoming costs, sunk investment, an app store's control of its users) holds actors *in* the coordination the way loyalty does — but it is *involuntary retention*, the opposite of the civic loyalty Hirschman meant, which produces neither healthy exit nor healthy voice. Naming this would sharpen the chapter: what looks like Hirschmanian loyalty in the platform case is coerced retention, which is why voice does not automatically follow from the loss of exit.

**(c) The exit/voice binary is too thin a normative palette — the missing third response.** Hirschman's frame assumes the organization's *ownership is fixed*; his members can leave or complain, not re-own. Lift that assumption and the necessary-mediator case opens onto a third response that is neither exit nor voice-as-complaint: **socialization of the mediator** — public utility, cooperative conversion, commons stewardship. This is the classic answer to a necessary monopoly, and it is exactly what Muldoon & Raekstad (2022) recommend (cooperative ownership) and what §7 of this very chapter surveys (platform cooperativism, data trusts). Yet §5.3 and §6 compress the necessary-mediator response to "only voice remains," which quietly demotes the strongest remedy to a footnote. "Voice" then has to carry weight it cannot — participation-within-someone-else's-firm — when the political tradition offers a stouter option: change who owns the necessary thing. The chapter should distinguish three responses, not two: exit (contingent mediators), voice-within-private-ownership, and transformation-of-ownership (necessary mediators as candidates for utility/commons treatment). §7 already believes this; §5.3 hides it.

**(d) Bodin and Mill — the citable error.** "The old sovereignty of independence — Bodin's indivisible authority, Mill's private sphere — is a sovereignty of exit. Its power is the power to stand apart, to deal directly, to be unmediated" (l.404–405). This yokes two incompatible constructs and misreads at least one of them.

- **Bodin is the wrong man.** Bodinian *souveraineté* (1576) is the supreme, perpetual, indivisible, absolute authority of the commonwealth — a doctrine about the *locus and indivisibility of political authority*, with nothing to do with exit, independence-from-a-mediator, or "dealing directly." If anything Bodin's sovereign is the **un-bypassable final node** through which all law passes; subjects cannot exit the sovereign's jurisdiction. Recruiting Bodin as the emblem of "unmediated directness" and "the power to stand apart" inverts him. Worse, it collapses the very macro/micro distinction the chapter works to maintain elsewhere — digital sovereignty as *state/infrastructure control* (Krasner, Bodin territory) versus coordinative sovereignty as *actor-level autonomy*. Bodin belongs to the state-authority axis, not the individual-independence axis.
- **Mill is closer but still mislabeled.** Mill's private sphere (*On Liberty*) is a protected zone of **non-interference** — the liberal negative-liberty construct that Pettit, whom the chapter endorses in §6, explicitly holds *insufficient*. So the chapter recruits Mill as the emblem of the old sovereignty in §5.3 and then, via Pettit, declares that very conception (non-interference) inadequate in §6. Non-interference and exit are also distinct: Mill's liberty is being-left-alone, not being-able-to-leave.
- **Neither is in the reference list** (confirmed: no Bodin, no Mill entry). They are doing rhetorical, not scholarly, work — flagged for a double-anonymized IGI chapter that otherwise cites meticulously.
- **The right emblem is available and already in the room.** The contrast the argument actually needs is *autonomy-as-independence/self-sufficiency* versus *autonomy-as-standing-within-relation* — which is precisely the target of the **relational-autonomy** critique of the atomistic liberal self (Nedelsky; Mackenzie & Stoljar), already cited in §6. Drop Bodin entirely, reframe Mill as negative liberty (and note it is non-interference, not exit), and anchor "the old picture" in the relational-autonomy tradition's own account of the independent self. This costs the chapter nothing and removes its most exposed sentence.

**Repair also strengthens the good idea underneath.** §5.3's genuinely strong claim — "contingency is a policy and strategy variable… whether a coordinated actor can leave becomes a designable fact" (l.402–406) — is a real contribution and resonates with republicanism's own thesis that liberty is an *artifact of institutions*, not a natural endowment (Skinner, Pettit). The chapter could draw that parallel: exit, like non-domination, is *institutionally constituted*, not found. That earns the diagnostic its normative bite far better than the Bodin/Mill flourish does.

### §6 Coordinative sovereignty — the four foundations (heaviest weight)

The definition (l.471–475) is clear and the "currency is effective voice" formulation is good. The four-strand convergence is the theoretical core; each strand is assessed below.

**Pettit / Skinner — non-domination. Accurate.** The rendering — freedom as absence of *arbitrary* power, stronger than absence of interference, the actor "left alone and still unfree… at the mercy of a power that could interfere at will" (l.481–482) — is Pettit's kindly-master/slave figure, faithfully compressed. Muldoon & Raekstad (2022) do argue algorithmic management is domination; the cite is apt. Two refinements a Pettit-steeped referee would raise, both *strengthening*: (i) late Pettit (*On the People's Terms*, 2012) replaces "arbitrary" with **"uncontrolled"** interference — power is non-dominating when it is *controlled by* those subject to it, which maps *even better* onto the chapter's "terms that hold because the actors have the standing to make them hold." Using the 2012 formulation would tie the definition to the theory more tightly. (ii) For Pettit non-domination is secured by *contestatory capability* — power that "tracks" the interests of the governed and is checked by their ability to contest. That is doing real work for the chapter and could be named as such; it is also what makes the necessary-mediator case *tractable* (a necessary mediator whose power is contestable is non-dominating without being exited — the chapter's central hope, and pure Pettit).

**Nedelsky / Mackenzie & Stoljar — relational autonomy. Accurate, with two flags.** The compression — autonomy constituted through relationships, a relationship can enlarge or shrink it, "the object of sovereignty is the shape" (l.492–493) — is faithful and the use is elegant: relational autonomy "dissolves the apparent choice between being mediated and being autonomous." Two referee flags. (i) *Procedural vs substantive.* "A well-shaped mediation enlarges autonomy, a badly-shaped one diminishes it" takes a **substantive** stance (some relationship-contents are autonomy-enhancing by their content), which is one side of the very debate the Mackenzie & Stoljar volume is organized around. The chapter cites the volume while eliding its central fault line; one clause owning the substantive commitment would inoculate it. (ii) *Bearer mismatch.* Relational autonomy is a theory of **persons** — selves formed through care, identity, capacities. The chapter repeatedly attaches coordinative sovereignty to **firms** ("a firm's coordinative sovereignty," "an organization's standing," the SME). A marketplace-seller *firm* having "relational autonomy" in Nedelsky's sense is a category stretch; the foundations are person-centric (non-dominated persons, relationally autonomous persons, Markell's participating agent, Ostrom's individual appropriators) while §8's managerial payoff is firm-centric. The chapter should decide, or explicitly bridge, whether coordinative sovereignty's bearer is the person or the organization. As written the construct wobbles between them.

**Markell — "involvement." Accurate use, but a live irony.** Markell (2008) does argue non-domination "protects without including," and the chapter's demand for "a share… a place in the determination" (l.497–498) is a legitimate appropriation of his participation theme. Two concerns. (i) Markell's own term is Arendtian **action**, and the harm he names is **usurpation** — displacement from *taking part*, not merely subjection to another's will. "Involvement" is the chapter's serviceable compression, but the Arendtian grounding should be named so the strand is not mistaken for a generic "let them participate." (ii) The sharper point: Markell's critique of non-domination is *more radical than the chapter uses it*. His target is precisely the **sovereign picture of agency** — freedom imagined as independence, control, mastery — which he argues non-domination smuggles back in. That cuts against the chapter's own foundational choice to call its construct **sovereignty**. The chapter cites the theorist who argues that our attachment to *sovereignty* (as mastery/independence) is part of the problem, in order to license a construct named *coordinative sovereignty*. This is not disqualifying — but it must be answered, because it connects to the deepest conceptual question below.

**Ostrom — commons governance. Asserted, not defended — the weakest strand.** The chapter "carries the commons model from the shared pasture to the shared platform and asks whether the coordinated can hold a share in its governance" (l.504–505). *Asks whether* is the tell: the extension is asserted, and an Ostrom-steeped referee will demand three defenses the chapter does not supply.
1. **The disanalogy of ownership.** Ostrom's common-pool resources are governed by *appropriators who collectively use a resource none of them privately owns*. A platform mediation is **owned by the adversarial third party**; the coordination surplus is *produced by* the mediator's algorithm, not a pre-existing stock the users draw down. Ostromian self-governance means the appropriators *control* the resource without a central authority — which in the platform case is not "voice within an owned platform" but *wresting control* (≈ platform cooperativism, §7). Conflating Ostrom-style self-governance with "a share in someone else's platform" overstates what Ostrom licenses. Distinguish the two.
2. **Subtractability and boundaries.** CPRs are defined by *rivalry* (subtractability) and *hard exclusion*, governed by a *bounded, identifiable community* of appropriators. Platform coordination is largely non-rival, and its user population is vast, shifting, and globally dispersed — exactly the scale condition Ostrom flagged as *hostile* to self-governance. §7 concedes the cooperative-scale problem but never connects it back to the Ostrom foundation's viability. The strand needs the concession folded in.
3. **The better framing is enclosure, not pasture.** The defensible Ostromian move is *not* "the coordinated can self-govern the platform" but "the platform **enclosed** a commons" — the data, network, and coordination surplus were the common-pool resource, and the mediator privatized it. That reframing (Ostrom-via-enclosure) is stronger, is what §7's data-trust discussion actually implies, and should ground the §6 strand.

**Does coordinative sovereignty do normative work beyond non-domination + relational autonomy, or is it a relabeling?** This is the question the construct must survive, and the honest answer is mixed. The *normative content* is assembled entirely from the four strands: protection from arbitrary power (Pettit) + a share in governance (Markell) + mediation-as-autonomy-constituting (Nedelsky) + self-governing institutions (Ostrom). Strip the diagnostic and what remains is republican non-domination plus relational autonomy applied to platforms — which Muldoon & Raekstad and Bannerman already do. So coordinative sovereignty is best defended not as a new *normative primitive* but as a **synthesis plus a domain-selector**: its contribution is (a) the necessary/contingent diagnostic that identifies *when* this is the only autonomy available, and (b) the wedge against the nearest neighbor. The chapter half-admits this ("Four strands converge") and then oversells ("the object the crowded neighborhood describes around and leaves unoccupied," l.524–525). The genuine, defensible wedge is stated but buried: Muldoon & Raekstad "often recommend exit through cooperative ownership" (l.519–520), i.e., they assume the necessary case can be *escaped* via co-ops; coordinative sovereignty theorizes the residue — voice under mediation that *cannot* be escaped even by cooperative ownership at scale. **Lead with that.** It is the one thing the neighbors do not do. Two cautions on the neighbor contrast: (i) do not overstate Muldoon & Raekstad's exit-orientation — republican non-domination is *centrally* about contestatory voice, so the difference is emphasis-plus-the-necessity-condition, not a clean exit/voice division; (ii) the "crowded neighborhood… leaves unoccupied" / "mark the space without occupying it" occupancy metaphor is used twice in §6 and reads as filler the second time.

**The deepest conceptual question — is "sovereignty" the right word?** Given the Markell irony *and* the relational-autonomy foundation, naming a construct that is relational, partial, held-with-others, and exercised *within an unexitable relation* "sovereignty" is in tension with its own theory. Sovereignty connotes supremacy, indivisibility, self-mastery — Bodin's core marks — which is precisely what the construct renounces: it is a **share**, not a supremacy; the opposite of indivisible. A political-theory referee will ask directly: is "coordinative sovereignty" an oxymoron by its own lights? The chapter can answer (popular sovereignty is *already* shared and collective; the people's sovereignty is not indivisible mastery), but it must answer, and the answer is the same sentence that fixes §1: declare the popular/democratic register up front. Do that, and the Markell irony dissolves and the Bodin sentence becomes unnecessary. Leave it unstated, and the construct's own name is its most vulnerable point.

### §7 Realizing coordinative sovereignty

The institutional survey is the chapter's most useful applied section and the diagnostic-sorting ("exit-restoring instruments at the contingent functions, voice-building institutions at the necessary ones," l.620–621) is a real organizing contribution. Platform cooperativism (Scholz), data trusts (Delacroix & Lawrence, Micheli), collective bargaining, contestability (Alfrink), oversight boards (Klonick) are used accurately. This section is also where the §5.3 "missing third response" surfaces: cooperativism and data trusts *are* the ownership-transformation option, yet §5.3 filed them under "voice." Reconcile: name in §5.3 that necessary mediators admit two distinct voice-family responses — voice-within-private-ownership and transformation-of-ownership — and §7 becomes the payoff of that distinction rather than an uncatalogued list. The transparency-without-standing point recurs here a fourth time (Ananny & Crawford again) — consolidate (Part 2).

### §8 Implications

The high-digital/low-coordinative and low-digital/high-coordinative pairings (app developer vs platform worker under the EU platform-work directive) are the chapter's best concrete demonstration that the two constructs vary independently, and the directive citation (2024/2831) is real and apposite. §8 is where the person/firm bearer ambiguity is most visible: the scholarly payoff is firm-level ("operationalized as a single capability"), the vivid case is person-level (the platform worker's information and human-review rights). Not an error, but the reader should be told the construct is claimed for *both* and why the foundations transfer.

### §9 Future research

Appropriately modest; the discriminant-validity worry against psychological empowerment (Spreitzer) and algorithm-literacy scales (Dogruel) is exactly the right one. No political-theory issue. The self-report/formal-model correspondence test is a genuine strength of pairing the construct with a diagnostic.

### §10 Conclusion

Clean, and it restates the revisionist definition of sovereignty ("the standing to hold power to account") that §1 should have owned as a *choice*. The closer "Neither reading alone nor control alone secures it" is an acceptable conclusion-line antithesis. The framing question (can algorithmacy be democratized as literacy was) is a good open note; "a new constitutive priesthood" is vivid without overreaching.

## Part 3 — Line-level revisions (paste-ready)

**1. §1 / §10 — own the register of "sovereignty" (fixes the Bodin exposure and the Markell irony at the root). Add to §1, after l.54:**
> This chapter uses *sovereignty* in the popular register, the standing of the governed over the governance to which they are subject, not the Bodinian register of supreme and indivisible authority. Coordinative sovereignty is accordingly a *share* held in common, not a supremacy held alone.

**2. §5.3 — replace the Bodin/Mill sentence (l.404–405).** From:
> The old sovereignty of independence — Bodin's indivisible authority, Mill's private sphere — is a sovereignty of exit. Its power is the power to stand apart, to deal directly, to be unmediated.

To:
> The old picture of autonomy as independence, the self-sufficient chooser the relational-autonomy tradition takes as its foil (Nedelsky, 2011), is a sovereignty of exit. Its freedom is the freedom to stand apart, to deal directly, to be unmediated, and it is exactly the freedom an unleaveable mediation withdraws.

**3. §5.3 — replace the rosy Hirschman gloss (l.400–402).** From:
> and Hirschman himself observed that monopoly restricts exit and so elevates voice. Formalizing his categories is what yields the payoff. Exit maps onto bypassability, voice onto necessity, and the necessary/contingent test tells which obtains.

To:
> Hirschman warned that removing exit does not by itself strengthen voice: his lazy monopoly may even welcome the departure of its most demanding members, and voice under monopoly is where he feared it would be feeblest. Necessity therefore does not supply voice; it forecloses exit and leaves voice as the only remaining lever, which raises rather than lowers the burden on the institutions that would make voice effective. Bypassability is the availability of exit, and the necessary/contingent test says whether exit is available at all.

**4. §5.3 — add the third response (after l.417, "only voice remains"):**
> Voice, though, is not the only response to a mediator that cannot be left. Where exit is foreclosed by necessity rather than by law, the classic remedy is not to complain within private ownership but to change the ownership: a necessary mediator is the standing candidate for cooperative conversion, a data trust, or public-utility treatment. Section 7 develops these. The diagnostic sorts three responses, then, not two: exit against contingent mediators, voice within a necessary mediator left in private hands, and transformation of who owns the necessary mediator.

**5. §6 — sharpen the wedge against the nearest neighbor (revise l.518–520).** From:
> The republican analysis of algorithmic domination (Muldoon & Raekstad, 2022) centers protection from arbitrary power and often recommends exit through cooperative ownership.

To:
> The republican analysis of algorithmic domination (Muldoon & Raekstad, 2022) centers protection from arbitrary power and reaches for cooperative ownership, a route that assumes the mediation can, at scale, be escaped or re-owned. Coordinative sovereignty theorizes the residue that assumption leaves: the standing available when even a cooperative cannot be built at the required scale and the mediation must be governed from within. That residue is what the neighboring constructs describe around and leave unoccupied.

**6. §6 — name the Ostrom disanalogy (revise l.500–505).** From:
> And from Ostrom (1990) it takes commons governance… Coordinative sovereignty carries the commons model from the shared pasture to the shared platform and asks whether the coordinated can hold a share in its governance.

To:
> And from Ostrom (1990) it takes commons governance: actors interdependent on a shared resource can govern it themselves, through institutions of their own design, without privatizing it and without surrendering it to a central authority. The transfer to platforms is not automatic. Ostrom's appropriators co-use a resource none of them owns, while a platform's coordination is owned by the mediator and produced by its algorithm, so the commons model applies best where the underlying resource, the pooled data and the coordination surplus, is read as a commons the platform enclosed rather than one the users already hold. On that reading her design principles, collective-choice arrangements, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict resolution, and recognized rights to organize, become institutional forms of effective voice under interdependence, and the question is whether the coordinated can recover a share in governing what was enclosed.

**7. §6 — acknowledge the Markell tension (add after l.498):**
> The debt to Markell carries a caution. His quarrel with non-domination is that it keeps picturing freedom as sovereign mastery, as control that makes one independent of others, and coordinative sovereignty answers the caution only by using *sovereignty* in the shared, popular sense, never the sense of a mastery held alone.

**8. §6 — tie non-domination to controlled (not merely arbitrary) power (add after l.487):**
> On Pettit's later statement the test is whether the mediator's power is *controlled* by those subject to it, not merely whether it is arbitrary, and control is precisely what standing supplies: terms hold because the coordinated actors can make them hold.

## Verdict

**Major revisions.** The scaffolding is sound and the diagnostic-linked synthesis is a real contribution, but three political-theory problems must be fixed before an IGI political-theory referee would pass it: (1) the Bodin/Mill sentence in §5.3 misreads the sovereignty tradition and must go; (2) the Hirschman mapping is presented in its rosy half and needs the lazy-monopoly correction plus the missing third response (ownership transformation); (3) the Ostrom strand is asserted rather than defended and needs the enclosure reframing. None is fatal; each is a bounded fix, and the paste-ready revisions above discharge all three.

**Most important fix:** own the *popular/democratic* register of "sovereignty" in §1 (Revision 1). That single move retires the Bodin error, dissolves the Markell irony, answers the "is this an oxymoron" objection, and stops the slippage between sovereignty-as-authority and sovereignty-as-standing that a referee will otherwise exploit paragraph by paragraph.

## Biggest strength

The necessary/contingent diagnostic genuinely earns its keep as a *political* instrument, not just a formal one: it turns "can this actor leave?" from a natural fact into a designable, institutionally constituted one, which is exactly the republican insight that liberty is an artifact of institutions rather than an endowment. Paired with the portfolio view (§5.4, "one firm, four kinds of mediator at once"), it gives the exit/voice literature something it has lacked — a rule for *which* response a given dependence warrants. That is the contribution the neighbors (Bannerman, Muldoon & Raekstad, Cohen, Pasquale) do not offer.

## The one thing only the author can supply

Decide, and state, **who bears coordinative sovereignty — the person or the firm — and defend the transfer.** The four foundations are person-centric (non-dominated persons, relationally autonomous selves, Markell's participating agent, Ostrom's individual appropriators), while §8's managerial and scholarly payoff is firm-centric (the SME, the developer, "an organization's standing"). Relational autonomy in particular does not straightforwardly extend from selves to corporate actors. The author must either (a) argue that the construct's bearer is the natural person coordinating through the platform, and treat the firm as a proxy, or (b) supply the bridging argument for why organizations inherit these person-grounded freedoms. This is a decision about the construct's identity that no referee or reviewer can make for the author, and every empirical instrument proposed in §9 depends on it.
