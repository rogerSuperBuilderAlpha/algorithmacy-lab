## Part 1 — Theory and structure, §1–§10

### §1 Introduction

The inventory (GDPR, AI Act, DMA, DSA, NIS2) is accurate at the level of instruments and the Bradford/Floridi/Fratini framing is apt. The hook remains the chapter’s most legally exposed sentence.

**Finding 1 (damage: high — residual).** Abstract lines 5–6 and §1 lines 28–30 still assert that a vendor’s or merchant’s market position turns on ranking and review decisions it “can neither see nor predict nor contest.” Positive law has moved on *see* and partly on *contest*:

- P2B Art. 5 (main ranking parameters); Art. 4 (statement of reasons for restriction, suspension, termination); Arts. 11–12 (internal complaint-handling and mediation).
- DSA Art. 27 (recommender main parameters); Arts. 20–21 (internal complaint-handling; certified out-of-court dispute settlement).
- DMA Art. 6(5) (no self-preferencing in ranking).

§3.2 and §8 already concede these instruments and convert the thesis into “legibility without standing.” The opening does not. A platform-regulation referee who reads only the first page will file the chapter as arguing against a straw “law = nothing on ranking,” then find the concession three sections later and discount the craft. The thesis is strengthened by putting the concession in the hook: Europe already discloses and partly channels complaint; what remains missing is standing over the coordinative determination. One paragraph after the merchant/creator examples would close this.

No other legal error in §1. Closed sovereignty-naming defense (Bodin / Habermas / Repetto) holds for this lens.

### §2 Sovereignty and its sensibilities

Clean for this lens. §2.3’s reading of AI Act / GDPR / NIS2 as “technologies of reading and writing” is a defensible characterization of the documentation, notice, consent, access, and written-governance duties those instruments impose. Oracy unevenness fix (authorized forms of speech) holds; no legal stake.

### §3 The platform turn — §3.2 is the legal crux

§3’s separation of old logics/topology from the opaque–adaptive–interested conjunction is sound. §3.2 now names P2B, DSA recommender transparency and researcher access, DMA gatekeeper conduct, and the GDPR contest right (lines 247–250). That closes the prior “instruments absent” finding.

**Finding 2 (damage: high — new taxonomy slip).** Lines 247–253 list four instruments and then say “What these share is the literate wager, that a legible object placed before an actor who can act on it will hold its operator to account.” GDPR Article 22 is not a disclosure instrument. It is a right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects, coupled with rights to human intervention and to contest the outcome. Filing Art. 22 under the same “literate wager” as P2B parameter disclosure collapses the chapter’s own distinction between legibility and standing — and hands a referee the retort that the standing modality the chapter demands already exists in hard law. The accurate claim is narrower: Art. 22 is case-level contestability with a high “solely automated” threshold, thin human-intervention practice, and a personal scope limited to natural persons, so it does not reach the firm-as-business-user cast that opens the chapter. Keep Art. 22; move it out of the disclosure bundle and into the standing/contestability cell that §7 already assigns it.

**Hildebrandt (lines 244–246).** Still cited mainly as a limit (“cannot straightforwardly govern”). Her project is re-articulating legal protection into the code/data environment. One clause acknowledging that constructive arrow would align §3.2 with §7’s contestability remedies. Secondary to Finding 2.

Cohen and Pasquale remain correctly enlisted for platform opacity and functional/platform sovereignty. §3.3’s bottleneck economics (Rochet–Tirole; Armstrong) is outside this lens’s hard bar and looks competent.

### §4 Algorithmacy

Not a legal section. Governance-relevant point that opacity preserves rent and concentrates competence like capital lands; Cotter & Reisdorf restored. Clean for this lens.

### §5 Diagnostic — exit/voice and essential facilities

The bypass test, the capability-of-direct-dealing clarification, the App Store payment-gate node/edge demo, and the filing of DMA anti-steering as the edge test run by a regulator (lines 452–458) are legally accurate and close the prior methodological gaps.

Essential-facilities / node-vs-edge contrast (lines 439–450) is correctly drawn: competition law’s access questions operate on the node; the diagnostic adds an edge. Ancillary-restraints parallel (lines 460–466) is apt and now matches the Booking.com framing in §5.5.

Exit/voice mapping (lines 480–505): contingent → exit instruments (deregulation, interoperability, antitrust, breaking exclusives); necessary → voice via institutions. Broadly sound as a heuristic. The §7 dual reading of interoperability (exit against contingent; access/voice against necessary) is the right refinement and is already in prose — do not reopen.

**§5.5 OTA.** Legal chronology holds: wide parity abandoned 2015; national enforcement through 2021; Case C-264/23 (19 Sept 2024) that parity clauses are not ancillary restraints; Booking designated gatekeeper May 2024 (chapter says “four months after,” which is correct relative to September 2024). Qualifications on the 2024 judgment postdating the Schegg 2024 / 2013–2023 data hold. No regression.

### §6 Definition

Clean for this lens. Muldoon & Raekstad correctly filed as cooperative ownership / voice, not exit. Neighbour placement (Bannerman; Pasquale functional sovereignty; Cohen platform sovereign) is careful. No legal error.

### §7 Building standing (heavily weighted)

The dual reading of interoperability (lines 678–688) and the table (lines 692–700) are the institutional payoff. COM(2026) 178 on messaging interoperability and the refusal to extend to social networking matches the Commission’s April 2026 DMA review (VERIFY item below confirms the cite). Essential-facilities logic for access-as-voice is correctly invoked.

**Instrument-to-cell audit of the table:**

| Instrument | Contingent cell | Necessary cell | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interoperability / portability | restores exit | access on regulated terms = voice | Correct (DMA Arts. 6(4), 6(9)–(10) vs Art. 7 messaging / OS-feature interop) |
| Antitrust / anti-steering | opens the bypass | alters conduct, not dependence | Correct as heuristic; Apple Art. 5(4) anti-steering is the contingent-gate case |
| Contestability of individual decisions | **marginal** | voice at case level | **Undercells the contingent column** — see Finding 4 |
| Collective bargaining | marginal | voice at rule level | Correct |
| Data trusts / intermediaries | leverage toward exit | leverage toward voice | Defensible; portability dependence should be stated in prose |
| Cooperative ownership | may fund alternative | changes owner, not leaveability | Correct |
| Oversight boards | marginal | voice, platform-bounded | Correct for private boards; statutory ODS missing — Finding 3 |

**Finding 3 (damage: medium — residual).** Oversight boards still lead with the private Meta-style body (Klonick, 2020 — cite now correct) while DSA Arts. 20–21 (internal complaint-handling; certified out-of-court dispute settlement) go unnamed. Those articles are the in-force statutory “standing channel” at Union scale. Lead with them; treat platform-constituted boards as the weaker private analogue. P2B Arts. 11–12 already get credit in §8; the DSA pair belongs in the same paragraph as Klonick.

**Finding 4 (damage: medium — cell placement).** Table row “Contestability of individual decisions → marginal against contingent” understates P2B Arts. 4 and 11–12: statement of reasons for restriction/suspension/termination, internal complaint-handling, and mediation are case-level voice instruments aimed precisely at contingent gates (delisting, account suspension, ranking demotion of a business user). Against a contingent mediator those channels are not marginal; they are the existing, thin form of contestability. Relabel the contingent cell “case-level channel (P2B / DSA 20–21)” or similar, and keep “voice at the level of the case” for the necessary column.

**Finding 5 (damage: medium — Omnibus asymmetry).** §7 lines 712–713 present the Data Governance Act as having “created a registered category of data intermediary,” with evidence still out. True as of the Act’s application. The same Digital Omnibus proposal that §8 cites for repealing P2B (COM(2025) 837, 19 Nov 2025) also proposes repealing the DGA and migrating data-intermediation and data-altruism rules into the Data Act. Naming the P2B repeal while treating the DGA as a settled, unthreatened vehicle will read as selective currency. One clause: intermediary registration exists under the DGA today; the Omnibus would relocate rather than abandon the category — if that is the Commission’s design — or flag the proposal and the uncertainty. Do not leave §7 silent while §8 is loud.

Data trusts (Delacroix & Lawrence) remain correctly classed. The composition paragraph (lines 737–742) half-says that exit instruments relieve pressure on necessary ones; a sharper sentence that bottom-up trusts pool what portability (GDPR Art. 20) lets members carry out would lock the exit/voice leak as a feature.

Platform cooperativism, collective bargaining (Lei), Art. 22 floor for contestability: correct once Finding 2 moves Art. 22 out of the disclosure bundle.

### §8 Implications (heavily weighted)

**Apple €500 million / April 2025 anti-steering** (lines 758–760): verified — Commission decision of 23 April 2025, DMA Art. 5(4), Case DMA.100109; Apple has appealed. Ranking transparency correctly split to P2B (2019) and DSA. P2B’s complaint-handling, mediation, and representative standing correctly noted.

**Finding 6 (damage: medium — overclaim inside a good paragraph).** Lines 764–766: the Commission proposed to repeal P2B “as part of a wider simplification package, which would remove the only horizontal ranking-transparency duty in European law.” The repeal proposal is real (Digital Omnibus, COM(2025) 837). “Only horizontal ranking-transparency duty” overreaches. DSA Art. 27 imposes recommender-parameter transparency on online platforms; DMA Art. 6(5) is a conduct rule, not a disclosure duty, but the DSA obligation is horizontal within its personal scope. The precise claim is that P2B Art. 5 is the only horizontal duty to disclose main ranking parameters to *business users of online intermediation services and search engines*. DSA 27 does not replace that business-user ranking duty in full. Tighten the noun phrase; keep the political point that an instrument attaching a channel to disclosure is proposed for withdrawal.

Platform-work directive (lines 769–779): transposition deadline 2 December 2026 correct; rebuttable presumption where the platform controls the work correct in substance (Art. 5); information rights, human review, data limits correctly summarized; Art. 25 promote-collective-bargaining quote accurate; “no member state had fully transposed” is a dated claim that needs a writing-date anchor or VERIFY before press. Soft finding: the presumption of employment is named but never sorted. Reclassification moves the worker from platform triad toward hierarchy — a change of coordination logic, not voice within the mediation. One sentence would stop a labour referee from asking the question in the worst order.

Independent-variation claim (high digital sovereignty / low coordinative sovereignty) remains the chapter’s sharpest testable proposition and is now legally illustrated without the 2025 ranking-transparency conflation. Closed.

### §9 Future research

AI Act literacy obligation (lines 870–871): Art. 4 AI Act requires providers and deployers to ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff and persons dealing with AI systems on their behalf — claim holds.

**VERIFY / underspecification.** “the platform-work directive requires member states to support workers’ representatives with expertise the platform funds” (lines 871–872). The operative rule is Art. 13(3): representatives may be assisted by an expert of their choice for information and consultation on automated monitoring/decision systems; where the platform has more than 250 workers in the Member State, proportionate expert expenses are borne by the platform. Not a general expertise voucher, and not owed by every platform. Compress to the Art. 13 threshold or drop the funding clause.

A legal-instrument-to-mediator-type crosswalk remains the empirical proposal a law-minded editor would ask for; still author-only.

### §10 Conclusion

Clean recap. “Instruments of digital sovereignty apply” where the mediator is contingent (lines 888–889) is slightly loose — some digital-sovereignty instruments (contestation, collective voice) are voice tools for necessary mediators — but a conclusion may summarize. No new legal error.

## Part 3 — Paste-ready revisions

**§1, after the merchant/creator examples (≈ line 35).** Insert:

> European law has begun to reach this surface. The Platform-to-Business Regulation requires disclosure of main ranking parameters, a statement of reasons for restriction or delisting, and internal complaint-handling with access to mediation; the Digital Services Act adds recommender-parameter transparency and statutory complaint and out-of-court dispute channels. What these instruments supply is legibility and a thin process right. What they withhold is standing over the determination that sets the actor’s market position. The gap this chapter names opens there.

**§3.2, lines 247–253.** Replace the four-instrument sentence and the “literate wager” sentence with:

> Recent European instruments do reach into ranking and automated decisions. The Platform-to-Business Regulation obliges platforms to disclose the main parameters of ranking; the Digital Services Act adds recommender transparency and researcher access; the Digital Markets Act constrains gatekeeper conduct, including self-preferencing in ranking. What these share is the literate wager: that a legible object placed before an actor who can act on it will hold its operator to account. Against an opaque and adaptive coordination the wager mostly fails, and the disclosures that succeed often produce facts without consequences for actors who lack standing to contest them (Cohen, 2017; Pasquale, 2023). A separate strand reaches for standing rather than disclosure. Article 22 of the GDPR gives a natural person subject to a solely automated decision the right to human intervention and to contest the outcome — case-level contestability with a high threshold, thin practice, and a personal scope that does not cover the business user who opened this chapter.

**§7, oversight paragraph (lines 730–733).** Replace with:

> The Digital Services Act’s internal complaint-handling and certified out-of-court dispute-settlement bodies (Articles 20–21) institutionalize case-level contestation at statutory scale. Platform-run oversight boards offer a weaker private analogue: a body with some authority to review the platform’s determinations, bounded by the platform that constitutes it (Klonick, 2020).

**§7, table row for contestability.** Replace the contingent cell “marginal” with:

> case-level channel (P2B Arts. 4, 11–12; DSA Arts. 20–21)

**§7, after the DGA sentence (line 713).** Add:

> The Commission’s November 2025 Digital Omnibus proposal would repeal the Data Governance Act and relocate data-intermediation rules into the Data Act, so the statutory vehicle is in motion even as the category of intermediary persists on the page (European Commission, 2025). Evidence that either vehicle delivers standing in practice is not yet in.

(Add reference: European Commission. (2025). *Proposal for a Regulation … (Digital Omnibus)* (COM(2025) 837 final).)

**§8, lines 764–766.** Replace “the only horizontal ranking-transparency duty in European law” with:

> the only horizontal duty to disclose main ranking parameters to business users of online intermediation services and search engines

**§9, lines 871–872.** Replace the platform-funds clause with:

> and Article 13 of the platform-work directive lets workers’ representatives call an expert for consultation on automated systems, with proportionate costs borne by platforms that employ more than 250 workers in the Member State concerned

**§8, after the presumption sentence (≈ line 772).** Optional one-liner:

> The presumption of employment changes the coordination logic toward hierarchy rather than granting voice inside the platform mediation; the rights that build coordinative sovereignty are the algorithmic-management ones.

## Verdict

**Minor revision — accept with nits.** The post-rebuild chapter survives a hostile law-and-governance read on the instruments that sank the prior draft. Exit/voice cell placement is mostly right; interoperability’s dual character is earned; Apple 2025, Booking C-264/23, COM(2026) 178, P2B channels, Art. 22, Art. 25, and the platform-work transposition deadline are in working order.

**Single most important fix:** Align the abstract and §1 hook with the §3.2/§8 concession (Finding 1), and pull GDPR Art. 22 out of the “literate wager” disclosure bundle (Finding 2). Those two edits stop a referee from refuting the chapter with the chapter’s own later pages.

**Biggest genuine strength:** The portfolio-of-mediators diagnostic married to a two-column instrument table that correctly sorts interoperability as exit *or* voice by mediator type, with DMA anti-steering and essential-facilities access as worked legal illustrations. That is a contribution a platform-governance volume can use.

**What only the author can supply:** A defended instrument-to-mediator-type crosswalk (which article bites which of the four mediator kinds on a named platform function), grounded in the companion’s formal classifications. The table in §7 is the sketch; the crosswalk is the test.
