## Part 1 — Theory and structure, §1–§10

### §1 Introduction

The compliance-yet-helpless opening still lands the chapter in the volume. The early sovereignty defense (lines 69–80) is the structural improvement that matters for this lens. Bodin is met, Habermas supplies the desubstantialized sense, and Repetto is used for specification-not-stretching rather than as a decorative citation. Closed finding #1 holds.

The load-bearing contrast remains lines 65–67: "Digital sovereignty asks who controls the infrastructure. Coordinative sovereignty asks what autonomy remains for the actor coordinating through it." That contrast is only as strong as §2.3's account of digital sovereignty. See below.

### §2.1–§2.2 Oracy, literacy

Clean for this lens. The authorized-exercise fix in §2.1 (competence general, standing restricted) removes the earlier contradiction with the unevenness frame. No new DS objection.

### §2.3 Digital sovereignty as extended literacy — **residual load-bearing weak point**

Line 132 still asserts: "Digital sovereignty, in most of its current forms, is the literate competence carried into the digital domain." The instruments listed (AI Act documentation, GDPR notice/consent/access, NIS2 written governance) are reading-and-writing instruments. For the *regulatory-transparency* strand this is accurate.

It is not accurate for the center of gravity of European digital-sovereignty policy the chapter otherwise addresses. Infrastructural and industrial instruments — sovereign cloud, Gaia-X-class capacity building, semiconductor and cloud procurement, data localization, strategic autonomy vis-à-vis hyperscalers — do not govern "by making the digital object legible and by attaching consequences to what the legible object shows" (lines 140–142). They govern by jurisdiction, ownership, and capacity. Roberts et al. (2021), cited at line 637, frame digital sovereignty around strategic autonomy and values. Fratini et al. (2024), the chapter's own survey warrant, include infrastructural and territorial models precisely because much of the concept is not about legibility. Pohle and Thiel (2020), cited at line 134–135, treat digital sovereignty as a contested discursive boundary object; the chapter cites them while presenting a settled literacy capability.

Closed finding #8 holds for the *centralization* misread: lines 637–638 now concede that "the move is not always centralizing" and cite Couture and Toupin (2019) on grassroots variants. What still fails is the *literacy assimilation*. The infrastructural strand is acknowledged as "control of infrastructure" in the contrast slogans (§1, §6, §8) but never admitted as a face of digital sovereignty that the "extended literacy" thesis does not cover. The hedge "in most of its current forms" is doing unearned work. Key Terms (lines 1269–1271) locks the over-reduction into the glossary: "This chapter treats it as a literacy-based form of governance."

**Damage rank: highest remaining for this lens.** A DS referee will accept the regulatory-strand reading if scoped; will reject the unqualified "most forms" claim.

**Repair:** restrict §2.3 to the regulatory/legibility strand; name the infrastructural-industrial strand in one sentence; say the chapter's contrast engages the control/legibility family specifically, not every usage Pohle and Thiel survey.

### §3 The platform turn

Strong. The three-way separation (logic old; topology old; conjunction of opacity, adaptivity, and firm interest new) is fair. §3.2's five blocking features remain the most persuasive stretch for why disclosure fails.

Residual overclaim at lines 253–255: "Surveys of the sovereignty concept confirm the residue: existing models sort sovereignty by the actor claiming it and the domain claimed, and none addresses the coordination through which platforms enroll and bind (Fratini et al., 2024)." One survey cannot underwrite "none." Soften to "the most systematic survey to date includes no coordination-layer model."

### §4 Algorithmacy

Outside the DS core. The concession to DeVito and the narrowing to the triadic setting are honest. Cotter and Reisdorf (2020) are present (lines 354–356). Closed finding #9's DS-adjacent piece holds. No objection.

### §5 Diagnostic

### §6 Definition and neighbors

The definition is tight. Muldoon and Raekstad / cooperativism is now filed as voice/ownership, not exit (lines 648–655): ownership "cannot make the mediator leaveable." Closed finding #7 holds. Bearer named via relation with Pettit (2016); conflict case marked as a position taken (lines 626–632). Closed finding #6 holds.

**Does the digital↔coordinative line survive?** Mostly yes. Lines 635–643 now treat digital-sovereignty work as answering the ownership/control question, concede non-centralizing variants, and use Repetto for innovation rather than dilution. Pasquale (functional sovereignty) and Cohen (platform sovereign) are handled fairly as theorizing the platform's authority from the other end.

Two residual blurs a DS referee will press:

1. **Data sovereignty already carries standing.** Hummel et al. (2021) — cited for digital sovereignty at lines 134 and 748 — review control over how data about a subject is used; indigenous and community data-sovereignty frameworks are self-determination within a relation. That is closer to "standing within a coordination" than the typology admits. The boundary between "control your data" (filed digital) and "standing in the coordination that runs on your data" (filed coordinative) needs one explicit sentence.

2. **Bannerman (2024) remains under-differentiated** (lines 645–646). Relational sovereignty is the nearest competitor. Lead with the diagnostic (necessary/contingent → exit vs voice), not "the other end."

The three clarifications (lines 657–664) still earn their keep.

### §7 Building standing

The table sorts instruments by mediator type; that is the section's value. Cooperative ownership is correctly "changes the owner, not the leaveability" (line 699). Closed finding #7 continues to hold here.

**Data-trust boundary still unnamed.** Data trusts and data cooperatives (lines 708–713) are data-sovereignty mechanisms in the literature the chapter cites (Micheli et al.; Delacroix & Lawrence; Hummel). The chapter's defense — they change terms of input, leave the mediator in place, count as voice — is available but unstated. Add one sentence or a referee will say the typology relabels digital-sovereignty instruments when convenient.

Interoperability as both exit (contingent) and voice (necessary) is correctly doubled; the DMA citation strengthens the chapter. Keep it.

### §8 Implications

The independent-variation cases remain the best evidence for the two-construct thesis: high digital / low coordinative (developer/seller, lines 755–767) and low digital / high coordinative arriving under the platform-work directive (lines 769–779). The openness/control reframe (lines 807–822) is a genuine contribution to the European DS debate: a closed national platform without user voice reproduces co-optation under a domestic flag. That passage partially supplies the infrastructural acknowledgment §2.3 withholds — but coming late does not repair the early overclaim.

VERIFY the Apple €500 million / April 2025 claim and the P2B repeal proposal (lines 758–766); both are load-bearing and specific.

### §9 Future research

Honest. Discriminant targets against algorithm-literacy scales and empowerment are right. No DS objection. Closed finding #5 holds: "computable" is absent from the conclusion; §9 keeps formal measures in the companion without promising them here.

### §10 Conclusion

Ties the arc without inflation. Closed finding #5 holds on re-read (no "computable").

**New muddle at lines 888–889:** "Where the mediator is contingent, the older sovereignty of exit still works, and the instruments of digital sovereignty apply." §7 showed that several flagship digital-sovereignty instruments (DMA interoperability, essential-facilities access) function as *voice* against necessary mediators. Equating "instruments of digital sovereignty" with the exit toolkit re-flattens digital sovereignty into independence/control and undoes work §7 did. Replace with: exit instruments and the independence strand of digital sovereignty apply where the mediator is contingent; voice instruments — including some digital-sovereignty tools aimed at access rather than departure — apply where it is necessary.

## Part 3 — Paste-ready revisions

- **Lines 132–142** (scope the literacy claim). Replace the opening of §2.3 through the "legible object" sentence with:

> Digital sovereignty, in its regulatory strand, extends literate competence into the digital domain. The term refers to the ability of states, organizations, and communities to exercise control over their digital infrastructures, data, and technologies (Floridi, 2020; Hummel et al., 2021; Pohle & Thiel, 2020). Pohle and Thiel treat the term as a contested boundary object; this chapter engages the control and legibility family of usages, not every industrial or geopolitical deployment. The instruments through which the regulatory strand is pursued are often technologies of reading and writing: the AI Act's documentation duties, the GDPR's notice, consent, access, and record-keeping, NIS2's written cybersecurity governance. Each is a mechanism by which an actor with enough legal, technical, and regulatory literacy can inspect a system, compare it against a standard, and hold its operator to account (Fratini et al., 2024; Schmuntzsch et al., 2025). An infrastructural strand — sovereign cloud, domestic capacity, data localization, strategic autonomy vis-à-vis foreign platforms — works instead by jurisdiction, ownership, and capacity (Roberts et al., 2021). The literacy frame reaches that strand only partly. What the two strands share is the ownership-and-control question; neither asks what standing remains for the actor who must still coordinate through whatever infrastructure prevails.

- **Key Terms, lines 1269–1271.** Replace the second sentence with:

> This chapter engages its control and legibility strand as a literacy-based form of governance, while recognizing an infrastructural strand that works by jurisdiction and capacity.

- **Lines 253–255** (soften Fratini). Replace with:

> The most systematic survey of sovereignty models to date sorts them by the actor claiming sovereignty and the domain claimed, and includes no model of the coordination through which platforms enroll and bind (Fratini et al., 2024).

- **After line 643** (data-sovereignty boundary). Insert:

> Data sovereignty already names a standing claim over how data about a subject are used (Hummel et al., 2021). Coordinative sovereignty differs in target: not control of the data resource but voice inside the mediation that runs on it. A data trust can serve both; when it changes the terms on which a necessary mediator obtains input without transferring ownership of the platform, it counts here as a voice instrument, not as reclaimed infrastructure.

- **Lines 645–648** (Bannerman). Replace the Bannerman sentence and the following clause with:

> Bannerman (2024) reads platform power through a relational and historical lens and works toward rebalancing structural asymmetry. Coordinative sovereignty shares the relational starting point and adds a test the relational account leaves open: the necessary/contingent diagnostic, which says when voice is the only autonomy left because whether the actor can leave is a property of the coordination's rules.

- **Lines 888–889** (conclusion muddle). Replace:

> Where the mediator is contingent, the older sovereignty of exit still works, and instruments that restore independence and direct dealing apply. Where the mediator is necessary, only voice remains, including those digital-sovereignty tools — access mandates, interoperability against non-bypassable facilities — that regulate terms rather than dissolve dependence, resting on non-domination, relational autonomy, involvement, and commons governance, and realized, if at all, through cooperatives, trusts, collective bargaining, contestability, and standing channels of governance.

- **Line 569.** Delete "Two qualifications keep the case honest." Keep the two qualifications as the paragraph's opening substance.

- **Lines 747–748.** Apply the agentive rewrite in Part 2, item 1.

## Verdict

**Minor revision.** The post-rebuild text has closed the make-or-break gaps for this lens: sovereignty is defended early as specification rather than stretching; cooperativism is filed as voice; digital sovereignty is no longer flattened to "centralize control." The necessary/contingent diagnostic, the portfolio reading, and the §8 independent-variation cases remain the chapter's contribution and fit the volume. What still fails is accuracy about digital sovereignty itself: §2.3 and Key Terms over-assimilate the concept to literacy, the data-sovereignty boundary is unnamed, and the conclusion briefly re-equates digital-sovereignty instruments with exit. Each is a scoping or one-sentence repair, not new research.

**Single most important fix:** scope §2.3 (and Key Terms) so the literacy thesis covers the regulatory strand and names the infrastructural-industrial strand it does not capture. Without that, a DS referee reads the digital↔coordinative contrast as built on a tidied foil.

**Biggest genuine strength:** the independently varying typology made concrete in §8, paired with a bypass diagnostic that converts "who controls infrastructure vs what standing the coordinated hold" from a slogan into a usable sort — including, now, a platform-native node/edge demonstration.

**The one thing only the author can supply:** none required for press on the DS line itself, provided the scoping sentences land. Empirical VERIFY items below need sourcing or softening; they are editorial, not theoretical.
