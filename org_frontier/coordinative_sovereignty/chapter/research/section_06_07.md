## PART 1a — NOVELTY THREAT-HUNT

### VERDICT: **SURVIVES, but must NARROW.** The label is unclaimed; the territory is partly occupied.

**The name is free.** An exact-phrase sweep of Google Scholar, Crossref (`query.bibliographic`), and OpenAlex (`title_and_abstract.search`) for "coordinative sovereignty" returns exactly one hit in all of scholarship: a passing, descriptive use in a 2026 Harvard Divinity School MDiv thesis (van de Fliert, *What it means to matter*, DASH handle 1/42738327; VERIFIED against the DASH DSpace REST API), which uses it about the Articles of Confederation and the EU. Not a construct, no claim, no prior-art risk. "Coordination sovereignty" and "sovereignty of coordination" return only EU fiscal-coordination and blockchain-logistics noise. Nobody owns the label.

**The territory is another matter.** A republican-algorithmic line already occupies "the autonomy of the algorithmically managed, analyzed as non-domination, with collective voice as the remedy." What is *not* occupied is the three moves that actually constitute the chapter's construct: (i) irreducibility of the coordination, rather than the identity of the owner, as what generates the problem; (ii) the necessary/contingent bypass test as a decision procedure; (iii) standing in governance treated as separable from ownership. The chapter must foreground those three, because at present the first is implicit and the third is buried.

### The near-neighbours, ranked by threat

**TIER 1 — the two that can kill the section if unaddressed.**

**(1) Muldoon & Raekstad (2023), EJPT 22(4) — most dangerous, because the chapter currently MISDESCRIBES it.**
The chapter says they "recommend exit through cooperative ownership." The word "exit" appears zero times in the paper (confirmed independently by grep on two separate full-text copies — the UvA-DARE PDF I read and the Essex repository PDF a second reader read). - Abstract, verbatim from the Crossref record: *"Some have pleaded for enhancing workers' bargaining power through unionisation; others for enhancing exit options in the labour market. The chapter's answer is a *diagnostic* — a test that says when exit is even available, before any programme is chosen. The chapter can present itself as prior to Christiaens's question rather than as a rival answer to it.

Repetto's abstract, **verbatim** from the Crossref record for `10.1002/poi3.70011` (Crossref carries the publisher's own abstract; the article is CC BY 4.0; Wiley's HTML and PDF endpoints both returned 403 to automated fetch, so I read the abstract only):

> "This study provides a review of 240 works, coded according to disciplinary area, actors mentioned in relation to digital sovereignty, and the types of relations explicitly or implicitly conceptualised within each work. **I argue that the abundance of actors to which digital sovereignty is ascribed is not an issue per se; rather, the issue is that significant portions of the literature cluster around a digital sovereign 'of choice' and limit conversation with other strands. Those works that acknowledge the possibility of multiple coexisting digital sovereigns, or that see digital sovereignty as something that arises from the interactions among multiple actors, neither stretch the concept of sovereignty nor turn digital sovereignty into something different from sovereignty tout court: instead, they explore ways to innovate the concept of sovereignty as a whole** and problematise our long-held assumptions about it."

Two things follow.

**(1) The charge the chapter attributes to Repetto is the charge he explicitly declines to make.** He says in as many words that the multiplication of actors "is not an issue per se." His complaint is *siloing*: strands of the literature each pick a favourite sovereign and stop talking to each other. As written, the chapter attributes to a named 2025 author a position he states he rejects. A referee who checks one citation will check this one, because it is the article that licenses the chapter's whole self-defence.

**(2) The correction is a gift.** Repetto's positive criterion is that work which sees "digital sovereignty as something that arises from **the interactions among multiple actors**" is *innovating* the concept, not stretching it. Coordinative sovereignty is defined by the relation between a coordinated party and a mediator inside an irreducible three-party coordination. It is sovereignty as a property of an interaction, by construction. Repetto's own criterion blesses it. The chapter currently positions itself as *surviving* Repetto's critique; it can position itself as *satisfying* his criterion for concept innovation, which is a far stronger place to stand and costs no new argument.

**Action:** rewrite both passages. §1's "That is the discipline the concept-stretching critique demands" and §6's opening of the near-neighbours paragraph both need to be re-based on what Repetto actually argues. Get the full text before rewriting — I read only the abstract, and the argument's fine grain matters here.

## PART 1b — THE BEARER PROBLEM

The chapter never says whether coordinative sovereignty is borne by a person or by a firm, and its examples slide across the gap: a gig driver, a marketplace seller, an independent software vendor. In a volume on organizational implications this is not a loose end a referee will forgive.

### THE ANSWER: Pettit has already built the template — and stated its price in his own voice

> Pettit, P. (2016). The globalized republican ideal. *Global Justice: Theory Practice Rhetoric, 9*(1), 47–68. https://doi.org/10.21248/gjn.9.1.101
- **VERIFIED** Crossref (`https://api.crossref.org/works/10.21248/gjn.9.1.101`): sole author Philip Pettit, *Global Justice: Theory Practice Rhetoric*, vol 9, issue 1. ⚠️ **Crossref's date field is corrupt** — it returns 1970-01-01 for this journal. Full open-access PDF read at `https://www.theglobaljusticenetwork.org/index.php/gjn/article/download/101/75/201`; page numbers below are the PDF's own.

Pettit makes a corporate body a bearer of non-domination (p. 48):
> "First, human beings should be organized into free peoples – peoples whose members count as suitably undominated – with everyone belonging to at least one such group. And, second, **free peoples should constitute corporate bodies that enjoy freedom as non-domination in their relations with one another and with other global bodies**."

He names the dominators, and they include the chapter's (p. 56):
> "Those agents may be stronger states or transnational churches, terrorist networks or **multinational corporations**, or indeed the sorts of bodies, such as the World Bank, the IMF or the World Trade Organization, that are set up by international treaty."

He raises the bearer problem in so many words (p. 59):
> "The question is important because, while it may be obvious why the freedom of an individual matters, **it may not be so clear why the freedom of a corporate body or agent should have the same significance**."

And he states the price (p. 60) — the single most useful sentence in this entire research pass:
> "This means that **whether or not we think there is disvalue in the domination as such of a corporate body – for the record, I do not think there is –** we must recognize that there is disvalue in the domination of a free people: the domination of the state that speaks and acts for them."

So the leading republican licenses a corporate bearer **instrumentally, not intrinsically**. That is the two-level construct, built, with the cost named by its own author.

**He also supplies a ready-made instrument structure.** Three tests, scaling from person to corporate body:
- **eyeball test** (person to person), p. 52: "the protection should enable the person to be able to look others in the eye — by the strictest local standards — without anyone else's power of interference giving them reason for fear or deference."
- **tough-luck test** (citizen to state), p. 55.
- **straight-talk test** (corporate body to global bodies), p. 59: "Different peoples enjoy freedom as non-domination in relation to others — and in relation to non-state bodies — to the extent that their representatives can talk straight in dealing with one another… **no one is forced to adopt the mealy-mouthed tones of the supplicant** and no one is entitled to adopt the tones of a master."

§9's second future direction asks for an instrument for coordinative sovereignty and worries about discriminating it from psychological empowerment. The straight-talk test is a better organization-level item than anything the chapter currently proposes, and it arrives with republican pedigree attached.

### Anderson forecloses the entity reading — by one phrase, which is also the opening

**Anderson, E. (2017). *Private government*. Princeton University Press.** Full book text read by the research stream (**I did not read it myself**; quotations below are theirs, and the page numbers should be spot-checked against a copy).

### The bearer problem is not merely theoretical — EU law has already split on it

Rainone (2025), verified in Part 3a: under the Platform Work Directive, information rights over automated monitoring and decision-making extend to *all* "persons performing platform work," including the self-employed, **but consultation rights remain limited to employees**. ### The three defensible options, and what each costs

**(a) Any coordinated agent, person or organization, on a flat functional criterion.** Whoever occupies the coordinated position in an irreducible triad bears the claim, full stop. This is what §6's "a coordinated actor" already implies.
*Cost:* it walks straight into the normative asymmetry that List, Rafanelli, Rehg, and Pettit himself all assert. A referee quotes List — group agents "should not be given the same rights as individuals" — plus Hindriks's Corporate Autonomy Problem, and the chapter has no reply. There *is* a reply available and it is cheap: coordinative sovereignty is *procedural standing*, not a right to life or a vote, so the asymmetry arguments — which turn on intrinsic moral worth and phenomenal consciousness — do not obviously bite. But the chapter would have to say so explicitly, and saying so is most of the way to option (c) anyway.

**(b) Organization only; persons handled by labour law.**
*Cost:* highest of the three. Anderson shows the workplace case is where the argument has most force and most evidence, and she rejects the entity reading of private government by name. The chapter would be handing away its best material, to a body of law that on Anderson's own showing does not reach the harm. The gig driver in §3.1 would have to go, and with her the vividness that carries §3.

**(c) One construct, two levels: the organization's standing derivative of its members'. ← RECOMMENDED.**
*Cost, stated honestly:* two things. First, the chapter inherits Pettit's admission at p. 60 — there is no disvalue in the domination of a corporate body *as such* — which means an ISV's coordinative sovereignty matters because and insofar as it transmits to the people in and around it. That is a real limit and it will read as a concession. Second, measurement bifurcates: §9's proposed instrument becomes the person-level instrument, and the chapter owes an organization-level one. Pettit's own three tests (eyeball / tough-luck / straight-talk) show how to build it without inventing anything.
*Why it wins anyway:* the precedent stack is unusually strong and unusually convergent — Pettit 2016, List 2018, List & Pettit 2011, Rafanelli 2017, Laborde & Ronzoni 2016, Rehg 2022, and Phillips's normative/derivative distinction all reach the same two-level shape from four separate literatures. A chapter that adopts it is not improvising; it is joining a settled consensus. And the "derivative" vocabulary is standard, so the move can be made in two sentences.

**What the chapter must do either way:** state the bearer in the definitional paragraph, in one clause. Right now the definition says "one" and "the coordinated actor" without ever fixing the referent, while §5.4's "the diagnostic classifies functions, not firms" quietly presupposes an answer. Make it explicit, and take Article 15 of the Platform Work Directive (below) as the demonstration that the question is not academic.

## PART 1c — THE NORMATIVE CORE

### HIRSCHMAN — and TWO CORRECTIONS THE CHAPTER NEEDS (both in §5.3, outside my sections, but they feed §6 directly)

**Neither I nor the research stream ever saw a page of *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*.** Internet Archive is lending-restricted; HathiTrust is Cloudflare-blocked. Every Hirschman quotation below is double-sourced from peer-reviewed articles that quote it at the same page.

> "[t]he presence of the exit alternative can therefore tend to **atrophy the development of the art of voice**" (Hirschman 1970, p. 43)

— quoted at p. 43 in **Taylor, B. R. (2016), *Economic Affairs* 36(2), 133–144, `10.1111/ecaf.12173`** and independently in **Dowding, K., & John, P. (2007), *Political Studies* 56(2), 288–311, `10.1111/j.1467-9248.2007.00688.x`**. Both VERIFIED by the research stream.

**Correction 1 — the "lazy monopoly" gloss looks wrong.** §5.3 says: "a monopoly the members cannot leave can let them grumble undisturbed. Hirschman called it the lazy monopoly, tolerating voice because it need not answer." Hirschman's lazy monopoly runs the *other* way: the monopolist is *comforted by* the exit of its most quality-conscious customers (the Nigerian Railways case). The book's table of contents — reproduced in Schwartz's review, *University of Pennsylvania Law Review* 120(6) (1972), p. 1210, `10.2307/3311348`, VERIFIED — gives ch. 5 as "**How Monopoly Can be Comforted by Competition**." **Check ch. 5 against a physical copy before that sentence stays in.**

**Correction 2 — "exit is what gives voice its force" is half of Hirschman.** The p. 43 line says the opposite direction too: exit *atrophies* voice. Both are in the book. Stating both makes §5.3 stronger, and it hands §6 its hinge for free — under a necessary mediator there is no exit to atrophy voice, so voice is the only power *and* it is unbacked, which is precisely the institutional problem §7 exists to solve.

**Counterweights to answer, not cite:** Warren, M. E. (2011), *APSR* 105(4), 683–701, `10.1017/s0003055411000323`, argues exit *induces* voice; Taylor (2016) argues exit raises voice's epistemic quality; Dowding, John, Mergoupis & Van Vugt (2000), *EJPR* 37(4), 469–495, `10.1023/a:1007134730724` (⚠️ the Wiley DOI `10.1111/1475-6765.00522` is **not** in Crossref — use the `10.1023` one) warn the atrophy thesis has been hard to test.

### THE PLATFORM EVIDENCE — narrower than one would like, and worth saying so

**Nobody has run the head-to-head test.** No study compares outcomes for actors who pursue independence against those who pursue voice. What exists, all **VERIFIED BY ME** via Crossref:

- **He, S., Peng, J., Li, J., & Xu, L. (2020). Impact of platform owner's entry on third-party stores. *Information Systems Research, 31*(4), 1467–1484. https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2020.0957** — the best single empirical hit. Third-party sellers respond to platform-owner entry by **disintermediating**, and their demand drop is significant *only offline* — self-inflicted. The authors: *"**disintermediation might be an overreaction to the entry of the platform, because their online demand is not significantly affected**."*
- **Maffie, M. D. (2023). Becoming a pirate: Independence as an alternative to exit in the gig economy. *British Journal of Industrial Relations, 61*(1), 46–67. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjir.12668** — ⚠️ **note the year: Crossref shows online 2022-04-10 but the issue is dated 2023-03. Cite 2023.** Drivers who cannot exit build off-app "pirate" clienteles (~1 in 5; dependence coefficient 0.400, p < 0.03), and the cost is **uninsured** — off-app trips void both commercial and personal auto coverage, plus deactivation risk. **Handle carefully: Maffie does not conclude these drivers are worse off and does not measure earnings.** Cite it for *the independence route is what dependence produces, and it privatises risk*.
- **Wood, A. J., & Lehdonvirta, V. (2021). Antagonism beyond employment: How the 'subordinated agency' of labour platforms generates conflict in the remote gig economy. *Socio-Economic Review, 19*(4), 1369–1396. https://doi.org/10.1093/ser/mwab016** — the theoretical anchor: platforms genuinely increase workers' agency *toward clients* while creating a new subordination *to the platform*, and workers themselves conclude the remedy is voice toward the platform. That is the chapter's argument, observed.
- **Maric, S., Schuessler, E. S., & Thäter, L. (2025). Neither employment nor self-employment: Avenues towards functional platform-mediated work systems. *British Journal of Industrial Relations, 63*(2), 287–304. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjir.12855** — the strongest theoretical warrant that independence is the wrong variable: what matters is "transparency, co-determination and control… **irrespective of whether platform workers are classified as employees or not**."
- Also: Cennamo, Ozalp & Kretschmer (2018), *ISR* 29(2), 461–478, `10.1287/isre.2018.0779` — multihoming, the standard way to reduce platform dependence, carries a measurable **quality penalty**. Zhu & Liu (2018), *SMJ* 39(10), 2618–2642, `10.1002/smj.2932` — a verified irony worth a footnote: the managerial implication is to **increase platform-specific investment** as protection, i.e. deepen dependence.

**Counter-evidence to engage honestly:** Gu & Zhu (2021), *Management Science* 67(2), 794–807, `10.1287/mnsc.2020.3583` (disintermediation is rational fee-avoidance and harms the *platform*); Nemkova, Demirel & Baines (2019), `10.1111/ntwe.12148` (freelancers with strong entrepreneurial orientation do better); Schor et al. ### The single largest gap: codetermination and works councils

### And the provision §7 should be quoting but is not

§7 says the platform-work directive "begins to attach collective rights to the algorithmically managed." The directive does considerably more than begin, and the operative text is quotable. **VERIFIED BY ME** — I fetched the full consolidated text at `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402831` and extracted these verbatim:

> **Article 13(2).** "Member States shall ensure that information and consultation, as defined in Article 2, points (f) and (g), of Directive 2002/14/EC, of workers' representatives by digital labour platforms also covers decisions likely to lead to the introduction of or to substantial changes in the use of automated monitoring systems or automated decision-making systems."

> **Article 13(3).** "The platform workers' representatives may be assisted by an expert of their choice, in so far as this is necessary for them to examine the matter that is the subject of information and consultation and formulate an opinion. **Where a digital labour platform has more than 250 workers in the Member State concerned, the expenses of the expert shall be borne by the digital labour platform**, provided that they are proportionate."

Article 13(3) is the chapter's own argument enacted in EU law. §6 says algorithmacy is the precondition of coordinative sovereignty and that the competence is costly and unevenly held; Article 13(3) makes the mediator *pay for the competence* of those who contest it. That is the answer to Ananny and Crawford written into a directive — legibility plus a funded institution able to act on it. §7 currently describes the directive too vaguely to use this.

> **Article 15.** "Representatives of persons performing platform work other than workers' representatives shall be able to exercise the rights provided to workers' representatives under Article 8(2), Article 9(1) and (4), Article 10(4) and Article 11(2) **only insofar as they are acting on behalf of persons performing platform work who are not platform workers, with regard to the protection of their personal data.**"

Article 15 is the **bearer problem in EU law** (see Part 1b): non-employee bearers get a narrower set of rights, confined to data protection. Rainone's assessment (Part 3a) makes the same point analytically. The chapter's protection/standing distinction is the fault line along which the directive itself splits.

Transposition deadline: **2 December 2026** (Article 29(1)). The chapter says "now in transposition," which is right, but the date is worth printing.

## PART 4 — FRESH GROUNDING

### 4.1 Ostrom "under enclosure": the move is defensible, but the chapter is citing the wrong Ostrom

The §6 sentence is:

> "It is not collective ownership of the resource but a claim to collective-choice arrangements over the coordination's rules, standing the owner cannot unilaterally rewrite without contest."

**Ostrom 1990 does not supply the warrant for that sentence. Schlager & Ostrom 1992 does — precisely, and by name.** This is the single highest-value find in this research pass.

> Schlager, E., & Ostrom, E. (1992). Property-rights regimes and natural resources: A conceptual analysis. *Land Economics, 68*(3), 249–262. https://doi.org/10.2307/3146375

- **VERIFIED** Crossref `https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2307/3146375`: authors Edella Schlager and Elinor Ostrom, *Land Economics*, vol 68, issue 3, 1992-08.
- **VERIFIED** full text read from `https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/PropertyRightsRegimesNaturalResources.SchlagerOstrom1992.pdf`. The article's own footer line reads: `Land Economics * August 1992 * 68(3):249-62`. Running heads confirm pages 250, 252, 254, 256, 258, 260, 262. Page range 249–262 confirmed from the article itself.
- JSTOR stable URL: `https://www.jstor.org/stable/3146375`.

**Why it is the right citation.** Schlager and Ostrom decompose property into five rights and sort them into two levels. Verbatim from the article:

> "Individuals who have access and withdrawal rights may or may not have more extensive rights authorizing participation in collective-choice actions. **The distinction between rights at an operational-level and rights at a collective-choice level is crucial. It is the difference between exercising a right and participating in the definition of future rights to be exercised. The authority to devise future operational-level rights is what makes collective-choice rights so powerful.** In regard to common-pool resources, collective-choice property rights include management, exclusion, and alienation."

Their definitions, verbatim:

> "Access: The right to enter a defined physical property."
> "Withdrawal: The right to obtain the 'products' of a resource."
> "Management: The right to regulate internal use patterns and transform the resource by making improvements."
> "Exclusion: The right to determine who will have an access right, and how that right may be transferred."
> "Alienation: The right to sell or lease either or both of the above collective-choice rights."

And the four positions (their Table 1, "Bundles of Rights Associated with Positions" — Owner holds all five; Proprietor holds access, withdrawal, management, exclusion; Claimant holds access, withdrawal, management; Authorized User holds access and withdrawal only):

> "We define as 'claimants' individuals who possess the same rights as authorized users plus the collective-choice right of management. With the right of management, claimants have the collective-choice authority to devise operational-level rights of withdrawal. They cannot, however, specify who may or may not have access to resources, nor can they alienate their right of management."

> "'Proprietors' are defined as individuals who possess collective-choice rights to participate in management and exclusion. Proprietors authorize who may access resources and how resources may be utilized, however, they do not have the right to alienate either of these collective-choice rights."

And the abstract's own headline claim:

> "We do not find that 'owners' are the only resource users who make long-term investments in the improvement of resource systems. Proprietors face incentives that are frequently substantial enough to encourage similar long-term investments. Even claimants may manage use patterns to an extent not predicted by a simpler property-rights dichotomy."

**The Maine lobster case is the chapter's exact structure, empirically.** Verbatim:

> "The state of Maine has owned the lobster groundsr off its coast since its founding. The most general property-rights regime is one of government ownership with de jure authorized users status extended to all who obtain licenses (Acheson 1975). In addition to de jure authorized user rights, lob[stermen have developed de facto proprietor rights among themselves]"

(The bracketed continuation is the running text across the column break; the substantive point — *de facto* proprietor rights held by users over a resource **owned by another party** — is stated in the article and elaborated in its §IV.)

**What this does for §6.** The chapter's "commons under enclosure" move stops being a hedge and becomes a named position in Ostrom's own framework:

- The platform-dependent actor as currently situated is an **authorized user**: access and withdrawal only, no say in the rules. That is exactly the chapter's "protected but unstanding actor."
- Coordinative sovereignty is the claim to move from authorized user to **claimant** (management rights: participation in devising the operational rules) or to **proprietor** (management plus exclusion).
- Crucially, **the chapter does not have to claim ownership or alienation.** Schlager and Ostrom's whole point is that management and exclusion rights are separable from ownership and can be held by non-owners over a resource someone else owns, both de jure and de facto.
- The chapter's phrase "collective-choice arrangements" is Ostrom's own term of art, and Schlager & Ostrom is where it is defined against the operational level.

**Recommended rewrite target for §6 (research note, not prose):** replace or supplement the Ostrom 1990 citation at the "collective-choice arrangements over the coordination's rules" sentence with Schlager & Ostrom 1992, and name the claimant/proprietor positions. This converts the chapter's weakest concessive paragraph into its most precisely grounded one, and it pre-empts the referee who says "Ostrom doesn't apply, the platform is owned."

**Caveat to state honestly.** Schlager and Ostrom analyze natural-resource property rights, not algorithmic coordination. The transfer is analogical. What transfers cleanly is the *analytic* claim — that management/collective-choice rights are separable from ownership — not any empirical finding about fisheries. Say so.

### 4.2 The Muldoon & Raekstad wedge — the reading needs THREE corrections

I read the full published version (UvA-DARE, CC BY-NC, final published version, `https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/144931560/muldoon-raekstad-2022-algorithmic-domination-in-the-gig-economy.pdf`).

The chapter's sentence is:

> "Muldoon and Raekstad (2022) analyze algorithmic management as domination and recommend exit through cooperative ownership, and that remedy locates the wedge. Worker ownership can change who sets a necessary mediator's rules, but it cannot make the mediator leaveable."

**Correction 1 — "exit" is wrong.** They do not propose exit from the mediator in any sense. Their thesis is the opposite: the algorithm stays, and its politics change with its ownership. Verbatim:

> "We then argue that the dominating aspect of algorithms in the workplace is not intrinsic to the technology itself, but is part of the power relations established within capitalist enterprises. As a result, we briefly examine an alternative possibility of algorithms utilized by platform co-operatives, which we argue could potentially involve a non-dominating use of algorithms in work processes."

Their worked example is CoopCycle, whose software they describe as keeping the dispatch function intact:

> "CoopCycle has created its own software, for example, that enables members of the federation to manage their deliveries and relationships with restaurants and customers using bespoke software created exclusively for use by co-operative partners. The software has two main components: a user-facing interface that allows customers to order from restaurants' menus and pay through an online payment system; and a dispatch system that uses geolocation data to match couriers with orders as they are entered into the system and calculates routes and estimated times for delivery."

So the correct verb is not "exit." It is a change of ownership and control over a mediator that remains in place. **This actually helps the chapter**: Muldoon and Raekstad are already arguing that the mediator is unleaveable and the question is who governs it. Saying they "recommend exit" misstates them and, worse, throws away the agreement.

**Correction 2 — "recommend" is too strong.** Their own framing is hedged: "we *briefly examine* an alternative *possibility*"; "Platform co-operatives offer an alternative vision"; "Democratic control by workers presents *one possible avenue*"; and they close the section with "some caution is needed in adopting co-operative solution." Their affirmative statement is:

> "We contend that democratic ownership and control of productive assets and equal rights of participation in decision-making over the conditions and processes of work could transform the role of algorithms at work and enable the beginning of a broader discussion about how democratic collectives should control the use of technology in the workplace."

"Point toward" or "gesture at" is fair. "Recommend" overstates.

**Correction 3 — and this is the referee risk — THEY ALREADY ANTICIPATE A RESIDUAL-DOMINATION OBJECTION.** Verbatim:

> "Another concern is that a similar relationship of algorithmic domination could emerge between a class of managers within the co-operative and workers who do not take advantage of the democratic rights available to them. In an organisation in which there is a strong ideology of harmony and mutual co-operation, workers may feel less able to bring up grievances than in a private firm. Without proper democratic participation, managers could attempt to drive up efficiency against at the expense of workers who are not given sufficient opportunity to voice their concerns (Kasmir, 1996: 13)."

A referee who knows the paper will say: *they already said domination can survive worker ownership; your "wedge" is their footnote.*

**But the wedge survives, and here is the exact distinction the chapter must draw.** Their residual-domination worry is about **internal managerialism and democratic apathy** — a co-op can develop a managerial class, and members may not exercise the rights they hold. That is a failure of the co-op's *democratic practice*. The chapter's claim is **structural and prior to any question of practice**: even a perfectly democratic driver-owned dispatch system still coordinates each driver through a mediator no individual driver can bypass, so the standing question does not go away when the ownership question is answered. Their worry is that democracy can decay. The chapter's point is that democracy at the level of the collective does not by itself constitute standing at the level of the individual coordinated actor inside an irreducible coordination. Those are different claims, and the chapter needs one sentence naming the difference.

**Two bonus finds inside the paper that the chapter should use.**

(a) Their account of domination is explicitly **interpersonal**, and they say so:

> "Our understanding of algorithmic domination in the gig economy therefore emphasizes the social relationship and structural power inequalities at the heart of the system. It is an interpersonal account of domination in which bosses possess a robust capacity to interfere with workers through the operation of a system of algorithmic management."

This is a second differentiator. Their dominator is a *boss* who acts through the algorithm. The chapter's mediator is a third party that commits outcomes for two parties neither of whom employs it (a marketplace seller has no boss). The chapter's territory includes cases theirs does not reach.

(b) They lean on Gädeke for the claim that domination is never dyadic — which is the chapter's triad, arriving from the political-theory side:

> "As Gädeke (2020: 211) persuasively argues, a relationship of interpersonal domination is never simply dyadic because it is structurally constituted by legal rules, social norms, and peripheral agents who help enforce and reproduce the structural power relationship between the dominator and the dominated."

> Gädeke, D. (2020). Does a mugger dominate? Episodic power and the structural dimension of domination. *Journal of Political Philosophy, 28*(2), 199–221. https://doi.org/10.1111/jopp.12202
- **VERIFIED** Crossref `https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/jopp.12202`: sole author Dorothea Gädeke, *Journal of Political Philosophy*, vol 28, issue 2, pp. 199–221.
- I have not read Gädeke's full text; the characterization above is Muldoon & Raekstad's, quoted. If the chapter cites Gädeke directly, read her first.

**A third source in the same seam, found via the same search:**

> Cicerchia, L. (2022). Structural domination in the labor market. *European Journal of Political Theory, 21*(1), 4–24. https://doi.org/10.1177/1474885119851094
- **VERIFIED** Crossref: sole author Lillian Cicerchia, EJPT, vol 21, issue 1, pp. 4–24, online 2019-05-24, issue dated 2022-01.
- From the Crossref abstract (abstract only; I did not read the full text): "Critics of neo-republicanism argue that this definition of freedom as non-domination precludes a structural analysis of domination, which would explain and critique the ways in which societies produce structural domination unintentionally... It argues that critics are correct to think that the neo-republican principle of non-domination has an insufficient scope, but that an alternative account of structural domination should still support the neo-republican idea that agency must be involved."
- Relevance: §6 currently rests non-domination on Pettit and Skinner alone. A referee from political theory will note that the chapter's mediator is a *system* whose "arbitrary power" is not obviously an agent's, which is the standard objection to applying non-domination here. Cicerchia and Gädeke are the two moves that answer it, and Muldoon & Raekstad already used Gädeke for exactly this purpose.
