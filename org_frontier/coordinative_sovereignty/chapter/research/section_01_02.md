## (1) REVIEWER THREAT-HUNT

### 1.1 THE HEADLINE PROBLEM: the chapter misreads Repetto (2025)

This is the most serious finding in the whole review, and it is load-bearing twice — at line 62 (§1's Bodin
defense) and at line 552 (§6's neighbour-distinguishing).

**What the chapter says Repetto argues.**

- Line 62: "That is the discipline the concept-stretching critique demands (Repetto, 2025)."
- Lines 551–553: "Repetto (2025) has charged the digital-sovereignty literature with concept stretching: the
  term reaches so many actors, from states to firms to communities to individuals, that it loses precision."

**What Repetto actually argues.** Verbatim abstract, retrieved from the Semantic Scholar Graph API record for
DOI 10.1002/poi3.70011 (`https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1002/poi3.70011`):

> "Digital sovereignty and its cognate concepts have become staples of the digital governance debate; however,
> in the search for a shared understanding of the concept, the proliferation of claims to digital sovereignty
> by multiple different actors remains a contested issue. This study provides a review of 240 works, coded
> according to disciplinary area, actors mentioned in relation to digital sovereignty, and the types of
> relations explicitly or implicitly conceptualised within each work. **I argue that the abundance of actors to
> which digital sovereignty is ascribed is not an issue per se; rather, the issue is that significant portions
> of the literature cluster around a digital sovereign 'of choice' and limit conversation with other strands.
> Those works that acknowledge the possibility of multiple coexisting digital sovereigns, or that see digital
> sovereignty as something that arises from the interactions among multiple actors, neither stretch the concept
> of sovereignty nor turn digital sovereignty into something different from sovereignty tout court: instead,
> they explore ways to innovate the concept of sovereignty as a whole and problematise our long-held
> assumptions about it.**" (emphasis added)

**Consequence.** The chapter attributes to Repetto precisely the position Repetto's article is written to
*reject*. He states that actor-proliferation "is not an issue per se." The chapter's line 552 gloss ("the term
reaches so many actors … that it loses precision") is the thesis of his title's first horn, which he answers in
the negative. His actual complaint is *siloing*: strands that pick a sovereign "of choice" and stop talking to
each other.

**This is recoverable and the repair strengthens the chapter.** Repetto is an ally, not an adversary:

1. His conclusion — that multi-actor and *relational* accounts "explore ways to innovate the concept of
   sovereignty as a whole" — is a licence for coordinative sovereignty, which is defined by a *relation*
   (standing within an unexitable mediation) and which posits multiple coexisting sovereigns (the platform as
   functional sovereign, the coordinated actor as claimant).
2. His actual charge (strands that do not converse) is a charge the chapter can *satisfy* by explicitly
   bridging the state-centric strand (Floridi, Roberts et al.) with the grassroots strand (Couture & Toupin)
   and the organizational strand (Fratini et al., Schmuntzsch et al.).
3. The chapter's Sartorian move (add attributes, narrow extension) can then be presented as its own discipline
   rather than as a concession extracted by Repetto.

**Risk if unrepaired.** Policy & Internet / digital-sovereignty studies is a small field. Repetto is a live and
plausible referee for this volume. A misattribution of this kind, in the sentence that carries the chapter's
answer to its central conceptual objection, is the sort of thing that ends a review badly.

**Recommended replacement framing:** cite Repetto (2025) for the *taxonomy* of positions ("stretching" vs
"innovation") and for the finding that the literature silos, then place coordinative sovereignty on the
innovation side of his own dichotomy — with Sartori (1970) supplying the discipline test.

### 1.2 THE BODIN DEFENSE (lines 55–64) — verdict: **NARROWS**, and needs two citations it does not have

The defense has three moves. They fare differently.

**Move A — "Bodin made sovereignty supreme and indivisible."** *Defensible.* This is Julian Franklin's own
reading, and Franklin is the editor of the very edition cited, so the chapter is on safe ground. (Franklin's
*Jean Bodin and the Rise of Absolutist Theory*, CUP 1973, argues Bodin abandoned the mixed constitution and
made sovereignty indivisible — UNVERIFIED this session, cited from memory of the secondary literature; do not
cite without checking.)

*But a well-read referee has a counter-move.* The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Sovereignty (Daniel Philpott;
last substantive revision 17 September 2024; `https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sovereignty/`) is more
cautious. Fetched this session, it reports that Bodin "thought that the body that exercised sovereignty was
bound by natural and divine law," would "respect customary and property rights," and that he held "the form of
government that exercised sovereign powers could legitimately vary among monarchy, aristocracy and democracy."

**How to use this rather than fear it.** Bodin's sovereign was *absolute but not unlimited*. That fact makes the
chapter's concession too generous: the classic sense already admitted external constraint, so a sovereignty
constrained by contestable rules is not the alien thing the chapter concedes it to be. One clause turns a
defensive paragraph into an offensive one.

**Move B — "The sense this chapter takes is the later, democratic one: sovereignty as the standing to hold
power to account through the medium in which power is exercised."**

*This is where the defense is weakest, and it is the panel's headline fix.* The gloss is not what the
popular-sovereignty tradition standardly means. Philpott's SEP entry, fetched this session, describes the
democratic transformation as a change in the *holder*, not in the concept:

> "Rousseau, far different from Bodin or Hobbes, saw the collective people within a state as the sovereign,
> ruling through their general will." … "In constitutional government, it is the people ruling through a body of
> law that is sovereign."

Popular sovereignty on the canonical account is about *authorship* — the people as ultimate source of authority,
constituent power — not about *holding power to account*. Accountability and contestation are, in republican
theory, treated as a distinct thing from sovereignty. A political-theory referee will say: "the author has
conflated popular sovereignty with contestatory accountability, and then called the conflation a tradition."

**Verdict: the tradition the chapter needs is REAL and CITABLE, but the chapter currently invents its own gloss
without naming a single source for it.** Two sources supply exactly the reading the chapter wants, and neither
is currently cited. Adding them converts an invented sense into a documented one.

**(i) Habermas, "Popular Sovereignty as Procedure" — the single best citation available.**

**Habermas, J. (1996). Popular sovereignty as procedure. In *Between Facts and Norms: Contributions to a
Discourse Theory of Law and Democracy* (W. Rehg, Trans., pp. 463–490). MIT Press. (Original essay published
1988.)**

Crossref record for the appendix: DOI **10.7551/mitpress/1564.003.0013**
(`https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Popular+Sovereignty+as+Procedure`). **The page range
463–490 is VERIFIED from the book's own table of contents** in the full text read this session ("Appendix I.
Popular Sovereignty as Procedure (1988) 463 / Appendix II. Citizenship and National Identity (1990) 491"). Also
reprinted in J. Bohman & W. Rehg (Eds.), *Deliberative Democracy*, MIT Press 1997, pp. 35–66, DOI
**10.7551/mitpress/2324.003.0005**. Both DOIs VERIFIED via Crossref.

Read this session in the full text of *Between Facts and Norms* (PDF at
`https://teddykw2.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/jc3bcrgen-habermas-between-facts-and-norms.pdf`;
the passage runs across the printed pages **486–487** of the MIT edition, page numbers visible in the scan):

> "The public sphere thus reproduces itself self-referentially, and in doing so reveals the place to which the
> expectation of a sovereign self-organization of society has withdrawn. **The idea of popular sovereignty is
> thereby desubstantialized.** Even the notion that a network of associations could replace the dismissed 'body'
> of the people — that it could occupy the vacant seat of the sovereign, so to speak — is too concrete." (p. 486)

> "This fully dispersed sovereignty is not even embodied in the heads of the associated members. Rather, if one
> can still speak of 'embodiment' at all, then **sovereignty is found in those subjectless forms of communication
> that regulate the flow of discursive opinion- and will-formation** in such a way that their fallible outcomes
> have the presumption of practical reason on their side. Subjectless and anonymous, an intersubjectively
> dissolved popular sovereignty withdraws into democratic procedures and the demanding communicative
> presuppositions of their implementation. … **Set communicatively aflow, sovereignty makes itself felt in the
> power of public discourses.**" (pp. 486–487)

This is, almost word for word, the chapter's own thesis: sovereignty relocated *into the medium of
communication*. Habermas even calls it "desubstantialized," which is a much better word than the chapter's
"later, democratic sense." The chapter already cites Habermas (1962/1989) for the public sphere; it is citing
the wrong Habermas book for its sovereignty claim. **This single addition closes the biggest hole in §1.**

**(ii) Pettit, *On the People's Terms: A Republican Theory and Model of Democracy*, CUP 2012.**
DOI **10.1017/CBO9781139017428** (VERIFIED via Crossref; ISBNs 9781107005112 / 9780521182126 / 9781139017428).
Pettit reconstructs popular control as *contestatory* and equally shared rather than as command — a democratic
conception of sovereignty whose currency is standing to contest, not the power to author. The chapter already
cites Pettit (1997) in §6; citing the 2012 book in §1 makes the "democratic register" claim a positioned one
rather than an assertion. **UNVERIFIED at the level of a specific quotable sentence** — I confirmed metadata
only, not full text.

**Optional third:** Richard Tuck, *The Sleeping Sovereign: The Invention of Modern Democracy*, CUP 2015 (some
records say 2016), DOI **10.1017/CBO9781316417782** (VERIFIED via Crossref). Tuck separates sovereignty from
government, which supports the chapter's "shared, partial, exercised through voice" without the chapter having
to claim that Bodin's absolutism was simply superseded. Metadata verified; content not read this session.

**Optional fourth:** Dieter Grimm, *Sovereignty: The Origin and Future of a Political and Legal Concept*,
trans. Belinda Cooper, Columbia UP 2015, DOI **10.7312/columbia/9780231164252.001.0001** (VERIFIED via
Crossref). The standard modern conceptual history; useful as a one-line hedge that the concept has already
survived several redefinitions.

**Move C — "It adds two attributes … and it narrows the term's extension rather than widening it."**

*This is Sartori's ladder of abstraction, stated correctly, and uncited.* Intension up, extension down. The
chapter should cite it directly:

- **Sartori, G. (1970). Concept misformation in comparative politics. *American Political Science Review,
  64*(4), 1033–1053. DOI 10.2307/1958356.** VERIFIED via Crossref API.
- **Collier, D., & Mahon, J. E. (1993). Conceptual "stretching" revisited: Adapting categories in comparative
  analysis. *American Political Science Review, 87*(4), 845–855. DOI 10.2307/2938818.** VERIFIED via Crossref
  API.

Two reasons this matters more than a courtesy citation:

1. **Repetto himself cites Sartori 1970.** Confirmed by resolving Repetto's `referenced_works` through OpenAlex
   (`https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1002/poi3.70011` → 45 referenced works, of which one is Sartori,
   "Concept Misformation in Comparative Politics," 1970). Citing Sartori puts the chapter on the same ground as
   the critique it answers, rather than paraphrasing that ground unattributed.
2. **Collier & Mahon is the pre-emption of the obvious counter.** They argue that many social-science concepts
   are *radial* or family-resemblance categories rather than classical ones, so the tidy intension/extension
   trade the chapter relies on does not always hold. A referee who knows the concepts literature will raise
   this. Citing Collier & Mahon and conceding that sovereignty is plausibly a radial category — with
   coordinative sovereignty as a non-central but structurally motivated variant — is stronger than pretending
   the classical ladder settles it.

**Overall verdict on the Bodin defense: NARROWS.** The move is sound. It is currently executed without any of
the three sources that would make it a scholarly claim rather than an assertion. With Habermas (1996, App. I),
Pettit (2012), and Sartori (1970) added, it becomes the strongest paragraph in §1. Without them, a
political-theory or digital-sovereignty referee will read lines 57–59 as invention.

### 1.3 The pairing thesis (every sovereignty ↔ a communicative sensibility) — verdict: **NARROWS, and it must
narrow explicitly or it dies**

> **Read §7.1 with this section.** A later full-text sweep found a nearer rival than any listed here —
> **Habermas 1962/1989**, whom the chapter already cites as decorative support at line 106 — and a
> third-term precedent the chapter must engage (**Vee 2017, *Coding Literacy***). The verdict below stands;
> §7.1 sharpens where the surviving novelty actually lives.

**The claim under test.** Lines 69–73: "This chapter adds a different axis. Every historical form of
sovereignty has depended on a communicative sensibility that made the authority legible and contestable to
those subject to it. … Three such sensibilities mark three eras: oracy, literacy, and algorithmacy."

**The problem.** The pairing of political authority with its communicative medium is not a new axis. It is one
of the most worked-over theses in twentieth-century media theory, and the chapter cites two of its members
(Innis 1951, Eisenstein 1979) while claiming to add the axis they established. A media-studies referee will
open at line 70 and stop reading charitably.

**THE NEAREST RIVALS, in order of danger.**

**(1) Goody, J. (1986). *The Logic of Writing and the Organization of Society*. Cambridge University Press.
DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511621598.** **VERIFIED** via Crossref (monograph, CUP, 1986-12-18). *Full text not read
this session — cite at thesis level.*

This is the most dangerous precedent and the chapter does not cite it. Goody's book is explicitly about how
writing reorganizes religion, the economy, the state, and the law — the *institutional* claim, not the
cognitive one. The chapter cites Goody (1977), *The Domestication of the Savage Mind*, which is the cognitive
book and therefore the one the ethnographers demolished. Citing the wrong Goody is a double cost: it hands a
referee the weakest version of the claim, and it leaves the strong version uncredited. **Cite Goody 1986.**

**(2) Deibert, R. J. (1997). *Parchment, Printing, and Hypermedia: Communication in World Order
Transformation*. Columbia University Press.** Book **UNVERIFIED at DOI level** — Crossref returns only review
records for it (e.g. Hampson's review, DOI 10.2307/40203258, which is a *review*, **not the book; do not cite
that DOI for the book**). Existence, title, author, year, and publisher confirmed from the review records.

This is the direct precedent in political science. Deibert argues that changes in the mode of communication
(parchment → printing → hypermedia) produce transformations in world order and in the form of political
authority — including the emergence and the erosion of state sovereignty. It is a book about media and
*sovereignty specifically*, published in international relations, three media-eras long. **If a
digital-sovereignty referee knows one media-theory book, it is this one, and the chapter's line 70 says
Deibert's thesis is a new axis.**

**(3) Innis, H. A. (1950). *Empire and Communications*; (1951). *The Bias of Communication*.** Innis's
time-bias/space-bias argument is that a civilization's dominant medium biases the form of empire it can
sustain — durable media (stone, parchment) toward temporal continuity and religious authority, light media
(papyrus, paper) toward spatial extension and administrative authority. That is the pairing thesis, in 1950.
The chapter's line 112 gets Innis right ("traced how a civilization's dominant medium biases its form of
power"), and then line 70 claims the axis anyway.

**(4) Havelock, E. A. (1963). *Preface to Plato*. Harvard University Press. DOI 10.4159/9780674038431.**
**VERIFIED** via Crossref. And *The Muse Learns to Write* (Yale, 1986). Havelock's argument that the alphabet
made Greek abstract thought — and, with it, a new political vocabulary — possible is the oral→literate
transition case the chapter's §2.1 rehearses without citing.

**(5) Clanchy, M. T. (1979). *From Memory to Written Record: England 1066–1307*.** **Book UNVERIFIED at DOI
level** — Crossref returns only reviews (DOI 10.1086/352578; DOI 10.2307/2847898), which confirm the title,
author, and subject. Clanchy is the *historical* case study of exactly §2.1→§2.2: how English royal
administration shifted from oral-memorial to documentary proof, and what the shift did to who could exercise
and contest authority. It is the source the chapter's §2.1 and §2.2 are implicitly reconstructing.

**(6) McLuhan, Kittler, Castells.** McLuhan's *The Gutenberg Galaxy* (1962) and *Understanding Media* (1964)
are the popular form of the pairing thesis; Kittler's *Discourse Networks 1800/1900* (Stanford UP, English
translation 1990, **DOI 10.1515/9781503621633**, **VERIFIED** via Crossref) makes the harder claim that the
media system determines what can be said, thought, and administered; Castells's *Communication Power* (OUP
2009) makes the contemporary version for networked media. **All content thesis-level; none read this session.**

**VERDICT: the novelty claim as currently phrased DIES; the chapter's actual contribution SURVIVES if it is
restated.** The pairing thesis belongs to Innis, Havelock, Goody, Clanchy, Eisenstein, Kittler, and Deibert.
What the chapter actually adds is three things none of them has:

1. **A third term with a structural definition.** Nobody in that lineage names a competence for coordination
   through an opaque, adaptive, *interested* algorithmic third party. The medium theorists' media are things
   one *reads or hears*; the chapter's medium is a party that *reads you back and commits your outcomes*. That
   is a genuinely different object, and the triad is what makes it different.
2. **A criterion for "change in kind" that is coordination-structural rather than cognitive.** Line 363 has it.
   None of the precedents has a test; they have periodizations. A test is what lets the chapter say that the
   digital-sovereignty layer is *not* a change in kind while algorithmacy is, and it is what connects §2 to §5.
3. **A remedy link.** Innis and Deibert describe transformations. The chapter says which sovereignty is
   available to a coordinated actor and which institutions build it. That is the move from media history to
   organizational and policy prescription, and it is what the volume wants.

**Recommended rewrite of line 69–70.** Concede the lineage in one sentence and claim the third term, not the
axis: *media theory has paired forms of authority with their media since Innis; what it has not named is the
competence a coordination through an interested algorithmic third party requires.* That costs a clause and
buys the chapter its credibility with the exact referee most likely to attack §2.

### 1.3b "Oracy" is a borrowed term from education research, and a referee will know it

**VERIFIED origin.** The word was coined by Andrew Wilkinson in 1965, on the model of literacy and numeracy, to
name competence in speaking and listening in school English:

- **Wilkinson, A. (1965). The concept of oracy. *English in Education, 2*(A2), 3–5. DOI
  10.1111/j.1754-8845.1965.tb01326.x.** VERIFIED via Crossref.
- **Wilkinson, A. (1965). The concept of oracy. *Educational Review, 17*(4), 11–15. DOI
  10.1080/0013191770170401a.** VERIFIED via Crossref. (The same issue carries "Influences on oracy," 40–57, DOI
  10.1080/0013191770170401c, and "Spoken English in school," 58–71, DOI 10.1080/0013191770170401d.)
- **Wilkinson, A. M. (1968). The implications of oracy. *Educational Review, 20*(2), 123–135. DOI
  10.1080/0013191680200205.** VERIFIED via Crossref.
- **Wilkinson, A. (1970). The concept of oracy. *The English Journal, 59*(1), 71. DOI 10.2307/811736.**
  VERIFIED via Crossref.

**The risk.** The chapter uses "oracy" for a *political competence in pre-literate orders* — giving testimony,
holding others to their word, carrying customary rules (lines 80–82). Wilkinson's oracy is a *pedagogical*
category about children's speaking and listening skills, and it has a live contemporary literature in UK
education policy. A media/literacy-studies referee will notice the repurposing and may read it as the author
not knowing where the word came from.

**The fix is cheap and it strengthens the chapter.** One footnote: the term is Wilkinson's (1965), coined for
speaking-and-listening competence in education, and the chapter extends it to the political competence an oral
order required, on the same analogy to literacy that Wilkinson used. Acknowledging the source turns a
vulnerability into evidence of care — and it does the same work the chapter needs for "algorithmacy," which is
also a coinage on the literacy model.

### 1.4 "Digital sovereignty is extended literacy (same kind); algorithmacy is a change in kind" — is this
consistent? Who objects?

**The internal tension.** The chapter cites Hildebrandt (2015) at line 240 for exactly the proposition that
undercuts its own §2.3:

> Chapter, lines 240–242: "Hildebrandt (2015) distinguishes the mode of law that operates through written rules
> from the modes that operate through code and data, and argues that the first cannot straightforwardly govern
> the second."

If text-driven normativity and code/data-driven normativity differ *in kind* — Hildebrandt's own claim — then
the change in kind happened at **code**, not at the algorithmic mediator, and §2.3's "digital sovereignty is
literacy extended, its logic continuous with the literate order" (lines 122, 130–131) is in direct tension with
the chapter's own §3.2 citation. A referee reading both sections will notice.

**External objectors, all of whom say the break came earlier than the chapter says:**

- **Chun, W. H. K. (2005). On software, or the persistence of visual knowledge. *Grey Room, 18*, 26–51. DOI
  10.1162/1526381043320741.** VERIFIED via Crossref. And **Chun, W. H. K. (2011). *Programmed Visions: Software
  and Memory*. MIT Press. DOI 10.7551/mitpress/9780262015424.001.0001.** VERIFIED via Crossref. Chun's argument
  is that source code is not transparently readable and that treating it as a readable text is an ideology of
  control. Content not read in full this session — cite at thesis level only, or read before quoting.
- **The New Literacy Studies / "new literacies" line** (Lankshear & Knobel and successors) treats digital
  literacy as a *different kind* of literacy rather than an extension. UNVERIFIED — no specific edition checked
  this session. Do not cite without verifying.
- **Kittler**, on the non-readability of the descent from source code to voltage. See the precedent supplement.

**The defense that works.** The chapter's own criterion for "change in kind" is not epistemic (readability) but
**coordination-structural**: it says so at lines 360–363 ("Oracy, literacy, and algorithmacy are qualitatively
distinct coordination *regimes* … Each enables forms of authority the prior regime structurally cannot
instantiate"). That is a good criterion and it does the job. The problem is that it is stated in §4, 200 lines
after §2.3 makes the "extended literacy" claim that needs it. **Recommendation: move or preview the
coordination-regime criterion into §2.3.** Then the Hildebrandt tension dissolves — code-driven normativity is a
new *object of reading* but not a new *coordination regime*, because a compliance officer and a subject of a
code-driven rule still stand on opposite sides of an inspectable artefact. The triad is what changes the regime.

**Who still objects after the repair.** Someone from media/literacy studies will say the chapter's ladder
(oracy → literacy → digital literacy → algorithmacy) flattens a field that has spent forty years arguing there
is no single ladder — that is the Street/New Literacy Studies position the chapter already concedes at lines
356–359 for cognition but not for its own periodization. The concession should be extended: the chapter's three
"eras" are *ideal types of coordination regime*, not a historical sequence societies pass through.

### 1.5 What does the oracy/literacy frame BUY that "opacity plus non-exit" would not? (the editor's open flag)

The frame earns its word count only if it does something the direct account cannot. Four candidate answers, in
descending order of defensibility:

**(a) It supplies a diffusion mechanism, and therefore a research question and a politics.** This is the
strongest answer and the chapter half-makes it. "Opacity plus non-exit" is a static diagnosis; it names a bad
condition and licenses complaint. The literacy frame supplies a *history of a gap closing*: an authority
outran the competence of the governed, and the competence then diffused — unevenly, through named institutions.
That converts a complaint into a program. It is what generates §4's distributional argument (competence
concentrates where capital already is), §9's diffusion question, and §10's closing question about whether
algorithmacy can be democratized. Without the frame, none of those exist. **This answer is available in the
chapter's own material and needs two sentences at line 91 to be stated.**

*Caveat the chapter must accept to use it:* the REVIEW's finding 5 is correct — §9 promises the diffusion
question and never names the mechanism. If §2 claims the frame buys a diffusion mechanism, §9 has to deliver
one (mass schooling, cheap print, vernacularization, compulsory education). Otherwise the frame buys a promise.

**(b) It defeats the exceptionalism objection.** "Opacity plus non-exit" invites the response that this is
simply the latest instance of information asymmetry plus market power — a problem economics and law already
have names for. The historical frame answers by showing that the *previous* instances of the same shape were
also describable in the flat vocabulary of their day and were nonetheless resolved only by the diffusion of a
competence, not by better disclosure. That is an argument about *what kind of remedy works*, which is exactly
the chapter's §7 claim.

**(c) It licenses the analogy that carries the definition.** Coordinative sovereignty is defined by comparison
(oracy made a member of an oral order capable of holding it to account; literacy made a citizen capable of
auditing a literate state; algorithmacy makes a coordinated actor capable of standing). Lines 587–592 depend on
the frame having been laid. Cut §2 and §6's closing paragraph loses its ground.

**(d) Weakest: rhetorical accessibility for a management readership.** True but not worth 1,186 words.

**Methodological cover for reasoning by media-historical analogy.** The honest framing is that the chapter
offers a *structural* comparison of coordination regimes, not a causal claim that history repeats. That framing
is defensible; "the transition is analogous therefore the outcome will be analogous" is not, and lines 826–833
currently flirt with the latter — the closing question ("whether algorithmacy can be democratized as literacy
was") reads as a prediction dressed as a question.

Three canonical sources supply the discipline, all with **metadata VERIFIED via Crossref this session** and
**content not read this session** (cite at thesis level or read first):

- **Marvin, C. (1988/1990). *When Old Technologies Were New: Thinking About Electric Communication in the Late
  Nineteenth Century*. Oxford University Press. DOI 10.1093/oso/9780195063417.001.0001.** Crossref dates the
  OUP record 1990-10-25; the first edition is 1988. The classic demonstration that every medium was once new
  and that the rhetoric surrounding it recurs — which cuts both ways for the chapter. It licenses the
  comparison and warns against the novelty claim.
- **Gitelman, L. (2006). *Always Already New: Media, History, and the Data of Culture*. MIT Press. DOI
  10.7551/mitpress/1208.001.0001.** VERIFIED. The standing methodological caution against treating media
  transitions as clean epochal breaks.
- **Bolter, J. D., & Grusin, R. (1999). *Remediation: Understanding New Media*. MIT Press.** **DOI UNVERIFIED**
  — Crossref returns only *review* records for this book (e.g. DOI 10.1177/135485650000600209, DOI
  10.1108/ccij.1999.4.4.208.1). **Do not cite either of those DOIs for the book.** Their thesis, that new media
  refashion rather than replace old ones, is the direct challenge to the chapter's "nested but distinct"
  ladder, and it is the argument a media referee will make against §2.3.

**What to do with them.** Cite Gitelman or Marvin in one clause conceding that media-historical periodizations
are suspect, then state the chapter's narrower ground: it is not claiming an epochal break but naming a
*structural* property of a coordination — a third party that reads both sides and commits outcomes — and
tracking which competences can govern a coordination with that property. That claim does not need history to
repeat. It needs only that the property be real and that reading not reach it, and §3.2 argues both.

**Recommendation.** Answer (a) explicitly in two sentences at the head of §2 and cut lines 86–95 and 152–154
(the ~180 words the REVIEW already identified as meta-commentary). The frame then buys something nameable and
costs 180 fewer words.

### 1.6 Other referee threats in §1–§2, ranked

**T1. The §1 opening claim is supported by two sources that do not support it.** Line 23–25: "Together these
instruments give European actors a degree of control over data, infrastructure, and code that no other
jurisdiction matches (Bradford, 2020; Floridi, 2020; Fratini et al., 2024)."

- *Bradford 2020* (**The Brussels Effect**) argues that EU rules propagate globally through market mechanisms —
  unilateral regulatory globalization. It is not a claim that EU actors have control over their own data,
  infrastructure, and code. Wrong instrument for this proposition.

### Useful verbatim material found during verification

**Floridi's definition** (p. 370–371 of the PDF, read this session):

> "these are all episodes in the fight for digital sovereignty, that is, for the control of data, software (e.g.
> AI), standards and protocols (e.g. 5G, domain names), processes (e.g. cloud computing), hardware (e.g. mobile
> phones), services (e.g. social media, e-commerce), and infrastructures (e.g. cables, satellites, smart
> cities), in short, for the control of the digital."

This is a better anchor for §2.3's "control over infrastructures, data, and technologies" than the chapter's
current paraphrase, and it makes the chapter's point *for* it: every item on Floridi's list is a thing to be
controlled. None of them is a *relation*. That is exactly the residue coordinative sovereignty claims.

**Two further things in Floridi 2020 the chapter should know:**

1. Floridi already scales sovereignty down to the individual: "the ultimate form of control is individual
   sovereignty, understood as self-ownership, especially over one's own body, choices, and data," and later
   "popular sovereignty (understood as the pooling of individuals' self-sovereignties)." The chapter's move down
   to the coordinated actor is therefore *less* of a stretch than §1 concedes — the field's most-cited short
   piece already licenses individual-level sovereignty claims.
2. Floridi quotes the US House Judiciary Subcommittee chair on the platform CEOs: "Their ability to dictate
   terms, call the shots, upend entire sectors, and inspire fear represent the powers of a **private
   government**." That is a direct bridge to Anderson (2017) and to §6's non-domination strand, sitting inside a
   source the chapter already cites.

**Fratini et al. 2024, verbatim abstract** (OpenAlex, reconstructed from the inverted index):

> "…we systematically analyse a corpus of 271 peer-reviewed articles to identify descriptive features (how
> digital sovereignty is pursued) and value features (why digital sovereignty is pursued), which we use to
> produce four models: the rights-based model, market-oriented model, centralisation model, and state-based
> model. … We find that none of the models fully combine comprehensive regulation of digital technologies with a
> sufficient degree of responsiveness to fast-paced technological innovation and social and economic shifts."

**Braun & Hummel 2025, verbatim abstract** (OpenAlex):

> "…we argue that references to digital sovereignty have largely operated with an implausibly one-dimensional,
> overly simplified notion of sovereignty in general and its application to the digital in particular. … we
> distinguish three aspects of the concept of sovereignty: (1) sovereignty as absolute power, (2) sovereignty as
> embodied power, and (3) sovereignty as institutional power. We argue that, at least in the European debate on
> digital sovereignty, two of these aspects pertaining to the intricate relation between sovereign and
> addressee(s) of claims to sovereignty are consistently overlooked."

**This is the nearest rival to the chapter's §1 conceptual move and it is currently cited for something else.**
Braun & Hummel say the debate uses a one-dimensional sovereignty concept and propose a three-aspect enrichment
attentive to "the intricate relation between sovereign and addressee(s)." The chapter says the debate uses a
control-based concept and proposes a relational alternative attentive to the standing of the coordinated.
Overlapping projects. The chapter's differentia is the *diagnostic* (necessary vs contingent mediators) and the
*competence* (algorithmacy), neither of which Braun & Hummel have. Say so explicitly in §2.3 or §6.

**Pohle & Thiel 2020, verbatim abstract** (OpenAlex): "…it then goes on to show how, today, the concept is
understood more as a discursive practice in politics and policy than as a legal or organisational concept."

**Couture & Toupin 2019, verbatim abstract** (OpenAlex): "This notion is increasingly used to describe various
forms of independence, control, and autonomy over digital infrastructures, technologies, and data. … we show
that while the notion is generally used to assert some form of collective control on digital content and/or
infrastructures, the precise interpretations, subjects, meanings, and definitions of sovereignty can
significantly differ." Supports both the §2.3 definition and the §6 grassroots point.

**3.1.1 THE HEADLINE: the Platform-to-Business Regulation is proposed for repeal.**

**COM(2025) 837 final**, 19 November 2025, CELEX **52025PC0837**, procedure **2025/0360(COD)** — the data half
of the Digital Omnibus — would **repeal Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 (P2B) outright**, alongside the Free Flow of
Non-Personal Data Regulation, the Data Governance Act, and the Open Data Directive. VERIFIED at
`https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex:52025PC0837`. The Commission's stated reason is that

**Status as of 1 August 2026: "Awaiting committee decision."** ITRE/LIBE jointly responsible (rapporteurs Aura
Salla, EPP; Marina Kaljurand, S&D); draft report PE786.818 of 22 June 2026; amendments tabled in committee

`https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/procedure-file?reference=2025/0360(COD)`.

**Why this is the chapter's best new exhibit.** P2B Article 5 is the *only horizontal* ranking-transparency

transparency contracts to the gatekeeper perimeter, and every non-designated marketplace, app store, booking
platform, and labour platform loses the one duty to explain how it ranks. Outside that perimeter the
coordination layer is not merely un-legislated — it is being **de-regulated**.

And the omission is itself evidence: the European Parliament's own IMCO study on the Omnibus **never uses the
word "ranking."** The repeal is discussed purely as overlap-elimination. Citable:

## (4) FRESH GROUNDING FOR THE TWO WEAK CLAIMS

### 4.1 Line 48 — the stronger normative claim (AGENDA item 6, REVIEW finding 7)

**The claim to be grounded.** Not merely that "where a mediator cannot be left, the sovereignty of independence
is empty," but that **demanding independence leaves an actor *less* sovereign** — because pressing for a bypass
that cannot exist spends finite leverage on nothing, and because the ideal of independence is the wrong measure
of the freedom actually at stake.

There are three separable arguments, and the literature supports all three at different strengths.

**(A) The exit-only conception of freedom is itself a source of domination. — STRONGEST SUPPORT.**

**Anderson, E. (2017). *Private Government: How Employers Rule Our Lives (and Why We Don't Talk about It)*.
Princeton University Press. DOI 10.1515/9781400887781 (De Gruyter e-edition; JSTOR edition DOI
10.2307/j.ctvc775n0). ISBN 9780691176512.** Metadata **VERIFIED** via Crossref and the Princeton catalogue page
(`https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691176512/private-government`).

Publisher's statement of the thesis, read this session: workplaces are "private governments with sweeping
authoritarian power over our lives, on duty and off"; "we still talk as if free markets make workers free"; the
early market advocates' dream of worker freedom "was shattered by the Industrial Revolution, but the myth
endures"; the book opens "space for discovering how workers can enjoy real freedom."

**Why this is the right source.** Anderson's whole argument is that treating the employment relation as governed
by *exit* — the freedom to quit, the market as the guarantor of liberty — is what allows employers to exercise
private government undetected. The exit conception does not merely fail to help; it **conceals the domination
and blocks the remedy**, which is voice. That is the chapter's stronger claim, made in a canonical text, about a
structurally identical case (an actor formally free to leave, substantively unable to, whose freedom talk is
organized around a leaving that does not happen). **Full text not read this session — the Tanner Lectures PDF
and the Princeton excerpt both refused automated fetch. Cite at thesis level, or read chapter 2 before quoting.**

**(B) Republican freedom is not achieved by withdrawal or self-sufficiency.**

- **Pettit, P. (1996). Freedom as antipower. *Ethics, 106*(3), 576–604. DOI 10.1086/233648.** **VERIFIED** via
  Crossref. Abstract elided by the publisher; **full text not read this session.**
- **Pettit, P. (2012). *On the People's Terms: A Republican Theory and Model of Democracy*. Cambridge University
  Press. DOI 10.1017/CBO9781139017428.** **VERIFIED** via Crossref.
- **SEP, "Republicanism" (Frank Lovett; last substantive revision 29 June 2022),
  `https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/republicanism/`.** Fetched this session. Section 1.2 ("Liberty as
  Non-Domination") makes the structural point through the slave case: freedom depends on the structure of the
  relationship, not on the subordinate's coping strategies. Section 4.2 discusses contestatory democracy.
  **CAUTION: the entry does *not* contain an explicit passage rejecting withdrawal/self-sufficiency as a route
  to freedom, and it does not discuss exit vs voice.** I looked for one and it is not there. Do not attribute
  that argument to the SEP entry, and do not attribute it to Pettit without reading *Republicanism* (1997)
  ch. 3 first. The republican point that a slave who flees to the woods is not thereby *free* (only *unexposed*)
  is standard in the literature but I did not verify a specific quotable formulation of it.

**(B-bis) Berlin's "retreat into the inner citadel" — the sharpest available statement of the chapter's point,
and it is fully verified.**

Fetched this session from the Stanford Encyclopedia entry "Positive and Negative Liberty" (Ian Carter and
Ilkin Huseynli; **last substantive revision 28 June 2026**;
`https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/liberty-positive-negative/`), §2 "The Paradox of Positive Liberty," quoting
Berlin:

> "a 'retreat into an inner citadel' — a soul or a purely noumenal self — in which the individual is immune to
> any outside forces."

and Berlin's own analogy, quoted in the same section:

> "if I have a wounded leg 'there are two methods of freeing myself from pain. One is to heal the wound. But if
> the cure is too difficult or uncertain, there is another method. I can get rid of the wound by cutting off my
> leg.'"

The SEP's gloss: such withdrawal "masks oppression rather than achieving genuine freedom."

**This is the chapter's argument in a canonical form.** Demanding independence from a mediator that cannot be
left is cutting off the leg: it removes the actor from the field in which its autonomy would be exercised and
calls the removal freedom. The chapter already reaches for this at line 450–451 ("a withdrawal from the field
in which autonomy would be exercised") without any source. Berlin supplies one.

**Citation, VERIFIED via Crossref:** Berlin, I. (2002). Two concepts of liberty. In H. Hardy (Ed.), *Liberty*
(pp. 166–217). Oxford University Press. DOI **10.1093/019924989x.003.0004**. (Container volume *Liberty*,
ed. Henry Hardy, OUP 2002, DOI 10.1093/019924989x.001.0001; the essay is Berlin's 1958 inaugural lecture,
collected in *Four Essays on Liberty*, 1969.) Also available as a chapter in *The Liberty Reader*, pp. 33–57,
DOI 10.4324/9781315091822-3.

And from §4 of the same SEP entry, "Republican Liberty: A Third Way?", on why republican freedom is
institutional rather than achieved by escape:

> "freedom is not merely the enjoyment of a sphere of non-interference but the enjoyment of certain conditions
> in which such non-interference is guaranteed."

**(C) Relational autonomy: pursuing autonomy through separation destroys its own conditions.**

- **Nedelsky, J. (2011). *Law's Relations: A Relational Theory of Self, Autonomy, and Law*. Oxford University
  Press. DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195147964.001.0001.** **VERIFIED** via Crossref (Crossref dates the online
  edition 2012-01-01; the print is 2011 — the chapter's 2011 is correct). The core chapter, "Reconceiving
  Autonomy," is pp. 118–157 (DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195147964.003.0004). Nedelsky's thesis — that autonomy
  is *constituted by* relationships rather than protected by boundaries against them — entails that an actor who
  pursues autonomy as separation from a constitutive relationship undermines the very capacity it seeks. **Full
  text not read this session; cite at thesis level.**
- **Mackenzie, C., & Stoljar, N. (Eds.). (2000). *Relational Autonomy: Feminist Perspectives on Autonomy,
  Agency, and the Social Self*. Oxford University Press. DOI 10.1093/oso/9780195123333.001.0001.** **VERIFIED**
  via Crossref. The editors' Introduction is pp. 3–31 (DOI 10.1093/oso/9780195123333.003.0001) — the standard
  citation for the critique of the "self-sufficient, independent" autonomy ideal. Already in the chapter's
  reference list; the §1 argument can lean on it directly.
- **Mackenzie, C. (2014). Three dimensions of autonomy: A relational analysis. In *Autonomy, Oppression, and
  Gender* (pp. 15–41). Oxford University Press. DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199969104.003.0002.** **VERIFIED**
  via Crossref. Useful because it separates self-determination (which depends on the *options actually
  available*) from self-governance and self-authorization. On that analysis, an actor whose available options do
  not include exit does not gain self-determination by demanding exit; it loses the self-authorization it could
  have gained by contesting the terms. This is the cleanest analytic route to "less sovereign." **Content read
  at metadata/structure level only; not quoted.**

**(D) Hirschman's own atrophy argument.** *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970) argues that the availability of exit
can atrophy the development of the art of voice. The chapter already uses the lazy-monopoly half of this and
could use the atrophy half for the stronger claim: an actor that organizes its whole strategy around leaving
never builds the institutional voice that is its only real power. **I could not verify a verbatim formulation
this session** (archive.org's search-inside endpoints timed out). **Do not put quotation marks around any
Hirschman phrasing without checking the printed page.**

**Recommended formulation for line 48**, in descending order of what the sources actually license:

1. *Safe and fully sourced:* demanding independence from a mediator that cannot be left is not a bid for
   freedom but a withdrawal from the field in which freedom would be exercised — Berlin's retreat into the
   inner citadel, curing the wound by cutting off the leg (Berlin, 2002/1958). Freedom in that position is not
   the absence of the relationship but the conditions under which the relationship is non-arbitrary
   (Pettit, 1997; SEP "Positive and Negative Liberty," §4).
2. *Stronger, supported at thesis level:* the ideal of independence, applied to a constitutive relationship,
   is self-defeating, because the relationship is a condition of the autonomy in question rather than a
   limit on it (Nedelsky 2011; Mackenzie & Stoljar 2000; Mackenzie 2014).
3. *Strongest, and the one the project wants — needs the reading done first:* demanding independence leaves the
   actor less sovereign, because the demand is what keeps the relationship ungoverned. An actor that spends its
   leverage on an exit that cannot be had never builds the voice that is its only real power, and the exit
   framing simultaneously conceals that the relationship is a government at all (Anderson 2017; Hirschman 1970
   on the atrophy of voice — **verify both against the printed text before writing this version**).

### 4.2 Line 139 — "The one completed change in kind is the first, from speech to writing"

**The structural problem, statable without further research.** The sentence concedes that only the oral→literate
