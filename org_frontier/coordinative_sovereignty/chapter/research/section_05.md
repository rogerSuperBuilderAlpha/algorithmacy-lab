## PART 1 — THE CENTRAL THREAT: answering Reviewer 2

### 1.0 The charge, restated precisely

Reviewer 2's Objection 5.1 and the Verdict make one claim in two forms:

- **Genealogical form.** The chapter's own citation trail (Hahl et al. 2016 on brokerage; Eisenmann et al. 2006
  on multihoming) shows the bypass test descends from brokerage/substitutability research.
- **Sufficiency form.** The chapter concedes that "the runnable counterfactual … is enough," which means the
  formal apparatus does no work inside these pages, so the four-way typology "maps one-to-one onto standard
  gradations of substitutability."

The demanded remedy is one borderline case where the naive counterfactual and the graded measure disagree.

**Important reframing before anything else.** The reviewer's demand is for a *Φ-versus-naive* disagreement.
That case has to come from the companion paper and cannot be manufactured here. But the charge as *stated* is
broader and weaker: that the bypass test "reduces to … substitutability relabeled." That weaker charge can be
defeated in this chapter, on published evidence, without invoking Φ at all — by showing that the bypass test
and the substitutability test **give different answers on real cases** (§1.5 below). Defeating the weaker
charge is worth doing, because the weaker charge is the one that would sink the chapter; the stronger demand
is a request for a contribution the chapter never claimed to make.

### 1.1 THE HEADLINE FINDING: node counterfactuals vs. edge counterfactuals

Researching the three neighbours turned up one structural fact that organizes everything else.

**All three neighbours run a counterfactual on a NODE. The chapter runs a counterfactual on an EDGE.**

| Test | Operation performed on the model | Question asked |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage / structural holes | delete or substitute the broker node | can someone else occupy this position? does the broker earn a return? |
| Ryall & Sorenson (2007) biform game | delete the broker from the coalition | does value survive the broker's removal? |
| Make-or-buy / TCE | move the function inside one party's boundary | which governance form economizes on transaction costs? |
| Essential facilities (Bronner) | build a second facility | is duplication economically viable for the excluded rival? |
| **The chapter's bypass test** | **add the forbidden A–B tie, keep the mediator in place** | **is there anything left for the mediator to do once the parties can reach each other?** |

The neighbours hold the network of who-may-deal-with-whom **fixed** and vary the identity or ownership of the
intermediary. The chapter holds the intermediary **fixed** and varies the network. These are not the same
operation and they do not always agree.

The franchised-dealer example in the chapter is exactly a case where they disagree, and the chapter should say
so explicitly rather than leaving it implicit:

- **Node counterfactual:** delete the dealer, and under state franchise law nobody else can deliver a new
  vehicle in that territory. The dealer is non-substitutable. Bronner-style indispensability: satisfied.
  Ryall–Sorenson essentiality: satisfied. Make-or-buy: the manufacturer's choice set does not contain "make."
- **Edge counterfactual:** restore the manufacturer-to-buyer tie, and the dealer's position evaporates.

So the three neighbours would classify the dealer as *necessary*; the chapter classifies it as *contingent*.
That is a demonstrated divergence between the chapter's test and the substitutability tests it is accused of
relabeling. **This is the single most useful thing in this research file.** It converts Reviewer 2's charge
from "identical" to "differently specified," and it costs the chapter nothing to say.

The reason the neighbours cannot ask the edge question is not oversight. Each takes the institutional
constraint as a **parameter** because each is answering a question posed inside that constraint: Burt asks what
a position yields *given* the network; Williamson asks how to govern a transaction *given* the legal
environment; Bronner asks whether a court should compel access *given* that the facility exists. The chapter
asks which constraints are load-bearing — a question none of the three is built to pose.

### 1.2 NEIGHBOUR ONE — Burt's structural holes and the brokerage tradition

**What the tradition measures.** Burt's apparatus is about what the *broker* gains. *Structural Holes* (1992)
introduces network constraint and structural autonomy: measures of how disconnected ego's contacts already are.
Burt (2004) extends this to idea generation. Both are **descriptive of the current network**, not counterfactual
about a restorable tie. Obstfeld (2005) adds the opposite orientation — *tertius iungens*, the broker who joins
the parties — but again as a behavioural orientation of the broker, not a test of whether the parties could join
themselves.

**Does brokerage theory have a test for whether the brokered parties could close the gap themselves?**
Yes — but it is a *behavioural/perceptual* test, not a structural one, and this is where the chapter's real
boundary lies.

**Hahl, Kacperczyk & Davis (2016) — what they actually claim.** VERIFIED. The chapter's characterization is a
fair paraphrase of the paper's opening sentence but misattributes the credit, and the paper's *own* contribution
is more useful to the chapter than the sentence currently cited.

Abstract, verbatim:

> "Although brokers who span structural holes have been shown to occupy a valuable position in organizations,
> emerging research suggests that the returns to these brokers can vary depending on whether alters can credibly
> threaten to disintermediate the broker and close the structural hole. Yet, the factors that shape the
> likelihood of disintermediation have not been extensively explored. In this article, we build from the premise
> that an alter's knowledge about the structural hole is a necessary condition for disintermediation. Without
> this knowledge, the alter will not know with whom to disintermediate. … we argue that individuals are most
> likely to be in a structural hole under the condition of knowledge asymmetry — that is, when brokers know
> about the structural hole, but alters do not — which reduces the likelihood of disintermediation by alters and
> increases the benefits for brokers."

Body text, verbatim:

> "A number of studies have consistently indicated that individuals are likely to occupy and benefit from
> brokerage positions when the threat of disintermediation, or the prospect that two alters may threaten to 'cut
> out the middleman,' is low (Bidwell and Fernandez-Mateo, 2010; Ryall and Sorenson, 2007)."

> "In our study, we explain an important condition for broker disintermediation: the alters' perception of the
> structural hole. Although this mechanism by itself is not sufficient to explain when disintermediation will
> happen, it is nonetheless a necessary condition for an attempt to disintermediate the broker."

**Three consequences for the chapter.**

1. The credible-threat proposition is **inherited**, not established, by Hahl et al. If the chapter wants the
   citation to carry weight it should cite Ryall & Sorenson (2007) and/or Bidwell & Fernandez-Mateo (2010)
   alongside, or reattribute.
2. The word the brokerage literature uses is **"disintermediation."** The chapter should stop pretending the
   vocabulary is new. Owning the word strengthens the position: the chapter is specifying a test for a
   phenomenon that literature names but does not operationalize structurally.
3. **The boundary.** Hahl et al. explain when disintermediation will be *attempted* — it turns on whether the
   alters know the hole exists. The chapter's test asks whether disintermediation would *succeed* — whether the
   mediator still binds once the tie is open. These come apart cleanly. A mediator whose alters are ignorant is
   never disintermediated and looks necessary in the data, while being structurally contingent. Hahl et al. is
   therefore not a rival to the chapter's test; it is an account of why the test's verdict and observed
   behaviour diverge. **The chapter should use it that way.**

**Ryall & Sorenson (2007) is the sharper formal neighbour and the chapter does not cite it.** This is the most
serious uncited-neighbour risk in §5, sharper than Burt. VERIFIED abstract:

> "The broker profits by intermediating between two (or more) parties. Using a biform game, we examine whether
> such a position can confer a competitive advantage, as well as whether any such advantage could persist if
> actors formed relations strategically. Our analysis reveals that, if one considers exogenous the relations
> between actors, brokers can enjoy an advantage but only if (1) they do not face substitutes either for the
> connections they offer or the value they can create, (2) they intermediate more than two parties, and (3)
> interdependence does not lock them into a particular pattern of exchange. If, on the other hand, one allows
> actors to form relations on the basis of their expectations of the future value of those relations, then
> profitable positions of intermediation only arise under strict assumptions of unilateral action."

Read carefully, this **helps** the chapter more than it threatens it. Ryall & Sorenson explicitly separate the
case where ties are *exogenous* from the case where actors may *form* ties, and show that broker advantage
mostly vanishes in the second. That is the chapter's contingent/necessary distinction in cooperative-game form —
which proves the distinction is real and formally respectable, and simultaneously proves the chapter is not the
first to draw it. The honest move is to cite Ryall & Sorenson as the prior formalization at the level of *value
appropriation* and to locate the chapter's addition at the level of *causal constitution and remedy*: R&S ask
whether the broker captures value; the chapter asks whether the coordination can exist without it and what the
dependent party should therefore do.

### 1.3 NEIGHBOUR TWO — Williamson's make-or-buy / transaction cost economics

**Is the chapter's test a relabeling of make-or-buy? No — and the reason is precise, not rhetorical.**

Four differences, in order of strength.

**(a) The counterfactual moves a different thing.** Make-or-buy moves the *function* across a firm boundary:
should party A perform this activity itself or procure it? The bypass test moves *a prohibition on contact*
between two parties who are each other's counterparty. Make-or-buy never asks whether the activity would still
exist once the parties can reach each other; it takes the activity as given and asks who should own it.

**(b) The institutional constraint is a parameter in TCE and the variable in the bypass test.** TCE optimizes
governance *within* a legal environment. Where a law forbids direct dealing, TCE's answer is that "make" is
outside the choice set, and it says nothing further. The bypass test's entire content is to ask whether that
prohibition is what holds the mediator's position. TCE has no category for a mediator whose position is a rent
from an institutional restriction rather than from economizing — that phenomenon belongs to the regulation and
rent-seeking literatures, not to Williamson.

**(c) TCE is dyadic and comparative-cost; the bypass test is triadic and structural.** Williamson's unit of
analysis is the transaction between two parties, and the criterion is comparative transaction cost given asset
specificity, uncertainty, and frequency — an efficiency criterion whose answer depends on magnitudes. The bypass
test's answer does not depend on whether bypass would be *cheaper*; it depends on whether the mediator still
binds. A mediator can be contingent and yet worth keeping on cost grounds. The chapter should say this out loud,
because it is the cleanest statement of what the test is *not*: **the bypass test is not a recommendation to
bypass.** It says what kind of power the actor holds, not what the actor should buy.

**(d) The remedy differs.** Make-or-buy is advice to a firm that has a boundary to move. The dependent actors
the chapter is about — the marketplace seller, the driver, the independent hotel — cannot integrate the
mediator. Their choice is not make-or-buy; it is press-for-a-rule-change or press-for-standing.

**Where the chapter is genuinely exposed.** Williamson's *fundamental transformation* — an ex ante competitive
supply relation becoming an ex post bilateral monopoly through relationship-specific investment — is a
mechanism by which a bypassable position becomes unbypassable. That is very close to the chapter's contingent
mediator held by lock-in, and the chapter should acknowledge it rather than be caught by it. Recommended
handling: TCE explains *how* a mediator acquires a grip (specific assets, switching costs); the diagnostic asks
*whether the grip survives the tie* and *what the dependent party can do about it*. Complementary, not rival.

**Citations.** Williamson, O. E. (1975). *Markets and hierarchies: Analysis and antitrust implications*. Free
Press. (Already in the chapter's reference list; not independently re-verified here — UNVERIFIED as to
publisher imprint details, though uncontroversial.)

### 1.4 NEIGHBOUR THREE — essential facilities, indispensability, and (the real threat) ancillary restraints

This section contains the most uncomfortable finding in the file. **Competition law already runs the chapter's
counterfactual.** But it runs it on a different object, from a different vantage, to a different end — and, as
of 2025, EU law is actively moving *away* from making it the pivot for access remedies.

#### (a) Bronner indispensability is NOT the same test

*Oscar Bronner GmbH & Co. KG v Mediaprint*, Case C-7/97, judgment of 26 November 1998, ECLI:EU:C:1998:569.
VERIFIED via `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A61997CJ0007`.

Paragraph 41, verbatim:

> "it would still be necessary … not only that the refusal of the service comprised in home delivery be likely
> to eliminate all competition in the daily newspaper market on the part of the person requesting the service
> and that such refusal be incapable of being objectively justified, but also that the service in itself be
> indispensable to carrying on that person's business, inasmuch as there is no actual or potential substitute in
> existence for that home-delivery scheme."

Paragraph 46, verbatim:

> "it would be necessary at the very least to establish … that it is not economically viable to create a second
> home-delivery scheme for the distribution of daily newspapers with a circulation comparable to that of the
> daily newspapers distributed by the existing scheme."

Three differences from the chapter's test, all clean:

1. **Duplication, not disintermediation.** Bronner asks whether a *second facility* can be built. The chapter
   asks whether the *coordination survives without any facility*. Node vs. edge again.
2. **The claimant is an excluded rival, not an enrolled dependent.** The party invoking Bronner is a competitor
   locked *out*; the party running the chapter's test is a complementor locked *in*. Their remedies differ:
   compelled access vs. exit-or-voice.
3. **Bronner's standard is economic viability** (¶46) — an outcome/magnitude standard. The chapter's is
   structural. This is where the chapter's "rule-based rather than outcome-based" differentiator actually holds.

US law is even further away. *Verizon Communications Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP*, 540 U.S. 398
(2004), decided 13 January 2004. VERIFIED via `https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-682.ZO.html`:

> "We have never recognized such a doctrine, see *Aspen Skiing Co.*, 472 U.S., at 611, n. 44; *AT&T Corp.* v.
> *Iowa Utilities Bd.*, 525 U.S., at 428 (opinion of Breyer, J.), and we find no need either to recognize it or
> to repudiate it here."

> "The indispensable requirement for invoking the doctrine is the unavailability of access to the 'essential
> facilities'; where access exists, the doctrine serves no purpose."

#### (b) BUT: the ancillary-restraints doctrine IS structurally the same counterfactual

This is the finding to be honest about. EU competition law's ancillary-restraints test asks exactly the
chapter's question — remove the restriction, does the main operation survive? — and applies it to contract
clauses.

Commission Notice — Guidelines on the application of Article 81(3) of the Treaty, OJ C 101, 27.4.2004, pp.
97–118. VERIFIED via `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52004XC0427(07)`.

Paragraph 29, verbatim:

> "In Community competition law the concept of ancillary restraints covers any alleged restriction of
> competition which is directly related and necessary to the implementation of a main non-restrictive
> transaction and proportionate to it."

Paragraph 31, verbatim:

> "If on the basis of objective factors it can be concluded that without the restriction the main non-restrictive
> transaction would be difficult or impossible to implement, the restriction may be regarded as objectively
> necessary for its implementation and proportionate to it."

That is the bypass test, written in 2004, for clauses.

**And a court has now run it on the chapter's own worked example.** In *Booking.com*, Case C-264/23 (below), the
Court held that both wide and narrow parity clauses fail objective necessity — i.e. the gate is contingent. The
chapter's §5.4 verdict on the OTA parity clause and the CJEU's holding are the same verdict reached by the same
counterfactual.

**How to handle this — recommendation.** Do not hide it; claim it. The strongest available framing:

> The counterfactual is not new. EU competition law has applied it to contract clauses since the ancillary-
> restraints doctrine, and the Court of Justice applied it to hotel-platform parity clauses in 2024. What is new
> here is the object and the addressee: the test is run on a mediator's *functions* rather than on a clause, by
> the *dependent party* rather than by a tribunal, and its output is a strategy — exit or voice — rather than a
> finding of liability.

That framing survives contact with a hostile competition lawyer. The current framing does not.

#### (c) The 2025 development that separates the two tests for good

*Alphabet Inc., Google LLC, Google Italy Srl v Autorità Garante della Concorrenza e del Mercato*, Case
C-233/23, Grand Chamber, judgment of 25 February 2025, ECLI:EU:C:2025:110, on a reference from the Consiglio di
Stato. VERIFIED via `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A62023CJ0233`.

Paragraph 44, verbatim:

> "where a dominant undertaking has developed infrastructure not solely for the needs of its own business but
> with a view to enabling third-party undertakings to use that infrastructure, the condition laid down by the
> Court of Justice in paragraph 41 of the judgment of 26 November 1998, Bronner … relating to whether that
> infrastructure is indispensable … does not apply."

Operative part (as fetched):

> refusal to ensure interoperability "is capable of constituting an abuse of a dominant position even though that
> platform is not indispensable for the commercial operation of that app on a downstream market" where "that
> platform has not been developed by the undertaking in a dominant position solely for the needs of its own
> business."

**Why this matters for §5, in two directions.**

- *It helps.* EU law has just **decoupled** the access remedy from indispensability for platforms opened to
  third parties. So "indispensability" and "what remedy is available" no longer track each other in law. The
  chapter's test cannot be indispensability relabeled, because indispensability no longer does the job the
  chapter's test does.
- *It complicates.* The chapter's clean mapping — exit-restoring instruments for contingent functions,
  voice-building institutions for necessary ones — assumed that mandated access is reserved for facilities that
  cannot be bypassed. After Android Auto, compelled interoperability is available against platforms that are
  *not* indispensable. §7's line that mandated interoperability against a necessary mediator is "a voice move
  wearing an exit instrument's clothes" is still right, but the chapter should add that the reverse now also
  happens: an access remedy can be imposed on a bypassable platform, which makes the instrument-to-verdict
  mapping many-to-many rather than one-to-one. Reviewer 2's Objection 5.3 (four categories acting as two) is
  partly answered here — the instrument space is richer than the chapter lets on.

#### (d) The DMA

Regulation (EU) 2022/1925 (Digital Markets Act), OJ L 265, 12.10.2022, pp. 1–66. Full title VERIFIED via
`https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/1925/oj/eng`.

Article 5(3), verbatim (text fetched from `https://www.eu-digital-markets-act.com/Digital_Markets_Act_Article_5.html`;
independently corroborated by the OTA-sourcing pass against the OJ PDF — VERIFIED):

> "The gatekeeper shall not prevent business users from offering the same products or services to end users
> through third-party online intermediation services or through their own direct online sales channel at prices
> or conditions that are different from those offered through the online intermediation services of the
> gatekeeper."

Article 6(7), verbatim (same mirror source — treat as VERIFIED-VIA-MIRROR; re-check against the OJ before
publication if quoted):

> "The gatekeeper shall allow providers of services and providers of hardware, free of charge, effective
> interoperability with, and access for the purposes of interoperability to, the same hardware and software
> features accessed or controlled via the operating system or virtual assistant listed in the designation
> decision pursuant to Article 3(9) as are available to services or hardware provided by the gatekeeper."

**Article 6(7) is the WRONG provision for the OTA case.** It governs interoperability with an operating system
or virtual assistant. Booking was designated for an *online intermediation service*, not an OS. Do not cite
6(7) in the hotel paragraph. **Article 5(3) is the correct provision** — it is a direct statutory abolition of
the parity gate.

### 1.5 THE DISAGREEMENT CASES — what Reviewer 2 asked for, in the form the chapter can actually supply

Reviewer 2 asked for a case where "the naive counterfactual and the graded measure disagree." The chapter cannot
supply that without importing Φ from the companion paper. It **can** supply the case that answers the charge as
stated: cases where the *substitutability/observed-bypass* answer and the *structural bypass* answer disagree.
Three are available, all sourced.

**Case A — the OTA data, and this is the strong one.** The naive substitutability test, applied to hotels and
online travel agencies, says: hotels can sell direct, the direct channel exists, the parity clause that blocked
price competition has now been struck down in Germany (2021) and denied ancillary-restraint status by the CJEU
(2024), therefore the agency is substitutable and its position should erode. The diagnostic disagrees: it splits
the agency into a contingent parity gate and a necessary demand-aggregation function, and predicts that opening
the gate changes prices without changing dependence. **The data side with the diagnostic.** Across the decade in
which the parity gate was progressively dismantled, the European OTA share of hotel overnight stays rose from
19.7% (2013) to 29.6% (2023), and direct bookings fell from 57.6% to 50.9% (Schegg, 2024 — see Part 2). A test
that treats the mediator as one substitutable thing gets the direction of travel wrong. A test that splits it by
function gets it right. This is a real, sourced, out-of-sample disagreement and it belongs in the chapter.

**Case B — Hahl et al. (2016): observed bypass is a perception variable, not a structural one.** Where alters do
not know the structural hole exists, no disintermediation is attempted and the mediator looks necessary in any
behavioural data. The structural test returns *contingent*. Quoted above; VERIFIED. This is the case that shows
"can they deal directly?" answered empirically and answered structurally are different questions.

**Case C — Gu & Zhu (2021): integrating work manufactures its own bypassability.** A field experiment on a major
online freelance marketplace found that raising trust — the platform's own integrating work — raises
disintermediation, offsetting the platform's revenue gains. VERIFIED: Gu, G., & Zhu, F. (2021). Trust and
disintermediation: Evidence from an online freelance marketplace. *Management Science, 67*(2), 794–807.
https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2020.3583 (`https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mnsc.2020.3583`). The
implication for §5 is sharp and the chapter does not currently have it: **necessity is not a fixed property.**
A mediator whose integrating work succeeds can convert itself from necessary to contingent, and the platform's
strategic response is to withhold the very integration that would make it necessary. That is a new and defensible
point, and it is the mechanism behind the "opacity is a rent-preserving strategy" claim already in §4.

Sekar, S., & Siddiq, A. (2026). Platform disintermediation: Information effects and pricing remedies.
*Operations Research, 74*(3), 1304–1320. https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.2025.2046 — VERIFIED via
`https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/opre.2025.2046`. Abstract finding relevant here: platforms benefit from
disclosing **partial** rather than complete information about buyer risk, "as full transparency diminishes the
platform's protective value and intensifies disintermediation incentives." This is a formal model of the same
mechanism and it independently supports the chapter's §3.3/§4 claim that opacity is rent-preserving.

### 1.6 VERDICT ON REVIEWER 2's CHARGE

**The contribution SURVIVES, but it must NARROW. Specifically: it survives at the level of *addressee and
remedy*, and it must give up the implicit claim to have invented the counterfactual.**

Testing each of the three differentiators the brief asked about:

| Claimed differentiator | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Function-level, not firm-level** | **FAILS as a differentiator** | Every neighbour is already sub-firm. TCE's unit of analysis is the transaction. Essential facilities attaches to a facility or input. Brokerage attaches to a position or tie. Nothing here distinguishes the chapter. |
| **Rule-based rather than outcome-based** | **PARTIALLY SURVIVES** | Genuine against brokerage (returns-based), TCE (comparative cost), and Bronner (economic viability of duplication, ¶46). **Fails against the ancillary-restraints doctrine**, which is an objective-necessity counterfactual of exactly the chapter's form (Guidelines ¶31), applied by the CJEU to the chapter's own OTA example in 2024. |
| **Remedy-linked: outputs exit vs. voice** | **SURVIVES, and is the real contribution** | No neighbour outputs a remedy for the *dependent* party. Brokerage tells you what the broker earns. Make-or-buy advises a firm that has a boundary to move. Essential facilities tells a tribunal whether to compel access for an excluded rival. None addresses an actor enrolled inside the coordination who cannot integrate, cannot litigate, and must choose between pressing to open the gate and pressing for standing. |

**Add a fourth differentiator the chapter does not currently claim, and make it the headline: the edge
counterfactual (§1.1).** It is precise, it is checkable, it produces a demonstrable disagreement with all three
neighbours on the chapter's own dealer example, and it explains *why* the neighbours cannot ask the question —
each holds the institutional constraint fixed because each is answering a question posed inside it.

**What is left after the narrowing, stated as the residue:**

> Given a mediator, a dependent party, and a set of rules, the diagnostic asks which of the mediator's functions
> would survive the removal of the prohibition on direct contact, and converts each answer into a strategy for
> the dependent party. Brokerage theory predicts the broker's returns and, in Hahl et al., whether bypass will be
> attempted. Transaction cost economics prescribes a governance form to a party that can move its boundary.
> Essential facilities tells a court when to compel access to an excluded rival. The ancillary-restraints
> doctrine runs the same counterfactual on a clause and returns a finding of legality. None of them returns a
> strategy to the party inside the coordination, and none of them is run by that party.

**Three honesty edits that would defuse the charge at low cost:**

1. Adopt Reviewer 2's own suggested rewrite of the §5.2 opening (name the make-or-buy family inline). It costs
   nothing and removes the appearance of concealment.
2. Add Ryall & Sorenson (2007) and the ancillary-restraints doctrine to the genealogy, then state the residue.
3. Replace "the check exact" with language that does not borrow the companion paper's authority, and put the
   *edge/node* distinction where "exact" used to be. The chapter then has a precise claim it can defend in these
   pages instead of an IOU.

**One thing the chapter should NOT do:** concede that Φ is decorative. The defensible position is that the
counterfactual is the chapter-level instrument and the integrated-information measures are what grade cases the
counterfactual returns as close or many-partied. The chapter can say that without claiming exactness it does not

## PART 4 — A DECISION RULE FOR THE "PARTIAL" VERDICT

The chapter defines a partial mediator (real integrating work alongside a bypassable gate) and never says what
to do about it. The obvious answer — contest the bypassable part, accept the integrating part — has substantial
support, from four directions. This also answers Reviewer 2's Objection 5.3, which complains that "partial"
never drives a different action: **it does, and the action is unbundling.**

### 4.1 The regulatory precedent: unbundling is the standard remedy for a mixed facility

Network-industry regulation faced exactly this problem — a facility that is part natural monopoly and part
contestable service — and its answer was to separate them and regulate each differently: local-loop unbundling
in telecommunications, ownership and functional unbundling in electricity and gas. The structural-separation
debate for platforms is the same instrument aimed at the same problem.
**STATUS: the general point is well known and safe to assert; specific instrument citations (Regulation (EC) No
2887/2000 on unbundled access to the local loop; Directive 2009/72/EC) were NOT fetched in this pass —
UNVERIFIED. Verify before citing.**

The chapter already has one verified instance of the rule in action: the CJEU in C-264/23 separated the parity
clause (struck out of ancillary-restraint protection) from the intermediation service itself (untouched). That
is unbundling by adjudication, on the chapter's own example. **VERIFIED, §2.3 above.**

### 4.2 Modularity supplies the criterion for *where* to cut

Baldwin, C. Y. (2008). Where do transactions come from? Modularity, transactions, and the boundaries of firms.
*Industrial and Corporate Change, 17*(1), 155–195. https://doi.org/10.1093/icc/dtm036 — VERIFIED via
`https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/icc/dtm036` (Crossref records the issue year as 2007 online / 17(1)
Feb 2008 in print; cite 2008). Baldwin's thesis: transaction costs are low at the **thin crossing points** of a
task network, which coincide with module boundaries, so transactions locate at module boundaries rather than in
module interiors.

**The decision rule this yields:** a partial verdict is actionable exactly where the bypassable gate sits at a
thin crossing point — that is, where the gate can be detached without severing the dense interdependencies that
constitute the integrating work. Where the gate is buried inside the integrating module, contesting it means
contesting the integration, and the actor should treat the whole function as necessary and go to voice. That is
a genuine decision that turns on partial-versus-necessary, which is what Reviewer 2 asked for.

### 4.3 The ecosystem/bottleneck literature supplies the strategic version

Jacobides, M. G., Cennamo, C., & Gawer, A. (2018). Towards a theory of ecosystems. *Strategic Management
Journal, 39*(8), 2255–2276. https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.2904 — VERIFIED via
`https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/smj.2904`. Modularity is what allows interdependent organizations to
coordinate without hierarchical fiat; ecosystems are held together by non-generic complementarities. The
generic/non-generic distinction is a near-twin of the chapter's contingent/necessary distinction and should be
acknowledged — **a further uncited neighbour, and a friendly one.**

Hannah, D. P., & Eisenhardt, K. M. (2018). How firms navigate cooperation and competition in nascent
ecosystems. *Strategic Management Journal, 39*(12), 3163–3192. https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.2750 — VERIFIED via
`https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/smj.2750`. Five US residential-solar firms, 2007–2014; identifies a
**bottleneck strategy** in which firms move scope to encompass the component holding back the system and then
blend cooperation and competition with the other component providers. That is the partial-verdict playbook in
empirical form: contest the bottleneck, cooperate elsewhere.

### 4.4 Plural sourcing shows the partial response is a distinct choice, not a midpoint

Parmigiani, A. (2007). Why do firms both make and buy? An investigation of concurrent sourcing. *Strategic
Management Journal, 28*(3), 285–311. https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.580 — VERIFIED via
`https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/smj.580`. Key finding for the chapter: concurrent sourcing "is a
distinctly different choice, rather [than] existing along a make/buy continuum." Applied to §5: the response to
a partial verdict is not a weaker version of exit or a weaker version of voice; it is the simultaneous pursuit
of both, against different functions of the same mediator. **This is the citation that most directly rebuts
Objection 5.3.**

Jacobides, M. G. (2005). Industry change through vertical disintegration: How and why markets emerged in
mortgage banking. *Academy of Management Journal, 48*(3), 465–498. https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2005.17407912 —
VERIFIED via Crossref bibliographic query. Useful for the conditions under which a segment of a chain becomes
separately tradable — i.e. when a partial mediator's gate can actually be detached.

### 4.5 The disintermediation literature supplies the caution

Sarkar, M. B., Butler, B., & Steinfield, C. (1995). Intermediaries and cybermediaries: A continuing role for
mediating players in the electronic marketplace. *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication, 1*(3).
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1083-6101.1995.tb00167.x — **PARTIALLY VERIFIED**: DOI, authors (Mitra Barun Sarkar,
Brian Butler, Charles Steinfield), journal, volume 1, issue 3 confirmed via
`https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/j.1083-6101.1995.tb00167.x`; Crossref records year as 2006 (deposit) and
returns page "0-0", and the title in the Crossref record is truncated. **Cite year 1995 and no page range;
verify the full title against the Wiley page before use.** Substance: the paper argued against confident
predictions of internet disintermediation, on the ground that intermediaries perform functions producers cannot
easily absorb — the original statement of "some mediators survive the direct tie and some do not." Thirty years
of subsequent evidence should make the chapter modest about how easily the contingent part is actually detached.
