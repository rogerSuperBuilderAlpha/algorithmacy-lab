## HEADLINE — what has to change

Ranked by how much damage it does if it goes to a referee unfixed.

1. **§3.3 inverts the two-sided-market result it cites (lines 264–272).** In Armstrong's competitive bottleneck
   the *multi-homing* side is extracted from and the *single-homing* side is subsidised. The chapter says the
   opposite. The term is also Armstrong's (2006), not Armstrong & Wright's. The correct version fits the
   chapter's own App Store and marketplace examples better. §5.3 (lines 445–447) repeats the error. → Part 4b.
2. **Two miscitations at lines 176–177 put Kellogg et al. (2020), Rahman (2021) and Vallas & Schor (2020) on the
   wrong side of the objections they are cited for.** All three argue that algorithmic coordination is *distinct*
   from bureaucratic control and from market/hierarchy hybridity. → T2.
3. **The Selznick reading (lines 164–169) is wrong about formal co-optation**, which "does not envision the
   transfer of actual power." The platform form is not "near its antonym"; it is formal co-optation minus the
   forms of participation. The fix is stronger than the current text. → T3.
4. **Robinson & Meijerink (2026) is a four-author paper.** Jarrahi and Keegan are missing. → Part 2.
5. **Line 347's rent claim has no support in the two sources attached to it**, but real support is available —
   including in Cohen (2017), which the chapter already cites. → Part 4a.
6. **§3.2 omits the EU Platform Work Directive**, the one instrument that regulates algorithmic management as
   such, which the chapter itself leans on in §6 and §8. → T2 item 1.
7. **§3.2's list is missing adaptivity**, the feature that most directly defeats disclosure and that §4 already
   builds a competence dimension on. → T4.

**Top three additions, if only three are made:** Vallas & Schor pp. 281–282 cited *for* the tertius gaudens
point at line 208 (they got there first); Armstrong (2006) replacing the Armstrong & Wright attribution with the
corrected mechanism; O'Reilly, Strauss & Mazzucato (2024) plus Cohen (2017) carrying line 347.

## PART 1 — REVIEWER THREAT-HUNT

### T1. The core move at lines 179–194: logic old, topology old, conjunction new

**Verdict: SURVIVES, BUT NARROWS — and it must narrow explicitly, because two of the three rivals are sources
the chapter already cites for something else.**

The 2025–26 sweep found no organization-theory or platform-studies paper that advances "opacity PLUS
interestedness of the mediator" as its own novelty thesis. So the conjunction is not taken. But the chapter
currently presents the topology point as though network sociology (Simmel, Burt, Obstfeld, J. D. Thompson) were
the only prior claimants, and that is not where the danger is. The danger is in the platform literature itself.

**Rival A — Vallas & Schor (2020), pp. 281–282. The most damaging, because the chapter cites this article two
lines earlier and gets it backwards (see T2).** Verbatim, from the published version
(read at https://www.bc.edu/content/dam/bc1/schools/mcas/sociology/pdf/connected/Vallas%20and%20Schor%20ARS%20copy.pdf,
and cross-checked against the Annual Reviews PDF):

> "In contrast to hierarchies (which centralize power), markets (which disperse it), or networks (which parcel
> it out to trusted collaborators), platforms exercise power over economic transactions by delegating control
> among the participants. They do so by establishing a digital infrastructure with which to govern the service
> triangle that links employers, workers, and customers (Leidner 1993). **The effect allows the platform to play
> the role of tertius gaudens—'the third who rejoices'—as in Simmel's classic theory of the triad (in Wolff
> 1950).**" (p. 282, emphasis added)

The chapter at line 208 writes "The mediator is Simmel's (1908/1950) tertius gaudens grown a machine" and cites
only Simmel. Vallas and Schor made that exact identification in 2020, in the Annual Review of Sociology, and a
platform-studies referee will know it. The chapter must cite them for it and then say what "grown a machine"
adds. What it adds is real — Vallas and Schor's tertius is an interested third that *delegates* control, not an
opaque one that *computes* determinations — but the chapter has to claim that difference rather than the whole
figure.

**Rival B — Kornberger, Pflueger & Mouritsen, quoted by Vallas & Schor at p. 282:** "control is radically
distributed, while power remains centralized" (Kornberger et al. 2017, p. 79, as quoted in Vallas & Schor 2020,
p. 282). This is the interested-mediator claim in one clause.

**Rival C — Stark & Pais (2020), p. 53.** They already name the topology and, tellingly, credit it to Vallas and
Schor: "what is not obvious (in much of the research on algorithmic management) is that this **triangular
geometry (Vallas & Schor, 2020, p. 282)** and the power asymmetries along its various axes are key to
understanding algorithmic management in the platform economy." (read at
https://sociologica.unibo.it/article/download/12221/12281/43213)

**Rival D — Kellogg, Valentine & Christin (2020). This is the closest rival to the CONJUNCTION itself, and the
chapter cites it on the wrong side of the argument.** Their section heading is *"Algorithmic Technologies:
Comprehensive, Instantaneous, Interactive, and Opaque."* Opacity is one of their four defining features, and
they take it from Burrell: "algorithms can be opaque, for three main reasons: intentional secrecy, required
technical literacy, and machine-learning opacity (Burrell, 2016)." Their whole frame — Edwards' contested
terrain — is that *employers* deploy these systems *in their own interest against workers who resist*. Opacity
plus interestedness is therefore already conjoined in the 2020 Annals review. What Kellogg et al. do not do is
draw the *governance* consequence: they treat opacity as a property of a control technology inside an
employment relation, not as the thing that defeats a whole modality of accountability across a triad in which
neither coordinated party is an employee.

**Rival E — O'Reilly, Strauss & Mazzucato (2024), "Algorithmic attention rents."** The political-economy rival.
See Part 4 below; it theorizes an interested mediator adjusting an opaque allocation to extract rent.

**Rival F — Xu, Xie & Burtch (2025), "An Empirical Study of Strategic Opacity in Crowdsourced Evaluations," MIS
Quarterly.** This is the sharpest analytic threat, because it attacks the *separability* of the chapter's two
prongs. If opacity is itself chosen by the interested operator, then "opaque AND interested" is not a
conjunction of two independent properties; it is one property (interestedness) expressing itself in a design
choice. The chapter's own line 347 ("Opacity is a rent-preserving strategy") concedes exactly this and thereby
undercuts the two-prong structure of §3. A referee will notice the inconsistency between line 186 (opacity as an
intrinsic property of high-dimensional systems) and line 347 (opacity as a strategy).

**Where the novelty should be relocated.** Not in the conjunction as a description — that is taken or nearly
taken — but in the consequence the chapter alone draws: that the conjunction defeats *disclosure as a modality*
and therefore requires a different competence and a different form of standing. Lines 254–255 already say this
("The failure is of a modality, disclosure, and it points at the modality that would succeed, which is
standing"). That sentence should carry the novelty claim, and lines 186–191 should be rewritten to concede
priority on the description while claiming the consequence.

### T2. "Algorithmic management is just hierarchy in new clothes" / "platforms are market–hierarchy hybrids"

**Verdict on the ARGUMENT: adequate. Verdict on the CITATIONS at lines 176–177: two of the four are wrong, and
both are wrong in the same direction — the chapter cites authors who reject the objection as though they
advanced it. This is the single most punishable error in §3.**

Line 176–177 reads:

> "...the familiar objections, that algorithmic management is hierarchy in new clothes, digital Taylorism, the
> automation of managerial control (Kellogg et al., 2020; Rahman, 2021), or that platforms are hybrids of market
> and hierarchy (Robinson & Meijerink, 2026; Vallas & Schor, 2020)."

**Miscitation 1 — Kellogg, Valentine & Christin (2020).** They argue the opposite. From their own abstract
(read at https://angelechristin.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/Algorithms-at-Work_Annals.pdf, p. 366):

> "Second, the technical capabilities of algorithmic systems facilitate a form of rational control that is
> **distinct from the technical and bureaucratic control** used by employers for the past century."

Kellogg et al. are on the chapter's side of the dispute, not against it.

**Miscitation 2 — Rahman (2021).** The invisible cage is defined *against* prior work on transparent-criteria
evaluation. From the abstract (OpenAlex record for 10.1177/00018392211010118):

> "Much of this research has focused on evaluations with transparent criteria, but increasingly, algorithmic
> evaluation systems are not transparent. ... These workers experienced what I call an 'invisible cage': a form
> of control in which the criteria for success and changes to those criteria are unpredictable."

Rahman's whole contribution is the novelty of opaque and unstable criteria. Citing him for "digital Taylorism"
inverts him.

**Miscitation 3 — Vallas & Schor (2020).** They explicitly deny the hybrid reading. Abstract, verbatim:

> "...which leads us to introduce an alternative image of platforms: as permissive potentates that externalize
> responsibility and control over economic transactions while still exercising concentrated power. As a
> consequence, **platforms represent a distinct type of governance mechanism, different from markets,
> hierarchies, or networks**, and therefore pose a unique set of problems for regulators, workers, and their
> competitors in the conventional economy."

And in the body, p. 281:

> "While platforms incorporate many of the features of prior economic structures—markets, hierarchies, and
> networks (Powell 1990)—they do so selectively, generating a type of governance mechanism that is qualitatively
> distinct from its precursors... we argue that platforms constitute a new type of governance mechanism with
> which employers can conduct economic transactions."

**Correct citation 4 — Robinson et al. (2026).** This one does make the hybrid argument, and correctly. From
the abstract (OpenAlex, 10.1002/hrm.70019): "how these algorithmic systems embed both market and hierarchy
management principles"; "we explore how task characteristics influence the joint application of both market and
hierarchy principles." Substance fine; author list wrong (Part 2).

### T3. The Selznick argument at lines 164–169

**Verdict: NOT DEFENSIBLE AS WRITTEN. A referee who has read TVA will punish it. But the correction makes the
paragraph better, not worse, and it strengthens §6.**

The chapter claims Selznick's co-optation was "a bargain that granted the co-opted a real seat in exchange for
their compliance" and that the platform form, keeping the enrollment and dropping the concession, is therefore
"near its antonym."

Selznick divides co-optation in two, and the chapter's characterization fits only one half. Full text read at
https://archive.org/stream/tvandgrassrootss00selzrich/tvandgrassrootss00selzrich_djvu.txt (University of
California Press, 1949).

**The definition (p. 13):**
> "To risk a definition: cooptation is the process of absorbing new elements into the leadership or
> policy-determining structure of an organization as a means of averting threats to its stability or existence."

**Formal co-optation (pp. 13–14) — the half that kills the chapter's sentence:**
> "Formal cooptation. — When there is a need for the organization to publicly absorb new elements, we shall
> speak of formal cooptation. This involves the establishment of openly avowed and formally ordered
> relationships. Appointments to official posts are made, contracts are signed, new organizations are
> established — all signifying participation in the process of decision and administration."

and, decisively:

> "**In general, the use of formal cooptation by a leadership does not envision the transfer of actual power. The
> forms of participation are emphasized but action is channeled so as to fulfill the administrative functions
> while preserving the locus of significant decision in the hands of the initiating group.**"

Selznick restates it at the head of Part Three (p. 217): formal co-optation is the case "in which is uppermost
the need to share the responsibilities for or the administrative burdens of power, **rather than power itself**."

**Informal co-optation (pp. 14–15) — the half the chapter is actually describing:**
> "Informal cooptation. — Cooptation may be, however, a response to the pressure of specific centers of power
> within the community... They may be brought into the leadership or policy-determining structure, may be given
> a place as a recognition of and concession to the resources they can independently command."

So: informal co-optation concedes real power to those who can already compel it; formal co-optation concedes
the forms of participation and nothing else. Part Three of TVA — a third of the book — is about the second.
Saying the platform form is "near the antonym" of Selznick's concept is, on Selznick's own text, wrong about
half of it, and wrong about the half whose structure the platform most closely reproduces.

**The recommended reframe, which is stronger than what is there now.** Platform co-optation is Selznick's
*formal* co-optation with the forms of participation removed. Selznick's formal co-optation preserved "the
locus of significant decision in the hands of the initiating group" while granting a ceremonial seat; platform
enrollment preserves the locus and grants not even the ceremony. That is a narrowing along Selznick's own axis
rather than a reversal of it, it is a much harder claim to attack, and it hands §6 its central figure — the
"protected but unstanding actor" (line 578) is precisely the formally co-opted subject.

**Is "co-optation" a term-hijack?** Partly, and not the chapter's hijack. Two things a referee should be told:
1. **Stark and Pais do not derive their "co-opt" from Selznick at all.** Selznick appears nowhere in Stark &
   Pais (2020) — confirmed by full-text search of the article PDF — and nowhere in Stark & Vanden Broeck (2024)
   either. Their term comes from Watkins, E. A., & Stark, D. (2018). The Möbius organizational form: Make, buy,
   cooperate, or co-opt? *Sociologica, 12*(1), 65–80. DOI 10.6092/issn.1971-8853/8364 (VERIFIED at OpenAlex),
   where "co-opt" means *appropriating assets and activities that are not part of the firm* — closer to the
   everyday commercial sense than to Selznick's.
2. Therefore the chapter is not correcting a Selznickian usage; it is *introducing* Selznick into a line of work
   that never invoked him. That is legitimate and it is a contribution, but it has to be stated that way. As
   written, lines 164–169 read as though Stark and Pais had borrowed Selznick's term and strained it.

Getting this right also lets the chapter keep the keyword "co-optation" (line 16) and the glossary entry
(Key Terms) without exposure.

### T4. §3.2's four blocking features — is the list complete? Is any feature weak?

**Verdict: two strong, one misplaced, one weak, and one important feature missing.**

**Feature 1 (lines 217–221, the object resists reading; Burrell). STRONG. Verified.** Burrell's three opacities
are correctly reported. Verbatim: "(1) opacity as intentional corporate or state secrecy, (2) opacity as
technical illiteracy, and (3) an opacity that arises from the characteristics of machine learning algorithms and
the scale required to apply them usefully." (read at https://www.morgan-klaus.com/readings/opacity-ml.html;
corroborated by Kellogg et al. 2020, p. 372, who paraphrase it as "intentional secrecy, required technical
literacy, and machine-learning opacity"). One caution: the chapter leans on Burrell's *third* opacity as the one
that "survives the release of source code," which is right, but Burrell herself also writes that firms may be
"embracing opacity for the specific purpose of control, manipulation, discrimination, and lack of regulation."
That sentence is useful for line 347 (Part 4a) and it slightly complicates the chapter's clean split between
intrinsic and strategic opacity.

**Feature 2 (lines 223–228, reading without a channel; Ananny & Crawford). STRONG. Verified.** Abstract:
"Being able to see a system is sometimes equated with being able to know how it works and govern it... we
critically interrogate the ideal of transparency... and present 10 limitations to its application." Supports the
chapter's use exactly. It could be strengthened with a 2025 empirical anchor: Papaevangelou, C., & Votta, F.
(2025). Trading nuance for scale? Platform observability and content governance under the DSA. *Internet Policy
Review, 14*(3). DOI 10.14763/2025.3.2037 (VERIFIED at Crossref) — 439 million DSA Statements of Reasons across
eight VLOPs, overwhelmingly automated. Disclosure at that volume is a fact without a consequence, empirically.

**Feature 3 (lines 230–238, no leverage; Cutolo & Kenney, Rahman, Wood et al.). SUBSTANTIVELY TRUE BUT
MISPLACED, AND IT BREAKS THE LIST'S OWN LOGIC.** Line 214–215 promises "four structural features [that] put
platform coordination beyond the competences," each of which "blocks the literate model of accountability at a
different point." Features 1, 2 and 4 are properties of the object or of the governing instrument. Feature 3 is
a property of the *actor's bargaining position*. It does not block literacy; it blocks contestation whether or
not the actor is literate. It is also where §3.2 starts duplicating §3.3, which `REVIEW.md` (lines 148, 305)
already flags as the section's redundancy problem. Either move it into §3.3, or re-describe it as a feature of
the *relation* rather than of the actor, so the list is internally consistent.

The sources under Feature 3 are all correctly used, though:
- Cutolo & Kenney (2021) abstract: "the power imbalance at the heart of the relationship between the platform
  and its ecosystem members is intrinsic to the economics and the technological architecture of digital
  platforms." Supports lines 230–232.
- Rahman (2021): "invisible cage" quote above. Supports lines 232–235 exactly.
- Wood et al. (2019) abstract: "Algorithmic management techniques tend to offer workers high levels of
  flexibility, autonomy, task variety and complexity. However, these mechanisms of control can also result in
  low pay, social isolation, working unsocial and irregular hours, overwork, sleep deprivation and exhaustion."
  Supports line 236 ("The flexibility such platforms advertise is real, and it operates inside that control")
  well. ABSTRACT ONLY for the specific "cannot see what her choices are measured against" clause — that is the
  chapter's gloss, not Wood et al.'s phrase; consider softening or moving that clause to Rahman.

**Feature 4 (lines 240–255, disclosure is a literate instrument; Hildebrandt, Cohen, Pasquale). WEAKEST.**
Two problems.
1. *It is not a fourth structural feature.* It is Features 1 and 2 restated at the level of the regulatory
   instrument. The paragraph then spends most of its length conceding ("The point is not that law never reaches
   the coordination layer... Recent European instruments reach into ranking and automated decisions directly")
   and walking the concession back ("Against an opaque, adaptive coordination the wager mostly fails"). The
   hedge "mostly" is doing a lot of work and a referee will pull on it.
2. *The Platform Work Directive is omitted* — see T2, item 1. As the list stands, the strongest counterexample
   to Feature 4 is the instrument the chapter itself later celebrates.

**MISSING FEATURE — adaptivity / non-stationarity. This is the one the chapter most needs and the one that most
directly carries its own novelty claim.** A disclosure is a snapshot; the object is a moving target. Lines
188–190 name the property ("reconfigures the system continuously"), §4 builds a whole competence dimension on it
(the temporal part, lines 295–296), and §3.2's list leaves it out. Rahman (2021) supplies the citation ready-made:
the invisible cage is "a form of control in which the criteria for success **and changes to those criteria** are
unpredictable." Adding this as a fifth feature — or as a replacement for the misplaced Feature 3 — would:
- make the list four features of the object/instrument rather than three plus one about power;
- give §3.2 the feature that the disclosure literature genuinely cannot answer;
- pre-empt the obvious rejoinder that periodic mandated disclosure would fix Features 1 and 2.

## PART 4 — FRESH GROUNDING FOR WEAK CLAIMS

### 4a. "Opacity is a rent-preserving strategy" (line 347)

**Confirmed: neither cited source makes the argument.**
- *Cutolo & Kenney (2021)* argue power asymmetry from Emerson's power-dependence theory, rooted in "the
  economics and the technological architecture of digital platforms." Opacity is not their mechanism and rent is
  not their vocabulary. (Abstract read via OpenAlex.)
- *Zuboff (2019)* argues about behavioural surplus, prediction products, and instrumentarian power. Secrecy
  appears, but as a condition of dispossession, not as a rent-preserving strategy over ranking discretion.
  O'Reilly et al. (2024) make this exact point about Zuboff in print: "The dominant policy narrative... focuses
  on user data and privacy... Zuboff (2019) calls this surveillance capitalism. We argue instead that it may be
  more productive to understand platform market power... [through] algorithmic control over user attention."

**Best available replacement, and it is a good one:**

**O'Reilly, T., Strauss, I., & Mazzucato, M. (2024). Algorithmic attention rents: A theory of digital platform
market power. *Data & Policy, 6*, e6. https://doi.org/10.1017/dap.2024.1**
VERIFIED at Crossref (`api.crossref.org/works/10.1017/dap.2024.1`). **FULL TEXT READ** — version of record at
https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10188892/1/Mazzucato_Algorithmic%20attention%20rents_VoR.pdf

Verbatim support:

> "Even when price is a factor, as in e-commerce or an advertising marketplace, the platform's proprietary
> matching algorithms internalize and centralize the otherwise decentralized market mechanism. **Furthermore,
> this internalized and centralized market is opaque.**"

> "Algorithmically driven attention allocations by the platform, matching users to suboptimal information, can
> create rents... A platform is increasing profits not from better matches (productivity improvements), but from
> information matches that are more profitable for itself, or from higher matching fees."

> "A major implication of this work is **the need for greater disclosure, to allow regulators, investors, and
> the public to better observe, measure, and ultimately regulate potential harms stemming from how user
> attention is allocated.**"

> "Platforms will claim, with some justice, that disclosure will harm their businesses, as it will allow third
> parties to game their systems more easily. But this is akin to the old approach to cybersecurity, of 'security
> through obscurity.'"

**What this supports and what it does not.** It supports: (i) that the platform's rent comes from discretion
over algorithmic allocation; (ii) that the market in which that discretion operates is opaque; (iii) that
opacity is what prevents observation and therefore contestation, which is why disclosure is the proposed remedy;
(iv) that the platform actively resists disclosure. It does **not** state, as a thesis, that platforms *choose*
opacity *in order to* preserve rent. That intentional formulation is one step beyond what O'Reilly et al. argue.

**Xu, L., Xie, Q., & Burtch, G. (2025). An empirical study of strategic opacity in crowdsourced evaluations.

### 4b. Bottleneck POWER vs RENT, and multihoming (lines 264–272)

**Armstrong, M. (2006). Competition in two-sided markets. *RAND Journal of Economics, 37*(3), 668–691. DOI
10.1111/j.1756-2171.2006.tb00037.x** (VERIFIED at Crossref), whose section 5 is titled "Model III: Competitive
Bottlenecks." I read the author's own working version (August 2002, revised May 2005) at
https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/14583/1/14583.pdf. Armstrong and Wright (2007) apply the concept; they do not coin
it. Cite Armstrong (2006) for the term.

**The mechanism, verbatim from Armstrong:**

> "...there are several important markets that look like configuration (iii), and in section 5 these cases are
> termed 'competitive bottlenecks'. Here, if it wishes to interact with an agent on the single-homing side, the
> multi-homing side has no choice except to deal with that agent's chosen platform. **Thus, platforms have
> monopoly power over providing access to their single-homing customers for the multi-homing side. This monopoly
> power naturally leads to high prices being charged to the multi-homing side**... By contrast, **platforms do
> have to compete for the single-homing agents, and high profits generated from the multi-homing side are to a
> large extent passed on to the single-homing side in the form of low prices (or even zero prices).**"

and later:

> "As with all the competitive bottleneck models, the equilibrium maximizes the joint surplus of the platforms
> and the single-homing group..., **and the interests of the multihoming side (the suppliers) are ignored.**"

**What the chapter says (lines 266–272):**

> "...where the coordinated can multihome to a rival platform, competition on the contested side erodes the
> toll; where they cannot, the mediator faces what Armstrong and Wright (2007) call a competitive bottleneck and
> can extract a share of the coordination's value for the position alone. ... They generate the coordination the
> platform monetizes, and, **single-homed**, they occupy positions from which the platform extracts the surplus."

**Three errors:**

1. **The homing direction is inverted.** In Armstrong's model the *single-homing* side is the side platforms
   compete for and subsidise; the *multi-homing* side is the side whose surplus is extracted. The chapter has
   the coordinated actor extracted from *because* it is single-homed. That is the opposite of the result it
   cites.
2. **"The mediator faces a competitive bottleneck"** reverses the subject. The platform does not face a
   bottleneck; the platform *is* the bottleneck — it holds monopoly access to its single-homing users.
3. **The term is misattributed** (see above).

**The good news: the correct version fits the chapter's own examples better.** Take the App Store and the
marketplace, which are §5.4's and §8's examples. Consumers single-home (one phone, one primary shopping app);
developers and sellers must reach *all* consumers and therefore multihome across stores and marketplaces. The
platform holds monopoly access to *its* single-homing consumers, and the toll — the 30% commission, the
placement fee — falls on the multihoming developers and sellers. That is Armstrong's competitive bottleneck,
stated correctly, and it delivers exactly the conclusion the chapter wants: platform-dependent actors are
"essential to a platform's value and powerless within it at the same time." The chapter has the right conclusion
via the wrong route.

**Rochet & Tirole (2003).** The citation at line 266 is metadata-correct but analytically loose. R&T 2003 builds
"a model of platform competition with two-sided markets" and "unveils the determinants of price allocation and
end-user surplus" (abstract). It theorises the *price structure* between two sides — how the total price is
split — not bottleneck power. As a founding-paper gesture ("the economics of two-sided markets studies the terms
of its exercise") it passes. If the chapter wants a source that actually theorises the bottleneck, that source
is Armstrong (2006). O'Reilly et al. (2024) independently confirm the reading: they note that their dynamics
"are similar to those in a competitive bottleneck model," with "users on one side of the market primarily
engag[ing] with just one platform."

**Recommended rewrite of the mechanism (content, not prose):** bottleneck power is topological — it follows from
standing where all coordination must flow. Whether it converts into rent depends on the *homing pattern*: where
the coordinated actor can reach its counterparts through more than one mediator, and where the mediator must
compete for the counterparts, the toll is disciplined. Where the mediator holds exclusive access to
counterparts who deal through it alone, the coordinated actor must go through that mediator to reach them, and
the mediator can price that access at will. Cite Armstrong (2006) for the result and Armstrong & Wright (2007)
for the exclusive-contracts extension.

**One caution to carry forward.** The same inverted mapping appears in §5.3 at lines 445–447, cited to Eisenmann,
Parker & Van Alstyne (2006). Fixing §3.3 without fixing §5.3 leaves the chapter inconsistent with itself.
