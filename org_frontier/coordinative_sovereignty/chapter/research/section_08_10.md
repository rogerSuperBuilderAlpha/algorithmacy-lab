### 1.2 The Platform Work Directive (chapter lines 660, 695–701) — **THE CHAPTER IS WRONG HERE**

VERIFIED (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/2831/oj/eng and
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/NIM/?uri=CELEX:32024L2831):

- Full title: *Directive (EU) 2024/2831 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 23 October 2024 on
  improving working conditions in platform work*
- **OJ L, 2024/2831, 11.11.2024**
- **Entered into force 1 December 2024**
- **Transposition deadline 2 December 2026** — three months AFTER this chapter's submission date
- **National transposition measures notified on EUR-Lex: NONE.** The NIM tab for CELEX 32024L2831 lists no
  member-state measures.
- Corroborating: Ius Laboris implementation tracker, article dated **27 April 2026**, states plainly that
  **no member state has fully transposed the Directive**
  (https://iuslaboris.com/insights/eu-platform-work-directive-which-countries-have-implemented/)
- Widely reported per-country breakdown (SECONDARY, treat as indicative not citable): as of mid-2026, 0 member
  states with a transposing law in force, ~4 with a pre-existing presumption partly in place (Spain's Riders'
  Law is the standard example), ~5 drafting, the rest not started

**Consequence for the chapter.** Line 696 says a platform worker "now benefits from a presumption of
employment." That is false in present tense. As of August 2026 no worker anywhere in the EU benefits from
the Directive's presumption by virtue of the Directive. The rights the chapter attributes to workers are
rights the Directive *requires member states to create by 2 December 2026*. Recommended re-wording: the
Directive *establishes* / *will require member states to provide*, and it is *in transposition, with the
deadline falling on 2 December 2026 and no member state having yet transposed*. Line 660's "now in
transposition across European jurisdictions" is accurate and should set the tense for line 696.

**Article numbers, all VERIFIED from the enacting terms**
(https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402831):

- **CHAPTER II — EMPLOYMENT STATUS.** Art 4 *Determination of correct employment status*; **Art 5 *Legal
  presumption***; Art 6 *Framework of supporting measures*
- **CHAPTER III — ALGORITHMIC MANAGEMENT.** **Art 7** *Limitations on the processing of personal data by
  means of automated monitoring systems or automated decision-making systems*; Art 8 *Data-protection impact
  assessment*; **Art 9** *Transparency with regard to automated monitoring systems and automated
  decision-making systems*; **Art 10** *Human oversight of automated monitoring systems and automated
  decision-making systems*; **Art 11** *Human review*; Art 12 *Safety and health*; **Art 13** *Information
  and consultation*; Art 14 *Provision of information to workers*; Art 15 *Specific arrangements for
  representatives of persons performing platform work other than platform workers' representatives*
- CHAPTER IV: Art 16 *Declaration of platform work*; Art 17 *Access to relevant information on platform work*
- CHAPTER V: Art 18 *Right to redress*; Art 19 *Procedures on behalf or in support of persons performing
  platform work*; Art 20 *Communication channels*; Art 21 *Access to evidence*; Art 22 *Protection against
  adverse treatment or consequences*

**The collective-bargaining clause at line 699–700 IS supported. Article 25 exists.**

> **CORRECTION LOGGED.** An earlier pass in this session concluded that no collective-bargaining obligation
> appears in the enacting terms. That conclusion was **wrong**, and it was wrong for an instructive reason:
> the EUR-Lex HTML rendering of this Directive truncates around Article 22 under automated fetch, and a
> keyword search run over the truncated text therefore returned nothing. The error was caught by an
> independent verification pass and then confirmed against the Official Journal PDF. Two independent
> readings of the same instrument disagreed, and the PDF settled it.

Verbatim from the Official Journal PDF
(https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202402831, p. 24):

> **Article 25 — Promotion of collective bargaining in platform work**
>
> Member States shall, without prejudice to the autonomy of the social partners and taking into account the
> diversity of national practices, take adequate measures to promote the role of the social partners and
> encourage the exercise of the right to collective bargaining in platform work, including measures with
> regard to the determination of the correct employment status of platform workers **and to facilitate the
> exercise of their rights related to algorithmic management set out in Chapter III.**

**That final clause is the single best textual gift the chapter will find in European law.** Article 25 does
not merely mandate the promotion of collective bargaining. It ties collective bargaining explicitly to the
exercise of the algorithmic-management rights in Chapter III. A European legislature has written into
binding law the proposition that rights over an algorithmic system need collective backing to be exercised
at all — which is the chapter's own argument at lines 452–458 and 594–597 about voice needing institutional
backing. Quote Article 25 in §8. It converts an assertion into a legislative fact.

The supporting provisions, all read off the same OJ PDF:

- **Art 13 *Information and consultation*** — member states "shall ensure that information and consultation,
  as defined in Article 2, points (f) and (g), of Directive 2002/14/EC, of workers' representatives by
  digital labour platforms **also covers decisions likely to lead to the introduction of or to substantial
  changes in the use of automated monitoring systems or automated decision-making systems**." **Art 13(3)**
  entitles representatives to an expert of their choice, at the platform's expense where it has more than
  250 workers in the member state.
- **Art 28 *Collective agreements and specific rules on the processing of personal data*** — member states
  may allow social partners to "maintain, negotiate, conclude and enforce collective agreements … which,
  while respecting the overall protection of platform workers, establish arrangements concerning platform
  work which differ from those referred to in Articles 12 and 13."
- **Art 29(3)** — member states "shall … take adequate measures to ensure the effective involvement of the
  social partners and to promote and enhance social dialogue with a view to implementing this Directive";
  **Art 29(4)** lets member states entrust implementation to the social partners on their joint request.
- **Art 6(d)** — member states must "provide for appropriate training for national competent authorities and
  provide for the availability of technical expertise **in the field of algorithmic management**."

Also verified from the OJ PDF: **Art 29(1)** — "Member States shall bring into force the laws, regulations
and administrative provisions necessary to comply with this Directive **by 2 December 2026**." **Art 30** —
Commission review by 2 December 2029. **Art 31** — entry into force on the twentieth day after publication
(hence 1 December 2024). **Art 32** — addressed to the Member States. Done at Strasbourg, 23 October 2024.

**Corrigendum.** Corrigendum to Directive (EU) 2024/2831, **OJ L, 2025/90196, 28.2.2025**, ELI
`http://data.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/283/corrigendum/2025-02-28/oj` (EN and MT only). It repairs a truncated
definition in **Article 2(1), point (g)** ("representatives of persons performing platform work"). It does
not touch anything the chapter cites, but use the corrected text if Art 2(1)(g) is ever quoted.
(Reported by the citation-verification pass; **not independently re-read** — PARTIAL.)

**So the only defect in the chapter's sentence at lines 695–701 is the tense**, not the content. Every right
it lists is in the Directive, at the articles named above. What is false is "now benefits from."

### 1.3 DSA vetted-researcher data access (chapter lines 245, 658–661)

VERIFIED (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32025R2050):

- **Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/2050 of 1 July 2025** supplementing Regulation (EU) 2022/2065
  "by laying down the technical conditions and procedures under which providers of very large online
  platforms and of very large online search engines are to share data with vetted researchers"
- **OJ L, 2025/2050, 9.10.2025**; **in force** from the twentieth day after publication (**29 October 2025**)
- Procedure: a researcher affiliated with a research organisation applies to the **Digital Services
  Coordinator of establishment**, which has **80 working days** to decide whether to formulate a reasoned
  request; the DSC verifies affiliation, independence from commercial interests, and necessity
- Article 3 establishes the **DSA data access portal** as "the central digital point for information on the
  data access process"
- The portal exists and resolves at **https://data-access.dsa.ec.europa.eu/** (the page is a JavaScript
  application; only the title rendered to an automated fetch, so its live content is UNVERIFIED)

**Has any researcher actually been vetted and granted access? UNKNOWN.** No official statistics page was
reachable. Do not assert either way in the chapter. Defensible wording: the delegated act is in force since
October 2025 and the access procedure runs through national Digital Services Coordinators, with the
operative record of grants not yet publicly established.

**Note for the threat-hunt (§5 below):** Art 40 DSA is the chapter's paradigm of an instrument that "stops
at legibility." That classification is contestable — Delegated Regulation 2025/2050 creates a *procedural
standing* for a third party (the researcher, via the DSC) against the platform, with deadlines and a
dispute route. The chapter itself concedes as much at line 658–661.

### 1.4 P2B Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 (chapter lines 244, 693–694) — **STILL IN FORCE, AND PROPOSED FOR REPEAL**

VERIFIED (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/ALL/?uri=CELEX:32019R1150):

- *Regulation (EU) 2019/1150 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 20 June 2019 on promoting
  fairness and transparency for business users of online intermediation services*
- **OJ L 186, 11.7.2019, pp. 57–79**; applicable since **12 July 2020**; **in force**, no end date
- Interpreted by the Court of Justice in preliminary rulings C-662/22, C-663/22, C-664/22

**The critical currency finding: the Digital Omnibus proposes to REPEAL it.** COM(2025) 837 final, 19
November 2025, procedure **2025/0360(COD)**, is a *Proposal for a Regulation … as regards the simplification
of the digital legislative framework, and **repealing Regulations (EU) 2018/1807, (EU) 2019/1150, (EU)
2022/868, and Directive (EU) 2019/1024***. VERIFIED at
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/HIS/?uri=CELEX:52025PC0837. **Status: ONGOING, not adopted.**
Procedural steps recorded: Commission adoption 19/11/2025; ECB opinion (CON/2026/9) 10/03/2026; EESC opinion
18/03/2026; Committee of the Regions opinion 07/05/2026. The proposal also amends the GDPR (2016/679), the
Single Digital Gateway Regulation, Regulation 2018/1725, the Data Act (2023/2854), the ePrivacy Directive,
NIS2, and the CER Directive.

**The chapter must therefore mark P2B as in force but under a live repeal proposal.** A law-and-governance
referee reading in late 2026 will know this, and the chapter's §8 sentence about "successor instruments to
the AI Act and the DMA" (line 738) is now a testable prediction that the omnibus partly answers — see §5
below.

**The chapter's characterisation of P2B at line 693–694 is wrong as written.** It says P2B and the DSA "let
a firm see how it ranks without letting it contest the ranking." That is true of P2B **Article 5 (Ranking)**
taken alone. It is false of the Regulation. VERIFIED article list
(https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019R1150):

- Art 5 *Ranking* — "Providers of online intermediation services shall set out in their terms and conditions
  the main parameters determining ranking and the reasons for the relative importance of those main
  parameters as opposed to other parameters." (legibility)
- **Art 11 *Internal complaint-handling system*** — "Providers of online intermediation services shall
  provide for an internal system for handling the complaints of business users." (standing, case level)
- **Art 12 *Mediation*** and Art 13 *Specialised mediators* — the provider must name two or more mediators
  in its terms and engage in out-of-court settlement, including for complaints unresolved under Art 11
  (standing, case level)
- **Art 14 *Judicial proceedings by representative organisations or associations and by public bodies*** —
  organisations "that have a legitimate interest in representing business users … shall have the right to
  take action before competent national courts in the Union … to stop or prohibit any non-compliance"
  (**collective standing**)

So the earliest and most modest of the instruments the chapter treats as a pure legibility instrument
already contains a case-level contestation channel and a collective-action right. Fix required. See §5.

## 4. THE DIFFUSION MECHANISM (§9 lines 802–805, §10 lines 826–833)

### 4.1 The chapter's exposure

§10 asserts a causal sequence: "democratic sovereignty followed the diffusion of the competence that made it
possible," and asks whether algorithmacy "can be democratized as literacy was." The chapter never says how
literacy diffused. A referee from history or governance will supply the answer, and the answer is not
flattering to the analogy.

### 4.2 How mass literacy actually diffused — the citable accounts

**Compulsory state schooling is the dominant mechanism, and the historiography now attributes it to social
control rather than to emancipation.**

- **Meyer, Ramirez & Soysal (1992)**, *Sociology of Education* 65(2) — the world-institutional account of
  mass education's expansion as a global model diffusing through the state system rather than through local
  functional demand. DOI 10.2307/2112679 (VERIFIED via Crossref).
- **Paglayan (2024), *Raised to Obey*** (Princeton UP; DOI 10.1515/9780691261775, ISBN 9780691261775,
  VERIFIED via Crossref) — the strongest recent statement. Testing the timing of compulsory-schooling
  legislation and enrolment spikes across Western Europe and Latin America from the eighteenth century,
  Paglayan finds the standard explanations (suffrage extension, industrial labour demand, nationalism,
  military need) do not fit the timing; what consistently precedes nationwide expansion is **internal
  unrest**. Mass education is an investment in social order, a cheaper substitute for repression. Reviewed
  in *History of Education Quarterly* (Koganzon, 2025) and *Economic History Review* (Cappelli, 2026), the
  latter pressing the objection that national school laws were often ineffective where implementation was
  decentralised — i.e. even the compulsion mechanism worked unevenly.
- **Vincent, D. (2000). *The rise of mass literacy: Reading and writing in modern Europe*. Polity.**
  (Bibliographic details reconstructed from two review articles in *History: Reviews of New Books*, not
  from the publisher page — **PARTIAL, verify before citing.**) The reviews agree on Vincent's thesis: the
  modern state and mass literacy stood in **mutual dependency**; states came to see literacy as a
  precondition for the spread of official culture; church schools that taught morality alongside reading
  were gradually supplanted by state-controlled schools "as literacy became recognized as a necessary
  instrument of socialization." Tsarist Russia is the counter-case — a state that judged reading subversive
  and withheld it.

**Protestant reading movements: real, and the causal claim is contested.**

- **Becker & Woessmann (2009)**, QJE 124(2), 531–596, DOI 10.1162/qjec.2009.124.2.531 — the canonical
  quantitative claim: Protestant counties in nineteenth-century Prussia were more prosperous because they
  were more literate, instrumented by distance from Wittenberg.
- **Edwards (2021)**, *Journal of Applied Econometrics* 36(6), 853–858, DOI 10.1002/jae.2851 — a replication
  concluding the reformulation is "untenable" once regional effects are included, because the spatial
  instrument fails the exclusion restriction.
- **Cantoni (2015)**, JEEA 13(4), 561–598, DOI 10.1111/jeea.12117 — finds no effect of Protestantism on
  growth across 272 cities, 1300–1900, and reads Becker & Woessmann as at best a story about the *rural*
  second industrial revolution, where "literacy was … a form of dormant capital only in the Protestant
  countryside."
- **Rubin, J. (2014). Printing and Protestants: An empirical test of the role of printing in the
  Reformation. *The Review of Economics and Statistics, 96*(2), 270–286. DOI 10.1162/rest_a_00368**
  (VERIFIED via Crossref) — cities with at least one printing press by 1500 were at minimum 29 percentage
  points more likely to be Protestant by 1600. This is the citable version of the print-capitalism channel,
  and it is about the *diffusion of the religious movement*, not directly about literacy.
- **Fernihough et al. (2024)**, *Explorations in Economic History*, on Ireland 1901: denominational literacy
  gaps existed **before** centralised national schooling (1831) and were **independent of school
  attendance** — Presbyterians attended less and read more. (Consensus record; DOI UNVERIFIED.) This is the
  best evidence that literacy diffused partly *outside* schooling, through household and congregational
  religious practice.

**The critical tradition.** Graff's "literacy myth" (Graff, 2018, *Early Modern Literacies*, and the earlier
book-length statement) holds that literacy's supposed emancipatory effects are a nineteenth-century
ideology; Street's ideological model and Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (already cited at chapter line 102)
carry the same charge — that literacy campaigns served state legibility. The chapter already cites Scott,
Street, and Scribner & Cole. It has the critical apparatus in hand and does not apply it to its own
concluding analogy.

### 4.3 Is there an analogue for algorithmic competence? Yes — and the evidence cuts against the chapter

Four candidate mechanisms, with what the literature actually shows.

**(a) A commercial expertise market. Documented, and documented as compliance-producing.**

- **Bishop (2020)**, *Social Media + Society* 6(1), DOI 10.1177/2056305119897323 — self-styled "algorithmic
  experts" sell theorisations of visibility to creators, positioning themselves as YouTube's adversaries
  while "ultimately they teach creators to be complicit with YouTube's organizational strategies and
  business models," with a meritocratic logic that yields "individualized and patchwork solutions that do
  not address the significant socio-economic inequalities."
- **MacDonald (2023)**, *New Media & Society* 25(6), 1412–1431, DOI 10.1177/14614448211021404 — algorithmic
  lore videos are "market devices" that are economically productive *for the platform*: they legitimise
  narratives of algorithmic objectivity, teach creators to value content by platform metrics, and justify
  continued production when it does not pay.

**This is the single most useful finding in this section.** The market analogue to the schoolmaster exists,
it is thriving, and two independent ethnographies find that it diffuses *compliance* rather than *standing*.
That is exactly the chapter's own distinction between legibility and standing, reappearing at the level of
competence rather than of instruments. The chapter can make this its answer.

**(b) Peer knowledge-sharing among the coordinated. Documented, and structurally limited.**

- **Bishop (2019)**, *New Media & Society* 21(11–12), 2589–2606, DOI 10.1177/1461444819854731 — "algorithmic
  gossip": communally formulated theories and strategies about recommender algorithms, shared to sustain
  visibility. This is the collective, bottom-up diffusion channel, and it is the closest living analogue to
  pre-school literacy transmission through household and congregation.
- **Cotter (2023)**, *Information, Communication & Society* 26(6), 1226–1243, DOI 10.1080/1369118X.2021.1994624
  — "black box gaslighting": platforms leverage their epistemic authority over their own algorithms to
  undermine users' confidence in what they know and to destabilise credible criticism. The chapter's claim
  at lines 347–354 that opacity is a rent-preserving strategy has an empirical name and a citation.
- Related and citable: Cotter (2018), "Playing the visibility game," *New Media & Society*; Cotter (2022),
  "Practical knowledge of algorithms: The case of BreadTube," *New Media & Society*; Duffy et al. (2022),
  "Platform governance at the margins," *Media, Culture & Society*; Meng (2024), "Cross-platform sensitivity
  and algorithmic adaptability," *New Media & Society*. (Titles and venues from Consensus records; **DOIs
  not verified** — look each up before citing.)

**(c) Public-interest algorithm auditing.** Metaxa et al. (2021), *Foundations and Trends in HCI* 14(4),
272–344, DOI 10.1561/1100000083 — the methodological synthesis of studying opaque systems "from the outside
in." Auditing is a professionalising mechanism, not a mass one: it builds the competence in a research and
NGO class and then hands the *findings* to everyone else. That is a priesthood, which is the outcome the
chapter's conclusion names as the bad case.

**(d) Regulatory-mandated competence.** Two live examples, and they are the strongest material the chapter
has for an affirmative answer:

- **AI Act Article 4** (applicable since 2 February 2025) obliges providers and deployers to ensure a
  sufficient level of AI literacy among their staff. This is a compulsory, employer-borne competence
  mandate — structurally the closest thing to compulsory schooling that European digital law contains.
  **PARTIAL: verify the verbatim text on EUR-Lex before quoting.**
- **Platform Work Directive Articles 6(d), 13(3) and 25.** Art 6(d) obliges member states to "provide for
  appropriate training for national competent authorities and provide for the availability of technical
  expertise **in the field of algorithmic management**." Art 13(3) entitles workers' representatives to an
  expert of their choice, **at the platform's expense** above 250 workers. Art 25 obliges member states to
  encourage collective bargaining "to facilitate the exercise of their rights related to algorithmic
  management." All VERIFIED, §1.2. These are *state-financed and employer-financed competence transfers to
  the coordinated side*, and they are collectively rather than individually addressed — which is precisely
  the mechanism the chapter's conclusion asks after, and precisely the respect in which they differ from
  the market mechanism Bishop and MacDonald document.

### 4.4 Verdict: what the chapter should do

**Keep the question, drop the romance, and name the mechanism.** Specifically:

1. **The chapter's implied causal order in §10 is contestable and should be softened.** "Democratic
   sovereignty followed the diffusion of the competence that made it possible" reads as literacy → democracy.
   Paglayan's evidence is that compulsory schooling followed *unrest*, and served order; Graff's is that the
   emancipatory reading is ideology. Rewrite as a claim about what became *possible*, not what followed.
2. **Name the mechanism, because the chapter's own argument needs it.** Literacy diffused chiefly by
   state-compelled, publicly financed universal schooling of children over roughly a century and a half,
   supplemented by church-driven household instruction that in places preceded schooling (Fernihough et al.
   on Ireland; the Swedish household-examination tradition). Vernacular printing and the Reformation created
   demand; the state supplied compulsion and finance.
3. **State the disanalogy honestly, and then use it.** There is no compulsory universal mechanism for
   algorithmacy, and the mechanism actually operating is a commercial expertise market that Bishop and
   MacDonald show diffuses compliance with the platform's objectives rather than standing against them.
   That is not a reason to drop the question. It is the chapter's own legibility/standing distinction
   recurring one level down, and it converts a rhetorical closing question into a specific empirical claim:
   **market-supplied algorithmacy reproduces co-optation; only publicly or collectively financed
   algorithmacy could distribute standing.**
4. **Point at the two live instruments** (AI Act Art 4; PWD Arts 6(d) and 13(3)) as the first regulatory
   attempts at compelled competence transfer, and make their evaluation a research direction in §9. This
   gives §9 a fifth direction that is concrete, dated, and testable, and it closes the mechanism gap without
   a claim the chapter cannot support.

## 5. THREAT-HUNT — reading §8–§10 as a hostile law-and-governance referee

### 5.1 The headline attack: the standing/legibility distinction does not survive contact with real instruments

The chapter's sharpest normative claim is at lines 661–664 and 730–737: a provision that creates standing
builds coordinative sovereignty; a provision that stops at legibility extends digital sovereignty and leaves
the coordination layer untouched. As a distinction between *provisions* this is clean and useful. As applied
to *instruments* in §8, it is falsified by the instruments the chapter itself picks.

**Exhibit A — the P2B Regulation.** Line 693–694 offers P2B as the paradigm legibility instrument: it "lets
a firm see how it ranks without letting it contest the ranking." Article 5 does exactly that. But the same
Regulation contains Article 11 (mandatory internal complaint-handling for business users), Articles 12–13
(mandatory named mediators for out-of-court settlement), and **Article 14 (a right for representative
organisations and associations to bring court actions to stop non-compliance)**. That is case-level
contestation plus collective standing, in the 2019 instrument, five years before the chapter's story says
standing arrives. A competition or platform-regulation referee will know Article 14 and will read the
chapter's characterisation as either careless or tendentious.

**Exhibit B — the DSA.** Line 659 treats Art 40 vetted-researcher access as legibility, then lines 658–661
concede it "begins to create channels … by parties with some standing to act." Both cannot be the settled
reading. And Delegated Regulation 2025/2050 gives the applicant a procedure, a decision deadline, and a
route through a public authority. Beyond Art 40, the DSA contains standing provisions of exactly the kind
the chapter says European law omits:

- **Art 20 *Internal complaint-handling system*** — case-level contestation against platform decisions
- **Art 21 *Out-of-court dispute settlement*** — recipients "shall be entitled to select any out-of-court
  dispute settlement body that has been certified in accordance with paragraph 3 of this Article in order
  to resolve disputes relating to those decisions, including complaints that have not been resolved by
  means of the internal complaint-handling system referred to in that Article"
- **Art 86 *Representation*** — "Recipients of intermediary services shall at least have the right to
  mandate a body, organisation or association to exercise the rights conferred by this Regulation on their
  behalf," with providers obliged to process such collective complaints "with priority and without undue
  delay"

**Verification caveat.** The EUR-Lex HTML of the DSA truncates before Article 20 under automated fetch, so
Arts 21 and 86 were read off the third-party mirror eu-digital-services-act.com (both pages fetched
directly). Substance and headings are standard, but re-read them on EUR-Lex before quoting.

Article 86 is the hardest single fact against a strong reading of the chapter's thesis: a general right of
collective representation against platforms, sitting inside the instrument the chapter treats as the
paradigm of legibility without standing.

**Exhibit C — the DMA fine itself.** The chapter offers the €500m Apple fine as an instance of regulation
reaching conduct without conferring standing. But the DMA's enforcement architecture is what gave the
developer side its leverage; the case originated in complaints, and the remedy is being administered as an
ongoing supervisory relationship. Meanwhile DMA.100204 (19 March 2025) regulates the *procedure* by which
Apple must receive, answer, and internally review third-party interoperability requests — a purely
standing-type provision inside an instrument the chapter classifies as exit-restoring.

**The repair, and it strengthens the chapter.** Restate the distinction as a property of **provisions**, not
of instruments, and say that real instruments are bundles. Then the diagnostic does more work, not less: it
sorts the provisions inside an instrument, tells you which of them bite on necessary functions and which on
contingent ones, and explains why a firm can be fully covered by an instrument and still hold no standing —
because the provisions that would give it standing (P2B Art 14, DSA Art 86) are collective and it has not
organised, while the provisions it actually uses (P2B Art 5, DSA recommender transparency) are individual
and informational. That is a better, more surprising, and more defensible claim than the one in the draft.

### 5.2 Secondary attacks a referee will make

**On §8:**

1. **Tense.** Line 696's "now benefits from" is false; see §1.2. A referee who checks one fact will check
   this one, because the transposition deadline post-dates submission.
2. **The collective-bargaining claim** (line 699–700) is sound — Article 25 — but the chapter should cite
   the article, because a labour-law referee will otherwise assume it has been asserted from a press
   summary. Quoting Art 25's closing clause ("to facilitate the exercise of their rights related to
   algorithmic management set out in Chapter III") converts the weakest-looking sentence in §8 into the
   strongest.
3. **Staleness.** A chapter submitted August 2026 that cites only the April 2025 Apple fine, and does not
   know about the €890m Google decisions of 23 July 2026 (including a €430m anti-steering fine), the
   General Court's 8 July 2026 dismissal of Apple's designation challenge, or **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744
   postponing the AI Act's high-risk obligations**, reads as written in 2025.
4. **The successor-instruments prediction** (line 738) now has an answer, and it is worse than the chapter
   expects. The successor instruments are the two Digital Omnibus proposals, whose declared purpose is
   *simplification*, and one of which proposes to **repeal the P2B Regulation** — the very instrument the
   chapter uses as its example of ranking transparency. The chapter can either not know this, or turn it
   into its best policy point: the drift of European digital law in 2025–26 is toward reducing legibility
   obligations, which leaves the standing question entirely unaddressed.
5. **The unnamed examples.** "State-integrated national platforms" and "a member-governed mobility
   cooperative" (lines 747–751) will read as evasion. Either name them with citations or drop the sentence.
   A governance referee will also note that the two cells the chapter fills are the ones its thesis predicts,
   and will ask for the case that would falsify it — a high-digital-sovereignty, high-coordinative-sovereignty
   platform, or a state-controlled platform where users *do* hold standing (public-service broadcasting's
   audience councils are the obvious candidate and would be a good addition).
6. **"Compliance investment does not address it"** (line 712) is asserted, not shown. The chapter's own
   evidence — that P2B and the DSA contain contestation channels — cuts against it. Weaken to: compliance
   investment is aimed at the wrong provisions.

**On §9:**

7. **The discriminant-validity target is too easy.** Spreitzer's (1995) psychological empowerment is an
   intra-organisational, psychological, four-dimensional self-report about one's own work role. Coordinative
   sovereignty is a relational and institutional property of an external, unexitable mediation. Showing they
   are distinct is trivial and therefore uninformative. A referee will name harder neighbours: organisational
   voice (Morrison), procedural and interactional justice (Colquitt), interorganisational dependence and
   power-asymmetry measures from the channel-relations tradition (Emerson; Heide & John), and psychological
   contract breach. Keep Spreitzer as the *anchor* for the response format and add a genuine rival.
8. **The two-step design may not be executable.** §9's third direction asks researchers to classify a
   mediator by modelling the coordination's rules. For a real platform those rules are the trade secret; the
   chapter's whole §3.2 argument is that they cannot be read. The design therefore runs on a *stipulated*
   rule set supplied by the analyst, and the classification inherits the analyst's stipulation. Concede this
   and say what stabilises the stipulation (published terms, worked cases, expert coding, sensitivity to
   plausible alternative rule sets). Left unaddressed it is the most damaging methodological objection
   available.
9. **The fourth direction has the same problem in reverse.** Correspondence between reported and afforded
   standing is presented as construct validation, but if the formal model's inputs are stipulated by the
   researcher, agreement between survey and model tests the stipulation, not the construct.
10. **Behavioural anchoring** (lines 770–773) — scoring the accuracy of an actor's predictions against the
    system's behaviour requires knowing the system's behaviour on counterfactual moves. That is an audit
    study, with all the access and legal problems Metaxa et al. (2021) document. Cite them and say so.

**On §10:**

11. **The diffusion analogy**, in full, per §4 above.
12. **"Computable"** (line 819) is a strong word for a diagnostic that reduces to a modelled counterfactual
    over a stipulated rule set. A formal-methods-literate referee will read "computable" as a claim about
    decidability and complexity. Consider "checkable" or "decidable given the coordination's rules."
13. **"Constitutive priesthood"** (line 832) is doing rhetorical work the evidence supports (Bishop 2020;
    MacDonald 2023; Metaxa et al. 2021 on auditing as professional practice). Ground it in those and it
    survives; leave it ungrounded and it reads as a flourish.

### 5.3 The one thing that would most improve §8–§10

Replace the instrument-level sorting with a provision-level table. Rows: P2B Arts 5 / 11 / 12 / 14; DSA
recommender transparency / Art 40 + DelReg 2025/2050 / Arts 20–21 / Art 86; DMA Art 5(4) / Art 6(7) +
DMA.100204; GDPR Art 22; PWD Arts 9 / 11 / 13. Columns: legibility or standing, individual or collective,
and which mediator type (necessary or contingent) it bites on. The table would be original, it would be
defensible against every attack in §5.1, and it would be the artefact readers cite.

### One error was made and caught — worth recording

The first pass through Directive 2024/2831 concluded that **no collective-bargaining obligation existed in
the enacting terms**, and would have told the author to delete a sentence that is in fact correct. The cause
was mechanical: EUR-Lex's HTML rendering of this Directive truncates around Article 22 under automated
fetch, and a keyword search run over that truncated text found nothing. **Article 25 exists.** The error was
caught only because a second, independent verification pass read the same instrument through a different
channel and disagreed, after which the Official Journal PDF settled it.

The operational lesson for the rest of this chapter's fact-checking: **a negative finding from a single
automated fetch of a long EU instrument is not evidence of absence.** Confirm negatives against the OJ PDF,
which paginates completely, before acting on them.
