# Adversarial pass 1 — verdicts on the load-bearing claims

Three deep-research investigations, each tasked with refuting its claim and locating the strongest critics.
Findings and the wording the chapter must adopt to survive review. New sources are added to the chapter's
references and the required revisions are applied to `chapter/chapter.md`.

## H5 — co-optation as a fourth coordination mechanism → REFRAME (does not survive as stated)

The strongest and most consequential result. Stark and Vanden Broeck (2024) write that "on platforms they are
co-opted," but they describe a distinctive organizational topology and management practice, not a fourth
coordination logic in Powell's (1990) sense. Powell's three mechanisms are logics — price, authority, trust —
and platforms coordinate through algorithmic versions of those same logics (algorithmic pricing, algorithmic
command, algorithmic reputation). Reviewers who know Powell (1990), Williamson, Adler (2001), and Thompson
(2003) will reject "a distinct fourth coordination mechanism" as a category error, and three lines of critique
are ready to hand: algorithmic management as digital Taylorism, i.e. hierarchy in new clothes (Kellogg et al.,
2020; Rahman, 2021); platforms as hybrids of market and hierarchy, not a distinct type (Vallas & Schor, 2020;
Robinson et al., 2026); and the observation that markets have always been mediated by brokers, dealers, and
exchanges, so a mediated triad is not itself new.

**Verdict.** Do not claim a fourth coordination *logic*. Separate three things the draft ran together: the
coordination logic (price/authority/trust — not new), the organizational topology (a mediated triad — ancient),
and the *governance challenge* (coordination through an opaque, interested, algorithmically adaptive mediator —
genuinely new). The chapter's contribution is the third, and algorithmacy and coordinative sovereignty are its
answer. Attribute "co-opted" to Stark and Vanden Broeck as their term while making the analytic point in the
chapter's own voice. Turn the "mediation is ancient" objection into a strength: mediation is old, but opacity
plus an interested, adaptive mediator is what the classical mechanisms do not address.

**New sources:** Powell (1990); Vallas & Schor (2020); Robinson et al. (2026); Kellogg et al. (2020, already
cited); Selznick (1949) for the origin of co-optation and the caution that platform enrollment lacks the
incorporation Selznick's term implies.

## H1 — sovereignty paired with a communicative sensibility → SURVIVES, soften to co-constitution

The pairing has a strong lineage: Innis (1951) on the bias of communication, Eisenstein (1979) on print and the
state, Anderson (1983/2006) on print-capitalism and the nation. The risk is technological determinism, met by
the objection that authority also selects its media (reverse causality) and that media coexist. The theory
absorbs this if stated as co-constitution rather than one-way causation.

**Verdict.** Survives. State the relation as co-constitutive — a medium enables a scale of coordination and a
form of authority, and an authority institutionalizes the media that amplify it — and cite Innis, Eisenstein,
Anderson. Avoid any claim that the medium determines the polity.

## H2 — the transitions are changes in kind → SURVIVES, pin to coordination not cognition

The "change in kind" claim has its pedigree in Ong (1982), Goody (1977), and Havelock, but the strong cognitive
version — that literacy restructures the mind — is refuted by Street (1984), whose ideological model holds that
literacy's effects are socially embedded, and by Scribner and Cole (1981), whose empirical study found literacy
effects to be practice-specific rather than general cognitive change. A reviewer will raise these against any
unqualified "change in kind."

**Verdict.** Survives if pinned to coordination infrastructure rather than cognition. The draft already frames
the change as one of coordination, which is the correct survival strategy; make it explicit and cite Street and
Scribner and Cole, conceding that cognitive effects are practice-specific while maintaining that each
sensibility enables a qualitatively distinct coordination regime a prior order structurally cannot instantiate.

**New sources:** Innis (1951); Eisenstein (1979); Ong (1982); Goody (1977); Street (1984); Scribner & Cole (1981).

## H10 — algorithmacy is distinct in kind from digital literacy → NEEDS REVISION

There is a developed literature the draft ignores: algorithmic literacy and critical data literacy
(Oeldorf-Hirsch et al., 2021; Dogruel et al., 2021; Pangrazio & Selwyn, 2019; Sander, 2020). A reviewer in
media or information studies will say algorithmacy is algorithmic literacy renamed. The defensible distinction
is not that algorithmacy is a wholly new competence but that it is a specific one the literacy literature
underdevelops: acting strategically from *inside* an opaque, interested, triadic coordination, as against
understanding algorithms from *outside*. Möhlmann and colleagues' (2023) algorithm sensemaking is the inside
competence observed in the field.

**Verdict.** Revise. Engage the algorithmic-literacy literature explicitly, position algorithmacy as the
practiced competence to model and act from within opaque triadic systems — algorithmic literacy in action under
opacity — and stake the distinction on the from-within/from-outside and the triadic-coordination structure, not
on a bare claim of a new kind.

**New sources:** Oeldorf-Hirsch et al. (2021); Dogruel et al. (2021); Pangrazio & Selwyn (2019); Sander (2020).

## H13 — coordinative sovereignty is novel → SURVIVES with explicit positioning

The term is unused in the literature, but the conceptual neighborhood is crowded and getting more so, and
Repetto (2025) has named the danger directly as "concept stretching" in the digital-sovereignty literature. The
chapter must position against the nearest neighbors rather than assume distance from them: Bannerman's (2024)
relational sovereignty (which centers power asymmetry and structural change), Muldoon and Raekstad's (2022)
algorithmic non-domination (which centers reducing arbitrary power and often proposes exit via cooperatives),
Pasquale's (2023) functional sovereignty and Cohen's (2017) platform sovereign (which theorize the platform's
authority, not the coordinated actor's standing).

**Verdict.** Survives with positioning. Add explicit differentiation: coordinative sovereignty is actor-centric
standing that takes the mediation as necessary and asks after voice within it, where relational sovereignty
centers asymmetry, non-domination centers protection and often exit, and functional/platform sovereignty
theorize the mediator's power. Engage Repetto's concept-stretching worry and claim innovation, not stretching,
on the ground that the construct specifies a distinct object (voice within necessary mediation) with its own
diagnostic.

**New sources:** Repetto (2025).

## H12 — exit/voice maps onto contingent/necessary → SURVIVES as formalization, reframe as actionable

Hirschman has already been applied to platforms (Dasgupta et al., 2025, on platform worker voice; Smith et al.,
2021, on exit and sovereignty), so the exit/voice framing is not itself new. The formal mapping of exit onto
bypassability and voice onto necessity appears to be original, but a skeptic will call it obvious, noting that
Hirschman himself observed that monopoly restricts exit and elevates voice. The defensible contribution is not
the mapping but its consequence: contingency is a policy variable, so whether an actor's power is exit or voice
is not a natural fact but a designable one, and the necessary/contingent diagnostic tells a policymaker or
manager which lever is live.

**Verdict.** Survives as an original formalization with an actionable payoff. Cite Dasgupta et al. (2025) and
Smith et al. (2021) as prior exit/voice-on-platforms work, and pitch the contribution as the formal, computable,
policy-actionable version rather than as a new reading of Hirschman.

**New sources:** Dasgupta et al. (2025); Smith et al. (2021).

## Net effect on the chapter

None of the claims collapses, and two are strengthened by the critique. H5 is reframed from a contested "fourth
mechanism" to the defensible and sharper "governance challenge of opaque interested mediation," which is
actually the chapter's real point. H10 is repositioned within the algorithmic-literacy literature it had
ignored. H1, H2, H12, and H13 are qualified and armed with the sources a reviewer would otherwise raise against
them. The revisions and the new references are applied to `chapter/chapter.md`.
