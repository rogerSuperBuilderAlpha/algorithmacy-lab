# At Home in the Other

*Roger Hunt · draft v1 (lab-generated from the plan and grounding maps; for the author to revise in his own
voice before anything posts). Sourcing note at the end flags what needs book confirmation.*

---

There is a picture of freedom that says: leave. Whatever holds you, walk away from it, and the walking away
is the freedom. A job that makes demands, a town that expects things, a marriage that asks you to change your
plans — each is a limit, and a free person is one who can always shed the limit and stand clear. Push the
picture to its edge and you get the person who owns nothing he cannot abandon by Friday, joins nothing he
cannot quit, loves no one he cannot leave, and calls the whole arrangement liberty. He is touched by nothing.
He also does nothing, builds nothing, and is no one in particular, because being someone in particular means
being held.

Hegel had a name for that freedom, and it was not a compliment. He called it the freedom of the void. My
claim in this post is that Hegel's alternative to it — freedom as being at home with oneself in the other,
*Beisichselbstsein im Anderen* — is the exact thesis my lab has been calling coordinative sovereignty, stated
a hundred and seventy years early in a grammar the lab does not have. Freedom, for Hegel, is not what you keep
by standing outside every whole. It is a quality you can only have *inside* one. And the coordination program
I build gives me one new thing to say about that claim, plus one hard line I have to hold or the whole reading
turns into propaganda for the platforms.

## The freedom of the void

Start with the freedom Hegel rejects, because the rejection is precise and the target is alive today. In the
Introduction to the *Philosophy of Right*, Hegel describes one side of the will: its power to abstract from
every content, to treat any determination it finds itself in as a mere limit and flee it.[^1] The will can
always step back from what it wants, what it has, what it is, and hold itself apart, pure and empty and
unbound. Hegel does not deny this is a real capacity. He denies it is freedom in any full sense, and he gives
it a hard name: "This is the freedom of the void, which rises to a passion."[^1]

The void is not an abstraction Hegel reaches for. He points straight at the Terror. When the flight from every
determination becomes a political program, he writes, it can only destroy — it tears down whatever structure
exists, suspects everyone attached to any order, and takes its own emptiness for purity.[^2] The fanatic of
absolute freedom cannot build an institution, because every institution is a determination, and determination
is the enemy. So the will that will not be held ends by leveling. Freedom as pure negation, freedom as the
permanent option to walk out, produces nothing it can live in.

The deeper point is in the *Phenomenology*, where Hegel traces "absolute freedom" straight into terror. A
freedom that recognizes no institution as its own, that treats every determinate arrangement as an alien
imposition on the general will, has nothing left to do but destroy — and its destruction, Hegel says, is the
coldest and flattest kind, "the most cold-blooded and meaningless death of all, with no more significance than
cleaving a head of cabbage or swallowing a draught of water."[^2a] The void does not build a better order. It
cannot, because building is determining, and it is at war with determination as such. So it clears the ground,
again and again, and calls the clearing freedom.

Set that beside the exit ideal in its modern dress and the fit is uncomfortable. The purest contemporary
version of the freedom of the void is the fantasy of exit as such: the seasteader who will float beyond every
jurisdiction, the crypto-libertarian whose whole political theory is the right to leave, the executive who
frames every obligation as a constraint on optionality. Each treats attachment as a tax on freedom and
detachment as its fulfillment. Hegel's verdict lands on all of them. A freedom whose content is only the power
to sever is a freedom you cannot actually inhabit, because the moment you commit to anything — a craft, a
city, another person — you have accepted a determination, and by the void's own standard you are less free for
it. The void has to keep leaving. That is the whole of what it can do.

Hegel's move is to deny that determination and freedom are opposites at all. The will is not most free when it
is emptiest. It is most free when it gives itself a content it can recognize as its own, and stays with the
content — "wills itself," in his phrase, so that in the determination it is still with itself rather than lost
in it.[^3] Freedom is not the absence of a limit. It is a certain relation to a limit: one where the limit is
yours, and holding it is how you are you.

## Being at home in the other

The relation has a name, and it is the most compressed thing Hegel ever said about freedom. In the
*Encyclopaedia Logic*, defining the free will, he writes that "freedom is just this: to be at home with
oneself in one's other, to depend upon oneself, to be one's own determinant" — *in seinem Anderen bei sich
selbst zu sein*.[^4] And, a line on: freedom is present only where there is no other confronting me that I
myself am not.[^5] Read those slowly. Freedom is a particular way of standing toward an other — being with
yourself *in* it, so that what looks from outside like a limit is, from inside, an extension of you. It is not
the absence of an other; it is the right relation to one.

This is deliberately paradoxical, and the paradox is the content. Ordinarily we think dependence and freedom
trade off: the more I am bound up with someone or something outside me, the less free I am, because now my
state turns on something I do not control. Hegel says that at a low level this is right and at the highest
level it inverts. There is a kind of other in which I am not diminished but completed, where depending on it is
not a subtraction from my agency but its exercise. In *that* other I am at home. The German idiom is exact:
*bei sich* is being-at-home, the way you are in your own house, unguarded, yourself. Freedom is being at home
while not being alone.

Hegel's cases for this are not metaphysical. They are friendship and love. In the Addition to §7 he puts it
almost conversationally: in friendship and love a person is not one-sided but "limits himself with pleasure in
relation to an other," and precisely in this self-limitation "knows himself as himself."[^6] The friend who
reorganizes his week around yours has not lost a slice of his freedom to you. He is doing something he could
not do alone — being a friend — and the limit is the medium of it, not the cost. In the account of the family
Hegel is blunter still: love, he writes, is the consciousness of my unity with another, in which I am not
isolated for myself but win my self-consciousness only as the giving-up of my independence, and know myself as
the unity of myself with the other.[^7] You do not enter a marriage and keep your freedom in reserve, ready to
exit. You become someone whose freedom now runs through the marriage. The independence you gave up was the
lower thing. What you got was a self you could not have had alone.

Hold the structure of the claim apart from its warm examples, because it is going to have to do cold work
later. Being-at-home-in-the-other has two components, and they come apart. One is objective: you are genuinely
in an other, bound to it, not standing clear — leaving would cost you something you are. The other is
subjective: you recognize the bond as your own, will it, are at home in it rather than trapped by it. Hegel's
friend and spouse have both. That is why they are his examples of freedom and not of captivity. Keep the two
components in view. Almost everything hard about applying this to the wholes my lab studies is that the first
can hold while the second fails.

## Freedom lives in institutions, or it does not live

Hegel does not leave freedom in the private warmth of love. His largest claim is that freedom is real only
when it is built — carried by institutions that outlast any individual's mood, not felt as an inner state. The
*Philosophy of Right* is the argument that a will is actually free only when it lives inside a family, a civil
society, and a state whose arrangements give its freedom a standing shape. Alan Patten put the point cleanly
in *Hegel's Idea of Freedom*: for Hegel freedom is not a condition of the solitary inner self but something
realized and sustained through participation in rational institutions.[^8] You are not free first, privately,
and then optionally social. You become free by being a member of the right kind of whole.

Which whole is the right kind is Hegel's whole question, and his answer at the level of the state is a formula
worth setting beside my own work. The state, he writes at §260, is the actuality of concrete freedom, and
concrete freedom consists in this: that personal individuality and its particular interests reach their full
development and win recognition of their right, and at the same time pass over of their own accord into the
interest of the universal, knowing and willing it as their own.[^9] Read past the architecture and the
condition is a demand on the institution. A whole realizes freedom only if the members can both pursue their
own ends *and* find themselves in the whole's ends — not sacrificed to it, not merely useful to it, but at
home in it. The universal has to be something they recognize as theirs.

Frederick Neuhouser drew the standard out of Hegel and made it a test, and his version is the one I have to
reckon with most carefully, because it is a rival to mine. In *Foundations of Hegel's Social Theory*,
Neuhouser reconstructs Hegel's account of "social freedom" as the criterion by which the family, civil
society, and the state count as rational at all. An institution is rational, on his reading, when it meets two
conditions together: it must actually furnish the objective preconditions of its members' freedom, and its
members must subjectively affirm it — grasp it as their own, be at home in it.[^10] Both, or the institution
fails the test. Neuhouser's Hegel does not certify a whole as free because it is powerful, or because it is
irreducible, or because everyone in fact depends on it. He certifies it only if the members can recognize
themselves in it and would, understanding it, endorse it.

That two-part standard is where Hegel's freedom and my instrument are going to meet, and it is also where they
are going to fail to be the same thing. Neuhouser's first condition is objective and structural; it is the
kind of thing you might hope to measure. His second is attitudinal, a fact about whether members affirm the
whole, and no measurement of causal structure will ever return it. File that. It is the honest limit of the
whole reading, and I will come back to it once the bridge is built.

## Coordinative sovereignty is this claim in the lab's grammar

Here is why I care, beyond the pleasure of reading Hegel. For a couple of years my lab has been working out a
concept we call coordinative sovereignty, and it is *Beisichselbstsein im Anderen* rebuilt for a
platform-mediated world, without our having noticed at first that Hegel got there.

The setup is this. More and more of what people do runs through mediators they cannot leave — the platform
that is the labor market for a driver, the app store that is the only road to a billion phones, the payment
rail every merchant clears through. These are not conveniences you take or leave. They are *necessary*
mediators: remove one and the coordination it carries does not route around it, it collapses. And here the old
picture of freedom, the freedom of independence, of standing clear, of exit, does exactly what Hegel said the
freedom of the void does. It fails. Telling a gig driver that his freedom lies in his right to quit the
platform is telling him his freedom lies in leaving the only market there is. Independence, demanded of a
necessary mediation, leaves you not sovereign but stranded. The exit is real and it is worth nothing.

So the coordinative-sovereignty claim is: in an irreducible coordination, freedom cannot mean getting out. It
has to mean something you have *while in*. Standing. Effective voice. A share in how the terms get set. The
sovereignty available to someone who cannot leave a whole is not the sovereignty of the person outside every
whole — that person is a fiction — but the sovereignty of a member who has weight inside the one he is in.
That is coordinative sovereignty, and stated that way it is word for word Hegel's turn against the void.
Freedom is not standing outside the other. It is being at home in it — and being at home, for someone bound
into a coordination he cannot exit, means having a voice in it he can recognize as his own.

The reason the lab reached this by a different road is that it came in through causation, not recognition. My
instrument does not ask whether members affirm a whole. It asks whether the whole is one at all: take the
coordination, partition it any way you like, and check whether some cut leaves it doing everything it did. If
a member can be severed and the coordination goes on unchanged, that member was a neighbor, and his freedom
can still be the freedom of exit — he can leave and lose nothing the arrangement needed. If no cut severs him
cleanly, if the coordination's own doing runs through him and would not survive his removal, then he is a
member in the strong sense, exit is a fiction for him, and the only freedom left to ask about is freedom of
the kind Hegel described: at home, or not, in a whole he is inside for good.

## Exit and voice, made causal

This gives me one genuinely new thing to say, and I want to mark it as new because I checked. Albert
Hirschman, in *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*, gave the twentieth century its cleanest vocabulary for what a member
can do about a whole that is failing him: he can exit — leave, take his business elsewhere — or he can use
voice — stay and complain, agitate, push to change it from inside. Loyalty is what holds him in long enough
to use voice instead of bolting.[^11] Hirschman's dichotomy has been mapped onto almost everything. It has
never, as far as a hard corpus search can tell, been mapped onto Hegel's two freedoms — the freedom of the
void and concrete freedom sit in one literature and exit/voice sit in another, and the two do not touch.[^12]
The mapping is unmade. I am going to make it, and make it in the one form that keeps it from collapsing into a
truism.

The form is causal. Exit and voice are not, at bottom, two attitudes or two values. They are two structural
situations, and my partition test is exactly what tells them apart. Exit is available precisely when a member
is *severable* — when the coordination survives cutting him loose, which in the lab's terms is when his
coupling to the whole is low, when the whole's cause-and-effect structure does not run through him. Voice is
what remains when he is *not* severable — when the coupling is high, when no partition leaves the coordination
intact without him, so that leaving is either impossible or self-destroying and the only leverage he has is
from inside. Loyalty, in this reading, is not sentiment. It is whatever holds the coupling intact — the switching
costs, the shared history, the sunk investment, the norm — the thing that keeps the member in the coordination
long enough that voice is his live option and exit is not.

Two cases make the difference concrete. A shopper at one of five competing grocery stores in town is
severable: she can walk out, the store loses one basket it will not miss, and she buys the same milk down the
road. Cut her from that store and nothing the store does changes; cut the store from her and nothing she does
changes. Her coupling is low, and her freedom really is the freedom of exit — the threat to leave is what
gives her whatever leverage she has, and it is a real threat. Now the driver whose entire livelihood clears
through one ride-hail app. He is not severable. The app is the market; his leaving subtracts one interchangeable
worker from a pool built to lose them, and it subtracts his whole income from him. Cut him from the platform
and the platform runs on; cut the platform from him and he has no work. The coupling is high in the one
direction that matters, and his exit is a threat to no one but himself. Same word, "leave," and it names a
freedom for the shopper and a fiction for the driver, and the partition test is what tells you which.

Now lay Hegel over it. The freedom of the void is the freedom of exit absolutized: the standing option to
sever, treated as freedom itself. It works only for the severable — the neighbor, the low-coupling member, the
customer who really can take his business elsewhere and cost the seller nothing he cannot replace. Concrete
freedom, being-at-home-in-the-other, is the freedom of voice: the freedom of a member so bound into a whole
that exit is empty, whose freedom therefore has to be found *in* the coordination or nowhere. Hegel's two
freedoms are Hirschman's two responses, and the partition test says which one a given member actually has —
not by consulting his preferences but by asking whether the whole he is in would survive losing him. The
driver on the platform is not free-by-exit because he is not severable; the platform is the market, and his
leaving changes nothing for it while ending everything for him. His only available freedom is voice. That is
not a moral observation. It is a fact about the coupling, and the instrument can read it.

I put the mapping this concretely because there is a loose version of it that says nothing, and I want to
stand clear of it. The loose version notes that Hegel has a negative freedom and a positive freedom, Hirschman
has exit and voice, negative goes with exit and positive with voice, and there — a tidy correspondence, and
empty, because it is just Isaiah Berlin's two liberties wearing a second coat.

## Why this is not just Berlin again

The deflation is worth meeting head-on. Berlin's "Two Concepts of Liberty" already split freedom into a
negative kind — the absence of interference, being left alone — and a positive kind — self-mastery,
self-direction, being one's own master. It is the most famous distinction in the field, and Hegel's void and
concrete freedom slot into it so neatly that a reader could reasonably say I have discovered a synonym. If the
exit/voice mapping is only Berlin restated, it carries no weight, and worse, it hands the whole thing back to
the normative camp, because Berlin's distinction is a distinction of *values* — two things worth wanting, to
be weighed against each other — and the coordination program is supposed to be doing something the normative
readings cannot.

What keeps it from being Berlin is that the axis is not a value axis. Berlin sorts freedoms by what they are
worth and warns that the positive kind, self-mastery, has a bad habit of licensing coercion in freedom's name.
That is an argument about which freedom to prize. My exit/voice split sorts members by a structural fact about
the coordination they are in: whether a partition severs them cheaply or not at all. It does not say voice is
nobler than exit, or the reverse. It says exit is *available* to the severable member and *unavailable* to the
non-severable one, as a matter of the wiring, before anyone decides what to prize. A libertarian who values
exit above everything and a communitarian who values voice above everything face the same fact when they look
at a gig driver: he is not severable, so the exit both of them are arguing about is, for him, not on the
table. The partition test is upstream of the value quarrel. It tells you which freedoms a given member can
actually have, and leaves the ranking to the reader.

So the mapping earns its keep by being causal or it is worth nothing. Made causally, it says something Berlin
does not: that whether you get the negative freedom or are thrown back on the positive one is not a choice or
a temperament but a structural property of the whole you are enmeshed in, readable off its cause-and-effect
structure. Made loosely, it is a synonym for a distinction philosophy has had since 1958. The lab's
contribution is the *first* version, and only the first.

## The line I have to hold

Now the danger, and it is the reason this post could go badly wrong. Everything so far tempts a conclusion
that is false and flattering: that if the coordination program can find the irreducible wholes — the ones no
partition severs — it has found the wholes where members are at home, the Hegelian wholes, the free ones. That
would be wonderful for the platforms and it is not true. A whole can be irreducible and estranging. High
coupling is not being-at-home; it is often the opposite.

Take the case that hurts. An Amazon warehouse or a gig platform at scale is, by my own test, deeply
irreducible for the worker inside it: he cannot be severed from it without losing his livelihood, the market
runs through it and not around it, no exit leaves him whole. He is a member in exactly the strong,
non-severable sense I have been building toward — and he is not at home. The coupling that makes exit
impossible is the same coupling that makes the arrangement a trap. He has Hegel's objective condition, the
being-genuinely-in-an-other, and he entirely lacks the subjective one, the recognizing-it-as-his-own. My
instrument reads the coupling. It cannot read the estrangement. It returns "irreducible" for the sweatshop and
the marriage alike, and the difference between them — the whole difference Hegel cared about — is invisible to
it.

Now put a worker cooperative beside the warehouse and watch the instrument fail to tell them apart. In a real
cooperative the members are just as non-severable — the enterprise is theirs, their livelihoods run through
it, walking out costs them what walking out costs the warehouse worker. By the partition test the two are
twins: both irreducible, both wholes no member can leave without loss. But one is governed by its members and
the other governs them, and that is the whole difference between a home and a cage. The co-op member has
voice built into the structure; the warehouse worker has a suggestion box. My instrument reads the coupling in
both and returns the same verdict, because the thing that separates them — who sets the terms, whether the
member can recognize the whole's ends as his own — is precisely what a partition of the cause-and-effect
structure does not see. The measure that cannot tell a co-op from a sweatshop is not measuring freedom. It is
measuring the closing of the exit, which is freedom's precondition and not freedom.

This is where Neuhouser's two-condition standard comes back to collect. His Hegel certifies an institution as
free only when it meets both conditions: it furnishes the objective preconditions of freedom *and* the members
affirm it as their own. My partition test measures something adjacent to the first condition and is flatly
silent on the second. It can tell you that a member is bound into a whole he cannot leave. It cannot tell you
whether the whole gives him his freedom or takes it — whether he is a spouse or a serf. And that second
question, for Hegel, is not a footnote to freedom. It *is* freedom. So a reviewer holding Neuhouser can say,
correctly, that the Φ criterion does not operationalize Hegel's freedom test; it operationalizes at most the
structural half, and the half it drops is the half that does the ethical work. I think that is right, and the
reading survives it only by conceding it out loud.

What survives the concession is still worth having, and I want to be exact about what it is, because the
temptation is to over-claim in either direction. The coordination program does not certify freedom and it does
not measure being-at-home. It does something narrower and prior: it locates, in the wiring, exactly the members
for whom the question of being-at-home becomes urgent and exit stops being an answer. Where the test returns
"severable," Hegel's void is a live option and the liberal story of freedom-as-exit still runs. Where it
returns "irreducible," the liberal story is finished, the driver is in the whole for good, and the only
freedom left to argue about is the Hegelian one — voice, standing, recognition within a coordination he cannot
leave. The instrument does not answer that argument. It tells you when you are in it. And that is not nothing,
because the loudest current answer to the gig driver is still "you're free, you can quit," and the partition
test is what shows that answer to be the freedom of the void — a real severance, available to no one who
matters, worth exactly what leaving the only market there is is worth.

Coordinative sovereignty, then, is not a property my instrument confers on the wholes it finds irreducible. It
is a demand made *on* them — the demand that a coordination no one can leave owe its members voice, since it
has taken their exit. Hegel grounds the demand in the rational content of the institution, in whether its
members can recognize themselves in it. I cannot measure that. What I can measure is when the demand comes due:
when the coupling has closed the exit and thrown the whole weight of freedom onto voice. Hold that line, and
the reading is a tool for finding where the political question lives. Drop it, and let irreducibility stand in
for being-at-home, and the same reading becomes a certificate of contentment for every whole strong enough to
trap the people inside it. The line is the difference between a diagnosis and an apology, and it runs exactly
where Hegel drew it: between being *in* an other and being *at home* in it.

## Freedom in the whole

Hegel's freedom is a quality of membership, not a distance from it. You are free not when you have escaped
every whole but when you are inside the right one and can recognize it as your own — at home in the other,
depending on it and thereby on yourself. The coordination program can now do one honest thing with that claim
and must refuse a second. It can read, off the causal structure, which members are severable and which are
bound in for good, and so tell exit apart from voice before anyone argues about which to prize. It cannot read
whether a member bound in for good is at home or trapped, and so it cannot, on its own, tell a Hegelian whole
from a well-built cage.

The next post keeps score on the whole series, and this is one of the entries it has to weigh: a genuine
bridge on the structural half of Hegel's freedom, a genuine silence on the ethical half, and, in between, one
small new thing — that exit and voice are not two values but two positions in a coordination, and that a
partition of its cause-and-effect structure is what sorts a member into one or the other. Hegel would have said
the sorting is only the beginning of the question of freedom. He would have been right. But it is the
beginning, and no one had the instrument to draw it before.
