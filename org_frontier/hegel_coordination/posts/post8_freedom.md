There is a picture of freedom that says: leave. Whatever holds you, walk away from it, and the walking away
is the freedom. A job that makes demands, a town that expects things, a marriage that asks you to change
your plans — each is a limit, and a free person is one who can always shed the limit and stand clear. Push
the picture to its edge and you get the person who owns nothing he cannot abandon by Friday, joins nothing
he cannot quit, loves no one he cannot leave, and calls the whole arrangement liberty. He is touched by
nothing. He also does nothing, builds nothing, and is no one in particular, because being someone in
particular means being held.

Hegel had a name for that freedom, and it was not a compliment: the freedom of the void. His alternative —
freedom as being at home with oneself in the other, *Beisichselbstsein im Anderen* — is the problem my lab
has been circling under the name coordinative sovereignty, the better part of two centuries before we had
the grammar to restate even its institutional half. Freedom, for Hegel, is not what you keep by standing
outside every whole. It is a quality you can only have inside one. What the coordination program adds is
narrow and, I will argue, real: it can read, off a coordination's causal structure, which members have been
stripped of the exit that the freedom-of-the-void story assumes — and it stops there, at the threshold of
the question Hegel actually cared about, which it cannot enter.

## The Freedom of the Void

Start with the freedom Hegel rejects, because the rejection is precise and the target is alive today. In the
*Philosophy of Right*, Hegel describes one side of the will: its power to abstract from every content, to
treat any determination it finds itself in as a mere limit and flee it. The will can always step back from
what it wants, what it has, what it is, and hold itself apart, pure and empty and unbound. Hegel does not
deny this is a real capacity — it is an essential moment of the free will, its universality and negativity.
He denies that it is freedom when it is made the *only* moment, and he names that: "the freedom of the
void" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §5, Remark) — a will that has turned its own emptiness into a program, taken on an
actual shape, and become a passion in its own right.

He means a real political actor, not an abstraction reached for from nowhere. When the flight from every
determination becomes a program, he writes, it can only destroy. He calls it "the fanaticism of destruction,
demolishing the whole existing social order, eliminating all individuals regarded as suspect by a given
order, and annihilating any organization which attempts to rise up anew" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §5, Remark, as
cited in Knowles, 2002). Dudley Knowles reads the passage as Hegel's diagnosis of a political pathology, not
a rhetorical flourish: a freedom with no content of its own has no way to build, only to clear, and clearing
looks like action right up until you ask what it produced (Knowles, 2002). The fanatic of absolute freedom
cannot build an institution, because every institution is a determination, and determination is the enemy.
The will that will not be held ends by leveling.

Hegel traces the same logic to its end in the *Phenomenology*, where absolute freedom runs straight into
terror. A freedom that recognizes no institution as its own, that treats every determinate arrangement as an
alien imposition on the general will, has nothing left to do but destroy. The sole work left to universal
freedom is death itself, a death empty of accomplishment, meaning nothing more than "chopping off a head of
cabbage or swallowing a mouthful of water" (Hegel, 1807/2018, ¶590). The void does not build a better order.
It cannot, because building is determining, and it is at war with determination as such. It clears the
ground, again and again, and calls the clearing freedom.

Set that beside the exit ideal in its modern dress and the fit is uncomfortable. The purest contemporary
version of the freedom of the void is the fantasy of exit as such: the seasteader who will float beyond
every jurisdiction, the crypto-libertarian whose whole political theory is the right to leave, the executive
who frames every obligation as a constraint on optionality. Each treats attachment as a tax on freedom and
detachment as its fulfillment. Hegel's verdict lands on all of them. A freedom whose content is only the
power to sever is a freedom you cannot inhabit, because the moment you commit to anything — a craft, a city,
another person — you have accepted a determination, and by the void's own standard you are less free for it.
The void has to keep leaving. That is the whole of what it can do.

Hegel's move is to deny that determination and freedom are opposites. The will is most free not when it is
emptiest but when it gives itself a content it can recognize as its own and stays with it — wills itself, so
that in the determination it is still with itself rather than lost in it (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §7). Freedom is
a relation to a limit, the relation where the limit is yours, and holding it is how you are you.

## Being at Home in the Other

The relation has a name, and it is Hegel's most compressed statement of the structure of freedom (see Roy,
2006, and Krijnen, 2019, on the phrase — Krijnen's chapter title alone states the claim: being at home with
oneself *in the whole*). In the *Encyclopaedia Logic*, Hegel names the structure directly: freedom is "to be
at home with oneself in one's other," dependent on itself, the determining factor for itself (Hegel,
1830/1991b, §24 Addition 2). He puts the limit case sharply: there is freedom only where nothing confronts
the self that is not also itself (Hegel, 1830/1991b, §24 Addition 2). Read those slowly. Freedom is a
particular way of standing toward an other — being with yourself in it, so that what looks from outside like
a limit is, from inside, an extension of you. It is the right relation to an other, not the absence of one.

Ordinarily dependence and freedom trade off: the more I am bound up with something outside me, the less
free I am, because now my state turns on what I do not control. Hegel says that at a low level this is right
and at the highest level it inverts. There is a kind of other in which I am not diminished but completed,
where depending on it is not a subtraction from my agency but its exercise. In that other I am at home. The
German idiom carries it: *bei sich* is being-at-home, the way you are in your own house, unguarded,
yourself. Freedom is being at home while not being alone.

Hegel reaches for the most ordinary cases: friendship and love. In the Addition to §7:

> But we already possess this freedom in the form of feeling, for example in friendship and love. Here, we
> are not one-sidedly within ourselves, but willingly limit ourselves with reference to an other, even while
> knowing ourselves in this limitation as ourselves. (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §7 Addition)

The friend who reorganizes his week around yours has not lost a slice of his freedom to you. He is doing
something he could not do alone — being a friend — and the limit is the medium of it. In the account of the
family Hegel presses harder: love is the consciousness of a unity with another so complete that
self-consciousness itself comes only through giving up independent existence and knowing oneself as one with
the other, and the other as one with oneself (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §158 Addition). Hegel makes the point in the
register of exit himself: marriage, he insists, is not a contract (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §§75, 163), precisely
because a contract binds separable persons who could walk away, and spouses are no longer separable in that
sense. The severability a partition test measures is the thing Hegel already knew marriage had left behind.

Hold the structure apart from the warm examples, because it does cold work later. Being-at-home-in-the-other
has two components — one objective, that you are genuinely in an other, bound to it, not standing clear; one
subjective, that you recognize the bond as your own and are at home in it rather than trapped by it. (The
two-part split is a reconstruction, Neuhouser's more than Hegel's own; Hegel states it as a unity.) For a
teleological reading of Hegel (Kreines, 2015; Ng, 2020), even the objective side is already purposive
activity, not inert structure, so the causal reading built below proxies only its threshold — the fact of
being bound — not the activity of purpose Hegel meant by it. The friend and the spouse have both — that is
what makes them cases of freedom. For Hegel the two halves come apart only in *defective* institutions, and
their coming-apart is what alienation *is* — being in the other without being at home in it. Almost
everything hard about applying this to the wholes my lab studies turns on the fact that the objective
component can hold while the subjective one fails, and that failure is not a gap in a measurement but the
presence of unfreedom.

## Freedom Lives in Institutions, or It Does Not Live

Hegel does not leave freedom in the private warmth of love. His largest claim is that freedom is real only
when it is built — carried by institutions that outlast any individual's mood, not felt as an inner state.
The *Philosophy of Right* argues that a will is actually free only when it lives inside a family, a civil
society, and a state whose arrangements give its freedom a standing shape. Patten (1999) put it cleanly: for
Hegel freedom is not a condition of the solitary inner self but something realized and sustained through
participation in rational institutions. Honneth (2014), reconstructing the same claim two centuries later
under the name social freedom, builds it explicitly on being-with-oneself-in-the-other and treats family,
market, and state as the historical answer to the question of what an institution has to look like to carry
it. Zabel (2015) reads the same institutional turn as Hegel's way past the individualism-versus-collectivism
choice that swallows most modern freedom talk: freedom belongs neither to the solitary self nor to the
collective as such, only to a self standing inside the right kind of whole. You do not become free first,
privately, and then optionally social. You become free by being a member of the right kind of whole.

Which whole is the right kind is Hegel's whole question, and his formula at the level of the state is worth
setting beside my own work. "The state is the actuality of concrete freedom" (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §260), and
concrete freedom consists, on Hegel's account, in personal individuality and its particular interests
reaching full development and winning recognition of their right, while at the same time passing over of
their own accord into the interest of the universal, known and willed as the individual's own. A whole
realizes freedom only if its members can both pursue their own ends and find their own reason in the whole's
ends — not sacrificed to it, not merely useful to it, but at home in it.

Neuhouser (2000) drew the standard out of Hegel and made it a test, and his version is the rival I have to
reckon with most carefully. He reconstructs Hegel's "social freedom" as the criterion by which the family,
civil society, and the state count as rational at all. An institution is rational, on his reading, when it
meets two conditions together: it must furnish the objective preconditions of its members' freedom, and its
members must be able to *recognize themselves* in it — to grasp its ends as rational and, understanding
them, endorse them as their own. Not that they happen to approve of it; that they could, seeing what it is,
be at home in it. Both, or the institution fails the test. Neuhouser's Hegel does not certify a whole as
free because it is powerful, or irreducible, or because everyone in fact depends on it.

That two-part standard is where Hegel's freedom and my instrument meet, and it is where they fail to be the
same thing. The first condition is objective and structural — the kind of thing you might hope to measure.
The second is recognitive: whether members can find their own reason in the whole's ends. It is not a
preference you could poll; it is a question about the whole's rational content and the members' warranted
recognition of it, and both lie past what a partition of a causal structure can see. That gap is where the
reading finally stops, and the co-op and the warehouse below are where it stops hardest.

## Coordinative Sovereignty and the Objective Half

Here is why I care, beyond the pleasure of reading Hegel. For a couple of years my lab has been working out
a concept we call coordinative sovereignty, and it answers the same problem *Beisichselbstsein im Anderen*
answers, for a platform-mediated world, though it reaches only one half of it. Coordinative sovereignty is
"the autonomy proper to being irreducibly coordinated — standing and effective voice within a mediation one
cannot leave, rather than independence from it or control over it" (coordinative_sovereignty/README.md). It
is deliberately not a claim about who owns the pipes: "Digital sovereignty asks who owns the infrastructure;
coordinative sovereignty asks what autonomy the actor coordinating through it can have"
(coordinative_sovereignty/README.md). Its currency is "voice, made effective — not the chance to speak, but
terms that hold because the coordinated actors have the standing to make them hold"
(coordinative_sovereignty/coordinative_sovereignty.md). Hold onto that word, *voice*: it is the
objective-institutional condition of being-at-home — a share in how the terms get set — and it is not yet
being-at-home itself. A member can have voice and be estranged, as a rabble with formal standing is
estranged; a member can be at home with little formal voice, as in the family. Voice is the form the demand
for the subjective half has to take once exit is foreclosed, not the subjective half achieved.

The setup is this. More and more of what people do runs through mediators they cannot walk away from — the
platform that is the labor market for a driver, the app store that is the only road to a billion phones, the
payment rail every merchant clears through. Some of these are necessary in the classifier's own sense:
nothing in them dissolves when a bypass is opened. Others are gates the classifier calls contingent, a bypass
real in the wiring, closed in fact by a monopoly, a network standard, or a law — the member who depends on
the gate is as stranded as if the mediator were structurally necessary. Either way, the old picture of
freedom — independence, standing clear, exit — does what Hegel said the freedom of the void does. It fails.
Telling a gig driver that his freedom lies in his right to quit the platform is telling him his freedom lies
in leaving a market a bypass could in principle restore him to and a monopoly forecloses in fact.
Independence, demanded where the exit is closed, leaves you not sovereign but stranded.

So the coordinative-sovereignty claim is: for a member of an irreducible coordination, the freedom worth
arguing about cannot be exit. It has to be something available while in — standing, effective voice, a share
in how the terms get set. That claim is the *objective-institutional* correlate of Hegel's turn against the
void: it says where being-at-home would have to be built, for someone who cannot leave, without pretending
the building is done. The program states the hinge in its own vocabulary — "exit where a gate is contingent,
voice where a mediator is necessary" (coordinative_sovereignty/README.md) — which is Paper 7's
necessary/contingent distinction, and it is the distinction this post now makes causal.

## Exit and Voice, Made Causal

The mapping is narrower than I first thought, and one piece of it is new. Hirschman (1970) gave the
twentieth century its cleanest vocabulary for what a member can do about a whole that is failing him: exit —
leave, take his business elsewhere — or voice — stay and agitate to change it from inside. Loyalty is what
holds him in long enough to use voice instead of bolting. Hirschman came to the vocabulary from an unlikely
direction: his own early formation touched the Hegelian philosophy of the family, though the 1970 book does
not cite Hegel and the two literatures have never crossed into each other's terms — recognition theorists
link Hegelian freedom to membership without going through Hirschman (Honneth, 2014), and Hirschman's
dichotomy migrates into democratic theory without crossing into Hegel's two freedoms. What has not been
done, so far as a search of the literature turns up, is the mapping of Hegel's freedom of the void and
concrete freedom onto exit and voice specifically, settled by an operation rather than an analogy.

The causal apparatus that tells exit from voice is not the partition test on its own — which grades whether a
coordination is a whole — but the bypass counterfactual laid on top of it, the operation Paper 7 was built
around: it grades whether the mediator a member routes through can be gone around. Both operations read exact
integrated information under IIT-4.0 (Albantakis et al., 2023, building on Oizumi et al., 2014). Exit is real
when the thing between a member and what he needs is a *contingent* gate: open the bypass and he reaches the
market directly, so leaving costs him nothing he cannot replace. Exit is a fiction when the mediator is
*necessary* — when no bypass restores a direct tie, because the mediator is the market and routing around it
routes to nowhere. A third pattern looks like the first on the wiring and behaves like the second in
practice: a gate the taxonomy calls contingent, its bypass real in principle, closed in fact by a monopoly, a
network standard, or a law. The classifier reads the wiring, not the closure. In any of the three patterns,
when leaving is impossible, self-destroying, or blocked by a fact the test does not compute, the only
leverage left is from inside. That is voice. Loyalty, in this reading, is not sentiment; it is whatever holds
the member in long enough that voice is his live option and exit is not — the switching costs, the shared
history, the sunk investment, the norm. The operation itself is real, and it runs on hand-built three-node
models. Nothing that follows about the gig driver is one of those models — he is a reading of the criterion
against a real case, not an output of it.

Two cases make the difference concrete, and they turn on the mediator, not the member. A shopper at one of
five competing grocery stores routes through a contingent gate with the bypass open: strike this store and
she reaches the same milk down the road, so her exit is real and the threat to leave gives her whatever
leverage she has. The driver whose whole livelihood clears through one ride-hail app routes through a gate
the platform's own dual-function reading also calls contingent. The app's real-time matching engine is the
necessary part, the internal function that does not dissolve when a tie is restored. The rider-driver gate he
personally depends on carries the whole margin instead, the way franchise law carries the car dealer's — the
entity does not classify, the function does. What separates the driver from the shopper is not the wiring; it
is that no second market exists for his bypass to open onto — the situation Muldoon and Raekstad (2023)
independently name algorithmic domination in the gig economy, arguing from republican political theory rather
than from causal structure. Notice what the causal reading does and does not say about the driver. The
platform runs on without him — one interchangeable worker out of a pool built to lose them — so on the causal
structure of the platform he is severable, a part the whole does not need. What forecloses his exit is not a
verdict the classifier returns on the gate; it is a fact the classifier does not read, that the market his
bypass would restore him to does not exist. Same word, "leave," and it names a freedom for the shopper and a
fiction for the driver — because her bypass opens onto a market and his opens onto nothing.

Lay Hegel over the pattern. The freedom of the void is the freedom of exit absolutized: the standing option
to sever, treated as freedom itself. It works only where a bypass is actually open — where leaving costs
nothing you cannot replace. Where the mediator is necessary, or where a contingent gate is closed in fact,
that freedom is gone, and the only freedom left to ask about is the concrete kind, being-at-home-in-the-other.
Its only available form is voice. Whether the member is actually at home in the coordination, or only trapped
in it, is the further question, and the bypass test does not reach it. What the test reads is a fact about
the mediation — Paper 7's necessary/contingent verdict, run on stylized models so far, not yet on any firm,
as the next section concedes.

## Why This Is Not Just Berlin — or Taylor

The mapping earns its keep only if it says more than the rival stories already on the shelf, and there are
two, not one. Isaiah Berlin (1958/1969) split freedom into a negative kind — the absence of interference —
and a positive kind — self-mastery. Hegel's void and concrete freedom slot into that pair naturally enough
that a reader could call this a synonym: exit looks like negative freedom, voice like positive freedom, and
the pairing suggests itself the moment you set the two vocabularies side by side. A targeted search finds no
one who has made that specific mapping before, which weakens the claim to be resolving a live dispute and
strengthens the claim to originality — but the point stands either way. If the exit/voice mapping is only
Berlin restated, it hands the whole thing back to the normative camp, because Berlin sorts two things worth
wanting, weighed against each other, and the program is supposed to do something the normative readings
cannot.

Robert Taylor (2017) is the sharper foil, because he does not just describe exit — he argues *for* it, as the
remedy for domination in the family, the workplace, and the state, on republican grounds. His book is called
*Exit Left* for a reason: the traditionally conservative-coded right to leave is, in his hands, the left's
best tool against arbitrary power. Taylor's argument needs exit to be available wherever domination is a
risk, which is everywhere a member depends on someone else's arbitrary will. The bypass test does not dispute
the value of exit where Taylor is right that it exists. It disputes that it exists everywhere he needs it to
— and the gig driver shows the dispute is not idle. The driver routes through a gate the catalog files as
contingent, a capture carrying the whole margin, the way franchise law rides the car dealer. What forecloses
the bypass is not the wiring of the matching engine but a fact the test does not compute: there is no second
market to reach. That is still a wall, but it is not the wall his remedy runs into — his remedy is aimed
exactly at what built it, the monopoly, not the mediation: antitrust, a rival platform, a public option,
anything that gives the bypass somewhere to go. Where his remedy would genuinely run out is the intrinsic
case, the integrator no restored edge removes, and this post has not shown the gig platform is that case.
Berlin's negative liberty fares worse: it can register that no one is stopping the driver from quitting and
still miss that quitting reaches nowhere, with no monopoly to indict, because it never asked why the bypass
is closed in the first place.

What keeps the mapping from being either of those is that the axis it sorts on is not a value axis. It is
computed. Berlin and Taylor sort freedoms, and remedies, by what they are worth and where they work. The
exit/voice split sorts members by whether the mediator they route through is contingent or necessary — the
bypass margin Paper 7 reads off the cause-and-effect structure. The classifier sorts the mediator into four
cells: a contingent capture carries the system's whole integration, a margin of 2.0; a partial capture
carries most of it, 1.585; an intrinsic mediator carries none of it as a debt to any removable constraint —
its margin is 0.0, because nothing in it dissolves when the bypass is opened; and a mediator that was never
in the core carries none of it either. For the exit/voice question specifically, only the poles matter — a
bypass exists or it does not — but the margin is there to be read at finer grain when the question calls for
it. It settles neither which freedom is nobler nor whether Taylor's remedy is right; it says exit is on the
table where a bypass is open and off it where the mediator is necessary or a contingent gate is closed by
capture, before anyone decides what to prize or which remedy to reach for. A libertarian who values exit
above everything, a communitarian who values voice above everything, and a republican who wants exit because
it blocks domination all face the same verdict on a gig driver: his gate is a capture with nowhere for the
bypass to go, so the exit all three are arguing about is, for him, not available. The reading sits upstream
of the value quarrel, an operation behind it rather than a relabeling.

## The Co-op and the Cage

Now the danger. Everything so far tempts a conclusion that is false and flattering: that if the program can
find the wholes no partition severs, it has found the wholes where members are at home, the free ones. That
would be wonderful for the platforms and it is not true. A whole can be irreducible and estranging, and the
closing of an exit is not being-at-home; it is often the opposite.

Take the case that hurts, and read it carefully, because the naive reading gets even the structure wrong.
An Amazon warehouse worker is bound the way the driver is bound, and for the same reason — not because the
operation could not run without him, since it replaces him by the next shift, but because the operation is
the only market for his labor and no bypass reaches around it in fact. His exit ends everything for him and
nothing for it. He is a member in the strong sense the earlier papers name, inside a coordination he cannot
leave. He is bound in the whole and not at home in it, and that gap — the being-in without the being-at-home
— is, in Hegel's own terms, alienation itself, not a missing datum. The instrument reads the mediation and
returns "no bypass." It cannot read the estrangement. It would return the same verdict for the warehouse and
for a marriage, and the difference between them — the whole difference Hegel cared about — is nothing a
partition sees.

Put a courier-member of a platform cooperative like CoopCycle beside the warehouse worker and the instrument
fails to tell them apart. In a real cooperative the members are bound as tightly: the enterprise is theirs,
their livelihoods run through it, walking out costs them what it costs the warehouse worker. On the criterion
the two would read as twins — the same closed exit, the same full-margin capture. But one is governed by its
members and the other governs them, and that is the difference between an institution that *could* be a home
and one that structurally cannot. Christiaens (2025) reaches the same practical conclusion from a different
direction, arguing that exit-enhancement and unionization both under-deliver for gig workers and defending
worker-owned cooperative platforms, on republican non-domination grounds, as the remedy Cole's guild
socialism already had a name for. He gets there without Hegel and without a partition test. What this post
adds to that argument is narrow but specific: a verdict, read off the same criterion, that the platform and
the cooperative share an identical closed exit, the whole margin riding on it, so the difference between them
cannot be read off the bypass test at all — and a Hegelian reason the difference matters that Christiaens's
framework does not supply on its own: governance is the *condition* under which being-at-home becomes
possible, not the achievement itself. Even the co-op is only a candidate; one pursuing irrational ends, or one
its members despise, has the condition and not the achievement. Member governance is the standing where
freedom could grow, the ethical shape Hegel gave the *Korporation*, the trade organized into a body whose
members have their recognition in it (Hegel, 1821/1991a, §§250–256) — standing, not yet the growth. The
measure reads the mediator in both and returns the same verdict, because the thing that separates them —
rational content, and whether the members can recognize their own reason in the whole's ends — is what a
partition does not see. A test that cannot tell a co-op from a warehouse is not measuring freedom; it reads
whether the mediator can be gone around, and returns "it cannot" for both, the closing of the exit, which is
freedom's precondition and not freedom.

This is where Neuhouser's two-condition standard comes back to collect. His Hegel certifies an institution as
free only when it furnishes the objective preconditions of freedom and its members can recognize themselves
in it. The bypass test reads neither. What it reads sits beneath both: that the exit is shut, that the member
is in for good. A warehouse shuts the exit and furnishes almost none of the preconditions; a marriage shuts
it and furnishes many. The test cannot tell them apart, because the closing of the exit is the threshold at
which Neuhouser's question begins, not an answer to either half of it. It can tell you a member is bound into
a whole he cannot leave. It cannot tell you whether the whole gives him his freedom or takes it — whether he
is a spouse or a serf. That second question, for Hegel, is freedom itself, and a reviewer holding Neuhouser is
right to say the causal criterion sits at its threshold and no further.

The concession is correct, and it is the part of this reading that sits worst with me. I built a test to
tell a genuine whole from a heap, and it hands the same verdict to the marriage and the sweatshop. It is
most confident about a coordination exactly where Hegel would have been most alarmed by it. The instrument
reads the closing of the exit — freedom's precondition — and goes silent on freedom itself, and the case
where that silence costs the most, the warehouse that reads like the co-op that could be a home, is not a
corner case, and it is the case that matters most for what platforms are doing to people right now. The
silence does not make the verdict false; the exit really is closed, and a test that reads the closing should
say so. But an instrument whose competence runs out exactly at the border of freedom has a real limit.

The scope is narrow: I have run no firm through the classifier for this. The program's own accounting is
direct — the operationalization "introduces no new computation," and "the bridge to organizations is
unbuilt: the parties are nodes in a Boolean model, and standing is explored on those models, not measured on
any firm" (coordinative_sovereignty/instruments/formal_standing.md). The App Store developer, the Amazon
Marketplace seller, the CoopCycle courier-member, the Som Mobilitat member are cases classified against the
criterion, populated cells in a taxonomy, not systems run through Φ. The warehouse-versus-co-op contrast is
an interpretive application of the criterion to two real arrangements.

It locates, in the wiring, the members for whom the question of being-at-home becomes urgent and exit stops
being an answer. What survives is narrower than the temptation in either direction: the program does not
certify freedom, and it does not measure being-at-home. Where the mediator is contingent and the bypass stays
open, Hegel's void is a live option and the liberal story of freedom-as-exit still runs. Where it is
necessary, or where a contingent gate is closed by capture, that story is finished — the driver is in the
whole for good, and the only freedom left to argue about is the Hegelian one: voice, standing, recognition
within a coordination he cannot leave. The instrument does not answer that argument. It tells you when you
are in it. That is not nothing, because the loudest current answer to the gig driver is still "you're free,
you can quit," and the bypass test is what shows that answer to be the freedom of the void — a real
severance, available to none of the people this argument is about, worth exactly what leaving the only market
there is is worth.

Coordinative sovereignty, then, is a demand made on the wholes my instrument finds irreducible, not a
property it confers on them — the demand that a coordination no one can leave owe its members voice, since
it has taken their exit. Hegel grounds the demand in the rational content of the institution, in whether its
members can recognize themselves in it. I cannot measure that. What I can measure is when the demand comes
due: when the mediator has closed the exit and thrown the whole weight of freedom onto voice. Hold that line,
and the reading is a tool for finding where the political question lives. Drop it, let irreducibility stand
in for being-at-home, and the same reading becomes a certificate of contentment for every whole strong enough
to trap the people inside it. The line is the difference between a diagnosis and an apology, and it runs
where Hegel drew it: between being in an other and being at home in it.

## Freedom in the Whole

Hegel's freedom is a quality of membership, not a distance from it. You are free not when you have escaped
every whole but when you are inside the right one and can recognize it as your own — at home in the other,
depending on it and thereby on yourself. The coordination program can do one honest thing with that claim and
must refuse a second. It can read, off the causal structure, which members route through an open bypass and
can still leave, and which route through a mediator that is necessary, or a gate a capture has closed, and
are in for good — and so tell where exit remains a freedom from where only voice does, before anyone argues
about which to prize, and before Berlin or Taylor gets a vote. It cannot read whether a member who is in for
good is at home or trapped, and so it cannot, on its own, tell a Hegelian whole from a well-built cage.

The instrument draws the line between the members who can leave and the members who cannot. Which of those
unleaveable wholes is a home and which is a cage, it cannot say — and that, not the drawing of the line, is
where freedom actually lives. Hegel would have said the sorting is only the doorway to the question of
freedom, not the room where it is decided. He would have been right. But it is the doorway, and until now no
instrument had found it.
