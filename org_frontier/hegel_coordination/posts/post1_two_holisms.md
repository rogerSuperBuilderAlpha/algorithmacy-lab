# Two Holisms

*Roger Hunt · draft v2 (lab-generated, incorporating the five-reviewer panel; for the author to revise in
his own voice before anything posts). Sourcing note at the end flags what still needs book confirmation.*

---

Hegel wrote that the true is the whole. In the Preface to the *Phenomenology of Spirit* the line arrives
almost as a slogan, and quotation has worn it smooth: "The true is the whole."[^1] The coordination program
I have been building runs on a sentence of its own. A whole is what no partition preserves: split the system
any way you like, and if no way of splitting it leaves its inner cause and effect intact, its parts were
members, not neighbors.

An example, since the sentence is abstract and about to carry weight. Two people who have worked side by side
for years finish each other's thoughts. Ask what each one contributes and the question misfires — the
finished thought is produced by the pair, in the back-and-forth, and there is no line you can draw down the
middle that leaves the thinking whole on either side of it. Draw the line anyway and you do not get two
smaller collaborators. You get two people who now have to work alone. That "no clean cut" is what my
criterion looks for, and where it finds it, the criterion says: one thing, not two.

Hegel's sentence and mine sound like a single thought in two idioms, and the pull is to read them that
way — to make Hegel an early and eloquent version of a claim now stated precisely. Hold off. This series
exists because the two holisms are different, and the difference earns more than the resemblance does.

Begin with what Hegel means by whole, because the slogan hides it. The rest of §20 does the work: the whole
"is only the essence completing itself through its own development," and the absolute "is essentially a
result, and only at the end is it what it is in truth."[^2] A few paragraphs earlier he had refused the
shortcut. The result by itself is not the whole; "the whole is the result together with the way the result
comes to be" (§3).[^3] Cut the becoming away and what is left is a corpse — his word, a few lines on.[^4] A
Hegelian whole is an outcome that carries its own genesis inside it.

A second thing is built into that whole, and it is the one that will give me trouble. Hegel's whole is
developmental, and it is also self-knowing. Everything hangs, he says at §17, "on apprehending and expressing
the true not as substance but rather even more as subject."[^5] A substance is what it is. A subject is what
it is by taking itself up — "this self-restoring sameness, the reflective turn into itself in its otherness"
(§18).[^6] Hegel's whole comprehends itself as its own result. It knows that it is a whole. That is the fact
the rest of the essay circles.

This reading sits near the center of a long argument about what Hegelian holism even is. Charles Taylor, in
the fullest statement of the cosmic-spirit reading, glosses the whole as "a totality, a whole whose parts are
intrinsically related to each other, that is, where each can only be understood by its relations to the
others" — and he sets that totality against "a group of finite things which are in contingent causal
interaction."[^7] James Kreines, who spends a book restoring a real metaphysics to Hegel against the readers
who would deflate it, still frames the project as a "metaphysics of reason," about "the explanatory reasons
why things do what they do, or are as they are."[^8] Taylor's Hegel is cosmic, Kreines's austere, and they
agree on little. They agree that Hegel's whole is a matter of meaning, or of grounds, or of a life coming to
know itself. Neither makes it a matter of which cut a structure cannot survive.

That last one is my sentence, and it belongs to a different family. The lab's whole is synchronic and causal.
It takes a system in a single state and asks whether any partition of it preserves the cause and effect the
system has upon itself; a whole is a configuration no partition preserves. The test is answerable in
principle, and it is blind to nearly everything Hegel wants — blind to development, to self-knowledge, to
whether the system means anything or is going anywhere.

Now the resemblance turns awkward, and it turns in a direction worth slowing down for. You might expect Hegel
to file my criterion under the crudest thing in his book: the relation of "Whole and Parts," which he ranks
the lowest of the essential relations and calls "untrue," a station the concept passes through on its way to
force, to inner and outer, to life.[^9] But look at *why* he calls it untrue. The fault is the additive
picture — a whole that is the mere sum of its parts, each part self-subsistent, the whole nothing apart from
them. That is the reducible case, the heap. And the heap is exactly what my criterion is built to catch and
throw out: a system that some partition leaves intact was an aggregate all along. On aggregation, Hegel and I
are on the same side. My test reports "untrue" where he does.

So the criterion is not the crude thing it first appeared to be. It is a detector for the very additivity
Hegel diagnoses. What keeps it low is subtler, and it is the honest limit. Whatever verdict the test
returns — even "irreducible" — it thinks the whole along one axis only: cut it, and watch what the cut cannot
preserve. Its entire vocabulary is division and resistance to division. It never reaches the register where
"part" stops being the organizing word — force expressing itself, the whole that lives, the whole that knows
itself. My instrument works at the level of parts and their partition and has nothing to say about the rungs
above it. That is an awkward place to plant a program that means, later, to reach the corporation and the
master and the slave, and I will not pretend otherwise. The bet of the series is that watching exactly where
a low instrument goes blind tells you something about what stands above it.

The blindness has a name and a living champion. Karen Ng argues that the whole Hegel cares about is a living
one: for Hegel the inner purposiveness of an organism is not just a lens we are permitted to use on it but
part of what it is, so that, in her phrase, "life opens up the space of reasons itself."[^10] Her word for
the living thing's basic act is judgment in the old sense, *Ur-teil* — an original division the organism runs
on itself, subject from environment, individual from species, and then takes back up. A whole that partitions
itself, and knows that it does, is not standing by for an external cut to certify that it holds together. My
test comes to divide a thing that has already divided itself, on purpose, and reintegrated. It measures from
outside a whole whose entire life is doing that from inside.

I owe Ng a real answer, and it needs its own post. Here is the shape of the disagreement. Both sentences
answer one question: when does interdependence become a whole? Civil society is thick with mutual dependence
that never resolves into one thing; so is a market. Hegel answered with recognition, and beneath recognition
with a living self-relation. I answer with a partition test. The series sets the two answers beside each
other and asks, post by post, whether a bridge between them holds.

Perhaps the whole difference is bookkeeping. Hegel's whole unfolds, mine is read in one state, and
"developmental versus synchronic" names two clocks and nothing more. It does not, and not because one clock
runs while the other has stopped. Hand my criterion the entire history — run the partition test frame by
frame across the whole becoming — and it still never turns up a thing that takes itself up. It will have
measured, exhaustively, and never once met a subject. The difference was never really about time. It is that
Hegel's whole knows itself as its own result, and mine knows nothing at all. Adding duration to a partition
test gives you a longer measurement, not a self.

Two holisms, then. One is a result that carries its becoming and knows itself as its own result. The other is
a configuration no partition preserves. They rhyme, and the rhyme is the reason to go slowly. The posts after
this one build the bridges: the syllogism and the mediated third, master and slave, the corporation. This one
draws the line those bridges have to cross, so that when one holds, we can be sure it is holding across
something real.
