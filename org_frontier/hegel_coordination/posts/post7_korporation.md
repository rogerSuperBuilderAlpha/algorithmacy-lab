# The Necessary Middle

*Roger Hunt · draft v1 (lab-generated from the plan and grounding maps; for the author to revise in his
own voice before anything posts). Sourcing note at the end flags what needs book confirmation.*

---

A cabinetmaker in a mid-sized German town around 1818 has a skill, a shop, a set of customers, and no
standing. He can make a chair as well as any man alive, and when he is asked what he is, the honest answer is
that he is a person who happens to make chairs. His trade is a private fact about how he earns money, and if
the buyers thin out one winter it gives him nothing at all. He is, in the vocabulary Hegel had just finished
building, a member of civil society and of nothing smaller — one node in the system of needs, dependent on
everyone and recognized by no one. Hegel looked at that man and decided he was a problem the state could not
reach directly. Between the isolated producer and the universal state, he argued, something has to stand: an
institution that takes a man's trade and makes it a station, that gives self-seeking a form in which it
counts as more than self-seeking. He called it the *Korporation*, and the argument that it is necessary — not
helpful, not decent, but necessary, a thing without which the passage from the individual to the state does
not complete — is the closest Hegel comes to a claim my coordination program can test.[^1]

I have been building a criterion that sorts wholes from heaps. A whole is what no partition preserves: cut
the system any way you like, and if no cut leaves its inner cause and effect intact, its parts were members
and not neighbors. The program lays a second sorting over the first, and it is the one this post needs. Among
the wholes that pass, it distinguishes the ones held together by the work the parts do for each other from
the ones held together by an external constraint that props the connection up. The first is irreducible
because integrating is what it does. The second is irreducible only while the constraint holds; lift the
constraint and it falls back into the aggregate it always was. Necessary integration and contingent binding.
The lab reaches the distinction through a catalog: every candidate whole gets coded both ways, and the coding
rule asks one counterfactual — restore the direct tie between the parts and take the intermediary out; does
what the arrangement did survive, or did it live only in the middle?

Hegel's corporation is a case built for exactly that question, and his own century ran the experiment. That
is the reason to spend a post on §§250 to 256 of the *Philosophy of Right*, four dense pages most readers
skim on the way to the state. Hegel argues there that a particular intermediary is necessary; the nineteenth
century, before and around him, was busy abolishing that very intermediary in the name of freedom. The
temptation is to let the experiment settle the reading, and I am going to resist it, because the experiment
is confounded and points nowhere clean. The claim itself is worth the whole post. It is the first place in
the series where Hegel says of a specific middle term that it is not optional, and that is the sentence a
program about necessary and contingent integration has been waiting for.

## What Hegel says the corporation does

Start with the problem the corporation answers, because the problem is what makes the answer necessary rather
than nice. Civil society, for Hegel, is the system of needs turned loose: everyone works for everyone through
the market, no one intends the result, the dependence is total and the unity external. He had already given
the arrangement its bleak name, the state of necessity and of the understanding, a *Not- und Verstandesstaat*
whose members hang on one another without ever becoming one thing.[^2] The cabinetmaker lives there. His
problem is not that he is poor, though he may be; it is that his work reaches the universal only as an
accident, and the whole apparatus of mutual service leaves him, as a person, alone. Civil society dissolves
the old bonds — the guild that told a man what he was, the estate he was born into — and puts a market in
their place, and a market recognizes no one. It clears prices; it does not confer standing.

Into that gap Hegel inserts the corporation, and he is precise about what it does. It gathers the members of
a trade into an association that is theirs, and turns a private skill into a public station. The member is no
longer a man who happens to make chairs; he is a cabinetmaker, a recognized practitioner of a recognized
craft, with a place others acknowledge and he can acknowledge in himself. Hegel makes the domestic comparison
directly: the corporation comes onto the scene "like a second family" for its members, taking over toward the
individual something of the office the family held before civil society pulled him out of it.[^3] The first
family raised him and let him go into the world of work; the second receives him there and gives that world a
form in which he can belong to it. Between the two lies the whole cold stretch of the market, and the
corporation is Hegel's warmth restored on the far side.

The good it confers is standing, and the word to hold onto is *honor*. Inside the corporation the member's
competence is not merely a fact about his output; it is recognized, something he is entitled to be respected
for. Hegel presses the point by describing its absence: the individual outside any authorized corporation is
without the standing membership would give him, his skill a private means of getting by, his security hanging
on the accident of demand.[^4] Membership is what lets a man make himself, by his own industry and skill,
into a recognized member of civil society, and so win recognition both in his own eyes and in the eyes of
others.[^5] That last phrase is load-bearing, and worth noticing now for how far it sits from anything my
instrument measures. The good the corporation delivers is recognition, an attitude the members take toward
one another and themselves. Hegel's corporation is an engine of standing, and standing is a thing people
confer on each other, not a thing you find by cutting a causal graph.

Then he raises the stakes as high as they go. The corporation is not a convenience bolted onto civil society;
it is, with the family, one of the two roots from which the ethical life of the state actually grows. Hegel
says it flatly: as the family is the first ethical root of the state, the corporation is the second, the one
planted in civil society itself.[^6] The architecture matters. The state, for Hegel, is not a contract among
individuals and not a machine sitting on top of them; it is an ethical whole, and an ethical whole cannot be
reached from atomized persons in one jump. The individual has to be gathered into something before he can be
a citizen of something. The family gathers him first; the corporation gathers him again, on the economic
terrain where the family's hold does not extend. Take the corporation out and you do not get a cleaner, more
direct relation between the individual and the state. You get a heap of individuals and a state with nothing
to grip — the very atomism the whole *Philosophy of Right* is built to overcome. That is the structure of a
necessity claim: the corporation is a middle term, and the extremes do not hold together without it.

The necessity gets a second face, and it turns an ethical claim into a constitutional one. Hegel's
corporation is also the channel through which the individual enters political life. In his legislature, the
members of civil society reach the assembly not as isolated voters dropping atomized ballots into an urn but
as members of the corporations and estates they already belong to, deputized through those bodies.[^16] He is
polemical about the alternative: direct election by disaggregated individuals strikes him as a way of
producing an inorganic heap of votes, because it reaches the citizen only after stripping away every
membership that made him more than a unit.[^17] The corporation is where the individual acquires the
membership that qualifies him to be represented at all. Take it away and he does not become a purer
participant in the state; he becomes a countable atom, and a polity assembled out of countable atoms is, for
Hegel, not a state but a crowd with a tally. The necessity runs on two levels, then. Ethically, without the
corporation the individual cannot find his standing in the economic world. Constitutionally, without it he
has no political existence except as a number.

George Heiman put the historical weight on that claim better than anyone. Reading Hegel's corporate doctrine
against its sources, Heiman argued that the corporation is not a nostalgic recovery of the medieval guild but
a deliberately modern institution, invented to do a job the modern economy creates and cannot do for itself:
to give the individual an ethical and political mediation between his private work and the public whole.[^7]
It is where Hegel's political philosophy answers the atomizing force of the market, and the answer is an
intermediate body with real standing, not a sentiment. Thomas Klikauer would later read the same institution
as Hegel's "moral corporation."[^8] Both put Hegel on the side of the claim I want to test: some
intermediaries are necessary, and this is one of them.

## The fork: integrating work, or a constraint held in place

Grant that Hegel thinks the corporation is necessary. There are two things "necessary" can mean here, and the
whole reading turns on which one he is entitled to. Let me name the fork in the program's terms first, then in
Hegel's.

The first reading calls the corporation a necessary integrator. The standing it confers is produced in the
middle — it exists nowhere in the individual alone and nowhere in the state alone, and comes into being only
in the joint arrangement where a trade is organized into an association that recognizes its members. Sever
the individual from the corporation and you do not recover the standing elsewhere; you destroy it, because it
lived in the coupling. This is what the program means by integrating work: the middle does not carry a good
from one extreme to the other the way a wire carries a current it did not make, it makes the good, and no cut
around it preserves what it made. That is a whole in my strict sense, of the necessary kind — irreducible
because integrating is the thing it does.

The second reading calls the corporation a constraint holding a conduit in place. Here the corporation is a
legal privilege: a monopoly on a trade, an authority to admit and exclude. It looks irreducible because the
privilege makes it costly to route around — you cannot simply set up as a cabinetmaker if the corporation
controls who may — but the irreducibility is an artifact of the constraint, not of any integrating the
arrangement performs. Lift the constraint, and the individual's connection to his livelihood survives
without the middle, because the middle was never making anything, only gatekeeping. The program's name for
the pure case is the self-liquidating broker: an intermediary irreducible exactly until someone removes the
barrier that made going around it expensive, at which point it dissolves and takes nothing with it.

The two readings make opposite predictions about one operation. Take the corporation away and watch: if the
standing it conferred evaporates and cannot be reconstituted by any direct tie between individual and state,
the integrating reading was right; if the standing turns out to have been a rent that vanishes with the
privilege, leaving the individual no worse at his trade, the constraint reading was right. This is the
restore-the-tie counterfactual the catalog runs on every candidate whole — remove the middle, reconnect the
extremes, ask what died. Hegel's corporation is unusually testable on the point, because his own century
removed the middle.

## The experiment his century ran, and why it decides nothing

The guilds were being abolished all around the argument. In France the Revolution swept them away fast and on
principle: the d'Allarde decree of March 1791 dissolved the guild monopolies and made trades free to enter,
and the Le Chapelier law that June forbade the corporations and associations of a given trade from reforming
in any guise, on the theory that between the individual citizen and the state no intermediate body should
stand at all.[^9] In Prussia the same dismantling came from above, through the reform ministry's economic
liberalization: the trade-tax edict of 1810 and the *Gewerbefreiheit* law of September 1811 broke the guild
monopolies and opened occupations to anyone who paid the license.[^10] Hegel wrote the *Philosophy of Right*
in 1820, into the immediate aftermath of a continent-wide abolition of precisely the kind of intermediate
body he was about to call a second ethical root of the state, and he knew it. The Addition to §255 opens on
the fact — the abolition of corporations in recent times — and treats it as the error to correct, the modern
conceit that the individual should simply look after himself with nothing between him and the whole.[^11]

Here is a natural experiment, tidy on its face. The claim is that the corporation is a necessary integrator;
the nineteenth century lifted the constraint that held it in place. Did the standing it conferred survive,
reconstituted by other means, or did it die? Run the restore-the-tie counterfactual on the historical record
and read off the verdict.

The experiment decides nothing, and the reason is instructive, because it is easy to paper over. Three things
break it.

First, the direction of the argument runs against the experiment. Hegel did not predict abolition would be
harmless and get refuted by events; he predicted the opposite, writing *against* the liberal abolition on the
grounds that stripping the individual of every intermediate membership and leaving him alone before the state
would cost the modern person his ethical footing. The abolitions are not a test he passed or failed. They are
the policy he was arguing with. Hegel and the reformers made the same prediction about what abolition removes,
a real bond and a real standing, and disagreed only about whether removing it was good — a normative
disagreement wearing the costume of an empirical one.

Second, the case is confounded past repair. The guilds did not vanish into a controlled vacuum; they were
abolished in the same decades that industrialization, mass urbanization, the factory, and the modern
bureaucratic state were remaking every condition the outcome depends on. Whatever happened to the standing of
tradesmen after 1811 happened for a dozen reasons at once, and no clean contrast isolates the corporation's
share of it.

Third, and this matters most for the series, even a clean result would not select the reading I am after.
Suppose the record showed that abolishing the guilds cost tradesmen something real and irreplaceable, a
genuine good dead with the middle. That supports the claim that the corporation was a necessary integrator.
It does not show the good was *causal* integration of the sort my partition test measures. It is at least as
consistent with the recognitive reading, on which what died was recognition — a form of social standing, an
attitude a community of practitioners took toward its members. Recognition is exactly what Brandom, Pinkard,
Pippin, and Honneth put at the center of Hegel's social thought, and it is a normative-attitudinal good, not a
causal-structural one.[^12] The experiment cannot tell a whole held by causal coupling from a whole held by
mutual recognition, because both predict that removing the middle destroys the good. The two criteria agree
on the sign of the effect and disagree on its nature, and no body count distinguishes them.

So the abolitions are context and illustration, and I use them as nothing more. They show what
constraint-lifting looks like and why Hegel thought it mattered; they dramatize the fork; they do not stand on
either tine of it. The evidence that bears on the fork is not in the historical record. It is in what Hegel
says the corporation is, and there the text is sharper than the history.

## Hegel already drew the line the fork needs

The best evidence that Hegel means integrating work and not constraint is that he draws the distinction
himself, inside the same passage, and comes down on the side the program cares about. He is emphatic that his
corporation is not the old guild. The medieval guild was closed — you entered by birth or privilege, it
guarded a monopoly, it was a fortress against the market and against the state alike. Hegel's corporation is
open: it co-opts its members by their competence, admits on the basis of skill and rectitude rather than
lineage, and takes its place inside the modern economy rather than walling itself off from it. In the
Addition where he confronts the abolition, he insists on exactly this difference, distinguishing the living
modern corporation from the dead closed body that deserved to fall.[^13] The translators split on the word
for the thing Hegel is rejecting — Knox renders it "caste," Nisbet "guild" — but the contrast is the same in
either: the closed, birth-bound, privilege-hoarding body on one side, and on the other the open association
that earns its members a standing.[^14]

This is the conduit-versus-integrator distinction, drawn by Hegel a century before the vocabulary. The closed
guild is the constraint that holds a conduit in place, irreducible only because the privilege made routing
around it costly, and when the eighteenth century routed around it the guild collapsed into the rent it had
always been. Hegel agrees it deserved to collapse. What he denies is that abolishing the *constraint*
abolishes the *need* — that because the guild was a mere privilege, the corporation as such is one. Clear away
the closed guild and you still need the open corporation, because the work the open corporation does was never
the guild's gatekeeping. It was the making of standing, which survives the loss of the monopoly because it
never depended on it. Hegel is separating the contingent constraint (the closed monopoly, rightly abolished)
from the necessary integration (the conferral of standing, wrongly abandoned with it); the reformers threw
out the integrator with the constraint because they could not tell them apart.

That is a strong result for the integrating reading, and an easy one to overstate. Hegel himself does not
think the corporation's necessity reduces to its privilege; he has the distinction and he uses it. A reading
that flattened his corporation into the old guild and declared it a self-liquidating broker would be
misreading him — the flattening the lab's passage card flags as the error to avoid.[^15] On the fork as Hegel
poses it, he is on the integrator side, by his own hand.

## The card, coded both ways

The lab's discipline for a case like this is to build a card and code the entity twice, so the disagreement
becomes a single explicit counterfactual instead of a mood. The entity is the corporation; its slot is a
middle between the individual, a private producer in the system of needs, and the universal state, which
cannot reach him as a bare atom; the good whose fate the counterfactual tracks is his standing. Coded as a
contingent binding, the middle is the monopoly over entry to a trade, and restoring the tie — abolish the
monopoly, open the trade — leaves the individual's livelihood intact and exposes the "standing" as a rent
that vanishes with the fence. Coded as a necessary integrator, the middle is the conferral of standing
itself, and restoring the tie destroys it, because it lived in the coupling and no direct link between atom
and state reconstitutes it. One counterfactual, two predictions, and the load-bearing rule is the whole
question: does the good survive direct reconnection?

It is a question about a specific institution in a specific state, not a verdict stamped on corporations in
general. Hegel's own text codes his corporation as necessary, splitting the historical institution along the
card's own seam — monopoly the contingent constraint, rightly abolished; standing the necessary integration,
wrongly abandoned with it. That is the card filled in by his hand, and the strongest thing the post can show.
What it cannot show is that any real corporation you point the instrument at today codes the same way. The
card is a template for a case; the verdict is the case's to give.

## The bridge, and where it thins

Now the honest accounting. The bridge from Hegel's argument to my program holds in one place and thins to
analogy in another, and running the two together would be the whole failure of the post.

The bridge holds at the level of the boundary. Hegel and the program both draw a line between a middle that
can be removed without loss and a middle whose removal destroys what the arrangement did — Hegel between the
closed guild and the open corporation, the program between contingent binding and necessary integration — and
the two lines fall in the same place on this case. Hegel is sorting intermediaries into the removable and the
necessary on grounds a partition test would recognize, what survives when you take the middle out, a full
century before anyone had a partition test. That is the claim the series can defend: Hegel is an early
theorist of the aggregate-versus-configuration boundary who runs it through an economic intermediary and gets
the sort right. He puts the corporation on the configuration side and the closed guild on the aggregate side,
and my instrument, pointed at the same case, agrees with the sort.

The bridge thins the moment you ask what the standing *is*. My criterion certifies that something in the
corporation is irreducible — that some good lives only in the middle and no cut preserves it. It is
constitutively silent on what that good consists in. Hegel is not silent. He says what the good is, and what
he says is honor, recognition, ethical membership, a standing a man wins in his own eyes and in the eyes of
others. That is a normative-attitudinal good, the recognition theorists' home ground, not mine. My partition
test can detect that removing the corporation destroys something; it cannot tell that the something is
recognition rather than a coordination benefit or an information channel or a risk pool. That the good is
*standing*, conferred by acknowledgment, a matter of how members regard one another, is exactly what the
instrument brackets, and exactly what Hegel cares about most.

So the refutation condition the plan set for this post is live, and I have to meet it. The condition: Hegel's
corporations confer honor and ethical standing, not coordination, and if reading them as necessary
integrators requires ignoring what Hegel actually says corporations do, the bridge should be downgraded. It
requires ignoring some of it. The causal reading captures that the corporation is on the necessary side of
the fork, and that its good is produced in the middle; it cannot capture that the good is recognition. To
insist the corporation is *nothing but* a causal integrator would be to do to Hegel what the reformers did to
the guild, throwing out the content because my instrument cannot hold it. The corporation is a necessary
integrator *and* what it integrates is standing, and the second clause is Hegel's, not mine, and my criterion
has no term for it.

That is the downgrade, stated at the right size — not a collapse of the bridge, since the boundary
convergence is real and is the series' thesis in miniature, but an honest marking of where the reading stops
carrying Hegel's weight. The program can say: here is an intermediary Hegel argues is necessary, the
necessity has the structural form the catalog codes, and the sort comes out the same both ways. It cannot
say: and therefore the standing it confers is a coordination effect. Hegel's necessity is ethical, the
catalog's is causal, and they coincide on the sort while parting on the good. The post that pretended
otherwise would be smuggling recognition into a causal box and calling the smuggling a result.

## Three middles now

Run the card on institutions we actually have and it sorts them, which is the test of whether it does any
work. A professional body with a real ethic — a bar association, a medical college — codes the way Hegel's
corporation codes: it admits by competence, organizes a trade into a recognized profession, and confers a
standing its members carry. To be a member of the bar is to be something beyond a person who argues cases for
money; abolish it and the profession scatters into individuals selling a service, the standing gone with the
body that made it. It is a necessary integrator, and what it integrates is recognition, so it codes the same
on both criteria, mine and the recognition theorists'. A pure occupational license — the permit to braid hair
that reserves a trade without organizing any profession behind it — codes the other way: the closed guild in
modern dress, irreducible only because of the fence, and striking it leaves the activity untouched and the
"standing" exposed as a rent. The instrument and the recognition reading agree on the professional body and
agree on the bare license. The interesting case is the one where they split.

The platform splits them, and that is why the lab cares about this whole line. A ride-hailing app or a gig
marketplace stands in the corporation's slot, between the worker and the market, and the worker is bound to it
more tightly than any guildsman to his corporation, because the matching, the pricing, the reputation, and the
payment all run through the middle and nowhere else. Run the card. Remove the platform and reconnect driver to
rider directly and much of what the arrangement did does not survive: the instant matching, the priced
coordination of a city's trips, the trust manufactured out of ratings. By my criterion the platform is a
necessary integrator, a whole no cut preserves, scoring where Hegel's corporation scores. Now ask the question
my instrument cannot. Does the platform confer standing — does the driver win through it a recognized station
he holds in his own eyes and others', the way the guildsman was a cabinetmaker and the lawyer is a member of
the bar? Usually not. The platform binds without recognizing. It integrates his labor into a whole and gives
him no membership in it, no honor, often not even a name the system uses. On the causal criterion it passes.
On Hegel's it fails at the thing corporations are for.

That split is the whole reason to build an instrument that reads causal structure and brackets recognition,
and the whole danger of it. It lets you see what the recognition reading can miss: that an arrangement can be
a real whole, strictly irreducible, and confer no standing on the people it binds — tight integration and
ethical membership come apart, and modernity has built machines that deliver the first without the second. It
also warns you what the instrument does if you trust it alone. It certifies the platform as a whole and says
nothing about the whole being estranging, because silence about recognition is what it was designed for.
Hegel held the two apart with a word; my criterion measures the binding and cannot see the standing, and the
platform is where that limit stops being academic.

## What the corporation adds to the series

Every post in this series takes one rung of Hegel's ladder and asks whether my low instrument can reach it.
The corporation is the rung where Hegel stops describing wholes and starts prescribing one — where he says
not merely *this is a genuine unity* but *this unity is necessary, build it, the modern person is impoverished
without it*. That is new. Master and slave was a whole that dissolves itself; civil society was a whole that
never forms; the syllogism was the form of a whole in the abstract. The corporation is the first place Hegel
plants his flag on a specific intermediate institution and says the passage from the individual to the state
runs through it and through nothing that would replace it. A program about necessary and contingent
integration should care about that sentence more than about any other in the *Philosophy of Right*, because
it is the sentence closest to its own central distinction.

It is close, and it is not the same, and the gap is the finding. Hegel's "necessary" means that without the
corporation the individual cannot reach his freedom, cannot find the standing that makes him a member rather
than an atom. The catalog's "necessary" means that no partition of the arrangement preserves what it does.
These rhyme, because both say a middle cannot be removed without loss. They differ because the loss Hegel
measures is ethical standing and the loss the catalog measures is causal structure — and the platform case
shows a system can suffer the second without the first. The corporation is where that difference first comes
into view: an intermediary that binds you against one that gives you standing, which Hegel could hold apart
with a word and my instrument cannot hold apart at all.

The corporation, then, is a win for Hegel on the classification and a limit on the instrument, in the same
proportion as the rest of the series. He sorts the closed guild from the open corporation correctly, on
grounds a partition test shares, and then says the thing the partition test cannot: that the open
corporation's work is the conferral of a standing men win in each other's eyes. The program keeps the sort
and loses the good, records that it has done so, and moves on to the next rung one honest bridge richer and
under no illusion that it has annexed what it merely came alongside.
