# Interdependence Without Unity

*Roger Hunt · draft v1 (lab-generated from the plan and grounding maps; for the author to revise in his own
voice before anything posts). Sourcing note at the end flags what needs book confirmation.*

---

In the first post I split a market between two rooms to show what my criterion does. A market coordinates a
million strangers who never meet, dense with dependence, every price a trader sees set by everyone else — and
you can cut it clean. Put the traders in two rooms, let each room clear its own prices, and each half goes on
doing what it did. The price mechanism survives the cut. Nothing the market *does* died on the table. Enormous
dependence, and still an aggregate.

Hegel ran that test in 1821, by hand, on the largest interdependent system anyone had described. He had read
the political economists. He looked at the web of buying and selling and mutual need a modern economy runs on,
the thing he called the system of needs, and he did not call it a state. He called it the external state, a
state of necessity and of the understanding, a system of all-round interdependence whose unity stays outside
the parts that depend on one another.[^1] That is my two-rooms verdict, reached a century and a half before
there was a number to attach to it, and reached in a register that had no number and wanted none.

This post is about that convergence and its exact limit. Hegel drew the boundary my criterion draws — dense
interdependence on one side, genuine wholeness on the other — and he drew it in the right place, putting the
market economy on the near side and calling its unity external. Connection was already not constitution. But he
drew it for reasons that are not mine, with an upgrade condition that is not mine, and the discipline of this
post is to hold those two things together: the same line, two registers, and no quiet annexation of one by the
other.

## What Hegel had actually read

Start with the fact that grounds everything else. When Hegel wrote about interdependence he was not gesturing
at "the economy" from a philosopher's armchair. He had read Adam Smith, Jean-Baptiste Say, and David Ricardo,
and in the Remark to §189 he says so, naming the three of them and praising what they did: the *Philosophy of
Right* points to "the interesting spectacle, as in Smith, Say, and Ricardo, of thought working upon the endless
mass of details" of economic life and drawing out its simple governing principles.[^2] That is a philosopher
tipping his hat to a science he thinks has found something real. Political economy, for Hegel, had done genuine
work: it had found the intelligible order inside the apparent chaos of the market, the Understanding at work in
the thing itself.

Norbert Waszek has documented how deep the reading went. Hegel did not skim Smith for a slogan. He worked
through the Scottish tradition — Smith, and behind Smith the earlier James Steuart, and Adam Ferguson, whose
account of civil society Hegel took the term itself from — and he digested the mechanism, not just the
vocabulary.[^3] The system of needs in the *Philosophy of Right* is Hegel's own compression of what he learned
from them: that in a developed economy each person pursues private ends, works to satisfy private wants, and in
doing so is bound into everyone else's satisfaction of theirs, so that "the livelihood, happiness, and legal
status of one man is interwoven with the livelihood, happiness, and rights of all."[^4] That sentence is a
description of universal interdependence as precise as anyone had managed. It is the thing my criterion was
built to weigh.

Be concrete about the mechanism, because Hegel was. The engine of the interweaving is the division of labor,
and Smith had given it its emblem: a pin factory where ten men who each perform one operation make more pins in
a day than the same ten could make working each alone. Hegel took the lesson and pushed it. The more finely
labor divides, the more each worker's product is useless to the worker and needful to everyone else, so the
tie of mutual dependence tightens with every subdivision — and at the same time, Hegel saw, the work itself
grows more abstract, more mechanical, so that "this abstraction of one man's production from another's makes
labour more and more mechanical, until finally man is able to step aside and install machines in his place"
(§198).[^5] He is
watching interdependence intensify and the individual task hollow out in the same motion. That double
movement, more binding and more mechanical, is the exact thing my criterion has to weigh, because the naive
reading of it is that intensifying dependence must be building toward a whole. Hegel did not draw that
conclusion. Neither does the test.

Hold on the word "interwoven." It is the whole question in one image. Interwoven threads make a fabric, and a
fabric can be a genuine whole or a heap of tangles, and the image alone will not tell you which. Everything
depends on whether the weave is the kind a cut destroys or the kind a cut merely interrupts. Hegel had the
image and did not stop at it. He asked what kind of unity the weave was, and he gave an answer.

## The answer: external unity

His answer is §183, and it is blunt. Civil society, the whole system of interwoven needs and labor and
exchange, is "the external state — the state of necessity and of the understanding."[^1] Three words in that
phrase carry the verdict.

*External* is the load-bearing one. The unity of civil society is real but it does not live in the members. It
stands over them, a resultant of their private pursuits that none of them intends and that holds them together
from outside. Each person wants his own livelihood; the interweaving of all those private wants into a working
system is a fact about the aggregate, not a purpose any member carries. A market clears, and no trader meant
for it to clear. The order is genuine and the order is external, which is exactly the condition my two-rooms
cut exposes. Where the unity of a system sits outside the parts, in a resultant they produce but do not
inhabit, some partition will find the seam and the system will survive being cut along it.

*Necessity* — *Not*, need, want, distress — names the glue. What binds civil society is that its members need
each other, materially, to live. I make shoes and you grow grain and neither of us survives alone, so we are
chained together by mutual dependence. That is a strong bond and a low one. It is the bond of the two rooms:
strong enough that no one leaves, loose enough that the system can be re-founded in each half. Dependence is
not the same as membership, and Hegel is marking the difference by calling the tie one of necessity rather than
of ethical life.

*Understanding* — *Verstand* — names the faculty whose kind of unity this is, and here the philosophical stakes
show through. Hegel's Understanding is the power that holds distinct things together while keeping them
distinct, that relates without fusing, that lays A beside B and grasps the relation between them as a relation
between two standing terms. Reason, *Vernunft*, is his name for the higher power that grasps a unity in which
the terms are not merely related but constituted by the relation, moments of one thing rather than neighbors in
a system. Civil society is a *Verstandesstaat*, a state as the Understanding pictures it: a well-related
manifold, an order of standing parts. It is not yet a whole in the sense Reason means. Hegel filed the market
economy under the faculty that relates without constituting, and that filing is the verbal form of my
result.[^6]

Notice what he did *not* say. He did not say civil society is a heap, a chaos, an absence of order. The
external state is highly organized; it has laws, a police, courts, a rational structure the economists mapped.
Hegel's point is sharper than "disorganized." A system can be maximally organized, dense with lawful
dependence, and still have its unity outside itself. Organization is not wholeness. That distinction is the
one thing the market's apologists and its Marxist critics both tend to lose, and Hegel kept it in 1821.

## The lab's version: everyone reads everyone

Here is where the criterion earns the comparison, because it explains *why* a system this interdependent lands
on the aggregate side, and the explanation is not obvious.

Build the smallest thing that shows it. Take a population of units, each one a little switch that is on or off,
and give every unit the same rule: on the next tick, turn on if at least *k* of the others are currently on,
otherwise turn off. Every unit reads every other unit. Wire it up and the dependence is total — no unit's next
state is settled without polling the whole population. By the crude measure of how connected the parts are,
this is as connected as a system gets. Each part reads everyone.

Now run the test. Split the population into two groups and ask whether the coordination survives the cut. It
largely does, and the reason is exact: every unit reads the *same thing*, a count, and responds to it the
*same way*. The coupling that looks like an all-to-all web is really each part's relation to a single shared
number, the tally of who is on. Cut the population in two and each half can keep a tally of its own members and
re-threshold against that; the shared number is reconstructible on either side of the cut because it was never
anywhere in particular. It rode a common variable that every part could read and no part owned. Unity that
lives in a shared scalar every member consults is unity that sits outside the members. My test finds the seam
and reports: aggregate.

This is the quorum, and it is worth naming as its own kind of thing because it is everywhere. Bacteria coordinate
by it: each cell secretes a signal, reads the ambient concentration, and switches behavior when the
concentration crosses a threshold — reading "everyone" through a common medium, the shared chemistry of the
broth. Split the colony and each half re-establishes its own quorum in its own smaller volume. Markets
coordinate by it: each trader reads the price, a public scalar that summarizes everyone's supply and demand,
and acts on it, and the two rooms each re-establish their own prices. A crowd reading a single applause meter,
a flock keyed to a shared alarm, a thousand firms watching one interest rate: everyone reads everyone, through
a common variable, and the common variable is the whole of what they share. Cut it, and the variable regrows in
each piece. That is the structure Hegel called external unity, and it is the structure the system of needs has.
The interweaving is real and it is scalar-mediated, and scalar-mediated interweaving is precisely the weave a
cut interrupts rather than destroys.

Set the differentiated whole beside it, because the contrast is the point. Take instead a system where each
unit reads *specific* others and does something *specific* with what it reads — where unit three's response to
unit five is not a response to a global count but to five in particular, and there is no single number that
summarizes the coupling. The two collaborators from the first post are like this: the finished thought is
produced in the specific back-and-forth of these two, and no shared scalar stands in for their coupling, so no
cut leaves the thinking whole on either side. That system has no seam. Its unity is not a public variable every
part could reconstruct alone; it lives in the specific mutual constraint that a partition breaks. My criterion
tells the two apart, the quorum from the collaboration, and it tells them apart by the very feature Hegel
marked: whether the unity is a resultant the parts consult from outside or a constitution they carry inside.

I want to be careful about what this shows and what it does not, because the tool is doing real work here and
the temptation is to make it do too much or too little. The measure does not "reduce" the quorum to nothing; a
quorum is a genuine coordinating structure, and a low score is not a verdict of "no coordination." The measure
discriminates. It separates dense dependence that rides a shared variable from dense dependence that does not,
and that discrimination is neither obvious nor free — you cannot read it off the wiring diagram, because the
quorum and the collaboration can have the identical all-to-all diagram and come apart only when you run the
partition. A number that tells scalar-mediated interdependence from irreducible interdependence, on systems
that look the same from outside, is the tool doing exactly the work the program built it to do. What it buys is
a decidable answer to Hegel's question about the market: external, or not. On civil society it answers the way
Hegel answered. A whole that reads everyone can still be an aggregate.

## Specialization tightens the tie and never closes the seam

There is a natural objection at this point, and Hegel's §198 is where I want to meet it. The objection says the
division of labor is going somewhere. As specialization deepens, each worker depends on more and more others,
the web thickens, and surely at some point thick enough dependence becomes a whole. The organism is the proof
of concept: a body is a triumph of the division of labor, heart and liver and nerve each doing one thing for
all the rest, and a body is as irreducible a whole as there is. Why should the specialized economy not be
climbing the same ladder toward the same result?

Because the two specializations are coupled in different ways, and the difference is the whole of it. In the
body, the liver and the heart are bound by specific reciprocal maintenance: the heart's output is not a public
number the liver reads off a ticker, it is blood the liver in particular receives and returns in particular,
and cut the coupling and neither organ, nor the function, survives. The parts are held by what each does *for
this other*, and there is no shared scalar that stands in for the coupling, so no partition preserves it. In
the specialized economy, the shoemaker and the grain farmer are bound by the market: the farmer does not need
this shoemaker, he needs shoes, and he reaches them through price, a public variable that summarizes the whole
supply of shoes and forgets which shoemaker made them. Deepen the division of labor as far as you like and this
stays true. A modern supply chain runs tens of thousands of specialized firms, each helpless without the
others, and you can still split it into regional chains, re-source a part, route around a failure, because the
coordination rides on prices and contracts — common variables every node reads and none owns. The dependence is
extreme and the seam is intact. Specialization made the members need each other more and did not make them
members.

So the intensity of interdependence is simply orthogonal to wholeness, and this is the finding Hegel's §198
already contains. He watched the division of labor deepen and the work grow mechanical, and he did not read the
deepening as an ascent toward the state. He read it as the external state perfecting itself as external — more
binding, more abstract, and no closer to being a unity its members inhabit. The market's answer to "how do I
get what I need from a thousand strangers" is a public price, and a unity carried by a public price is a unity
that lives outside the parts. The organism's answer is a specific circulation no member could read off a
number. The criterion tells the two apart, and Hegel told them apart, and both told them apart by the same
feature: not how much the parts depend on each other, but whether a common variable can carry the dependence
or a specific irreducible constraint must.

## The other register: what Hegel was actually complaining about

Now the guard, and it is the whole ethical weight of the section, so I will not soften it. Hegel's verdict that
civil society is the external state is a claim about its *form* — about the kind of unity it has. But that
structural claim is not the complaint Hegel spends the most pages on. His complaint is about what civil society
*does to people*, and that is a different claim in a different currency, and running the two together would be a
serious mistake.

Read the pages and the ethical charge is unmistakable. Civil society, Hegel writes, "affords a spectacle of
extravagance and want as well as of the physical and ethical degeneration common to them both."[^7] The system
of needs, left to itself, does not merely fail to be one thing. It polarizes. It heaps wealth on one side and,
on the other, drives a mass of people below the standard of life a member of this society is owed, and it does
this by its own working, not by accident. At §243–245 Hegel follows the mechanism to its end: the accumulation
of wealth on one side and, on the other, the deepening dependence and distress of the class tied to labor,
until a part of the population falls into what he calls the rabble.[^8] And the rabble, for Hegel, is not
defined by poverty. "Poverty in itself does not reduce people to a rabble," he says; the rabble is made by "the
disposition associated with poverty, by inward rebellion against the rich, against society, the
government."[^9] What civil society produces at its lower edge is not just want but the loss of the feeling of
right — people the society has failed so specifically that they no longer recognize its claims on them.

The pauperization is not, for Hegel, an accident that befalls the market from outside. It is the market's own
product. The same dynamic that piles up wealth on one side drives the dependence and distress on the other; the
two are one movement, and Hegel says as much when he traces the accumulation and the impoverishment to a single
process at §243. This is why the deficiency is a deficiency of the *form* of provisioning and not just bad luck
in the distribution — the system of needs, working as designed, generates a class it cannot secure. That
structural point about the ethical failure is easy to mistake for my structural point about wholeness, and the
mistake is the exact one to guard against. Hegel is saying the market structurally produces poverty. He is not
saying the market's poverty is what makes it non-whole. The first is a claim about what the economy does to
people; the second, which is mine, is a claim about the kind of unity the economy has. A market could be
engineered to distribute perfectly and would remain, on my criterion, precisely as external as before.

That is Hegel's deficiency claim, and it is economic and ethical to the core. Civil society fails because it
distributes badly and because it corrodes the freedom and standing of the people it impoverishes. It is a
failure at *distribution* and a failure at *freedom*. It is not, in the first instance, a failure to be
causally irreducible. My partition test is deaf to the whole of it. Run the criterion on the system of needs
and it reports "external unity, aggregate," and it reports the identical thing whether the distribution is just
or monstrous, whether the lowest members flourish or fall into the rabble, because distribution and standing
are exactly the variables the test brackets. The measure cannot see pauperization. It was not built to, and
pretending it can would be the worst thing this series could do — it would annex Hegel's ethical argument to a
structural one that has no room for it.

## Kain, and the remedy that makes the registers stay apart

Philip Kain has written the paper that keeps me honest here, and it is worth stating his correction precisely,
because it cuts against a reading I might otherwise drift into. The standard line on Hegel and civil society is
Marxist: Hegel saw the polarization and the pauperization clearly — saw them better than most — but had no
solution, and the honesty of his diagnosis only sets up Marx, who did. Kain rejects the second half of that.
On his reading it is "plausible to understand Hegel as proposing a solution, one that is similar to that of
social democrats, and one that could actually work."[^10] Civil society left to itself cannot cure its own
polarization. But civil society is never left to itself in Hegel: it comes with the police (in the old broad
sense, the public authority that regulates and provides), with the corporations, and above them with the
state, and with the assistance of those institutions, Kain argues, the deficiency is workable. Do not put "no
solution" in Hegel's mouth. That is the reading Kain is refuting, not the one he holds.

Kain matters to this post for a reason beyond fairness. His correction is what forces the two registers to stay
apart, and shows why merging them would falsify Hegel twice over. Look at what his remedy is a remedy *for*. The
police, the corporations, the redistributive hand of the state — these answer the *economic-ethical*
deficiency. They keep members from falling into the rabble; they secure standing and livelihood; they repair
the failure of distribution and freedom. They are a fix in the register of Hegel's ethical complaint. They are
not, and do not claim to be, a change in the *formal* status of civil society as external unity. A civil
society with a good welfare state and strong corporations still coordinates its economy through the market,
still binds its members by mutual need, still has its economic unity as a resultant they produce and consult
from outside. It reads, on my criterion, as it read before: interdependence whose unity is external. Fixing the
distribution does not fix the externality, because they were never the same defect.

Make it concrete with the case that tests it hardest. A Nordic social democracy distributes about as well as a
market economy has ever distributed: strong unions, a deep welfare state, corporations and public authorities
that keep almost no one in Hegel's rabble. In the register of the ethical complaint, this is close to Hegel's
own remedy realized — the police and the corporation and the state doing exactly the work Kain says they can
do. And in the register of my criterion it is unchanged. The economy still coordinates through markets, still
binds its members by mutual need, still carries its unity in prices and contracts every member reads from
outside. Split it between two rooms and the price mechanism runs in both. A just distribution repaired the
ethical deficiency and left the structural externality exactly where it was, because they were never the same
thing, and no amount of welfare converts a scalar-mediated aggregate into a whole its members inhabit as their
substance. That is the two registers made visible in one country: one defect fixed, one defect untouched, and
the fix for the first is no fix for the second.

So the two registers come apart cleanly under Kain's pressure. In one register, civil society is structurally
an aggregate — external unity, a *Verstandesstaat*, the two-rooms verdict — and that stays true no matter how
well it distributes. In the other register, civil society is ethically deficient — it pauperizes and it
corrodes freedom — and that deficiency has, per Kain, a workable social-democratic remedy that leaves the
structural status untouched. A reader who lets the structural reading swallow the ethical one gets Hegel wrong
in both directions: he makes the pauperization sound like a mere symptom of non-wholeness (it is not; a
well-distributing market is still non-whole), and he makes the non-wholeness sound curable by redistribution
(it is not; that is a fix in the other register). Keep them apart, and each stays true. Run them together and
both go false.

## Same boundary, two reasons

The registers stay apart, and yet they draw the same line, and that is the thing worth ending on because it is
easy to state loosely and important to state exactly.

Both the structural verdict and the ethical verdict put civil society on the near side of a boundary — not yet
the state, not yet the genuine whole, not the final home of freedom. My partition test puts it there because
its unity is external, a resultant that some cut preserves. Hegel's ethical argument puts it there because it
fails at distribution and freedom, produces the rabble, and needs the state above it to complete it. Same
place, two roads. And the roads do not merge, because the upgrade conditions differ. What my criterion would
count as crossing the boundary is a change in the causal structure: a coupling no partition preserves, unity
that stops being a shared scalar and starts being irreducible constraint. What Hegel counts as crossing it is
something else entirely — the state proper is not the external state made more tightly coupled but the external
state *raised* into a unity his members inhabit as their own substance, held by mutual recognition and ethical
life, freedom that knows itself as freedom in the whole. His upgrade condition is recognition, and beneath it
the inner teleology of an ethical life that is its own end. It is not higher integrated information. A causal
criterion neither entails it nor stands in for it.

This is the shape the whole series keeps finding, and civil society is its cleanest case. Hegel drew the
aggregate/configuration boundary before anyone had a measure for it, and he drew it in the right place. Reading
the political economists, he saw universal interdependence and refused to call it a whole, exactly as the
partition test refuses. That convergence is real and it is worth the nine posts. But the reason he refused, and
the condition under which he would have upgraded his verdict, belong to a register the measure cannot enter:
freedom, recognition, an ethical whole that is a subject and not a resultant. The measure and the man agree on
where the line falls and disagree on what the line is. On civil society, in 1821, that disagreement is small
enough to see past and real enough that seeing past it would be a mistake.

The disagreement is where the next posts live. Hegel does not leave civil society as a bare aggregate; he
builds inside it the corporation, the association where a trade gives its members standing and a second home,
and post 7 asks whether that is the market beginning to grow specific irreducible ties the price cannot carry —
whether the corporation is where the seam starts to close. And the state above it, post 8's subject, is where
Hegel's real upgrade condition finally speaks its name: freedom, being at home with oneself in the other, a
unity the members inhabit rather than consult. Civil society is the clean case because there the two registers
barely diverge. The corporation and the state are where they pull apart, and where the measure will have to say
plainly how much of Hegel it can follow.

Two things drew the same boundary. One counted what no cut preserves. The other asked whether a people could be
free and at home in the world its own labor built, and found, in the market, that it could not yet — and then
went looking, in the corporation and the state, for where it could. The boundary is the same. The question is
not.
