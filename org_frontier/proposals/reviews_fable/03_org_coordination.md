# Review 3 — Organizational coordination / classical organization theory

**Manuscript:** "When is a combination a configuration? Integrated information and the constitution of
organizational wholes"
**Venue:** *Organization Theory*, Special Themed Section "Theorizing the Configurational Nature of
Organizational Phenomena" (Catalysing & Crystallizing)
**Reviewer lens:** coordination theory and the classical canon — Thompson, Simon, Weick, Williamson, March

---

## Verdict

**MAJOR REVISIONS.** The essay has a genuine idea and belongs in this section, but its central novelty
claim — that the tradition has no within-case criterion for constitution — is stated against an incomplete
reading of the classical lineage, and the criterion itself carries a face-validity problem with
organizational redundancy that the essay implies but never confronts.

## Summary of the contribution

The essay proposes a within-case criterion for when a combination of organizational elements is one
configuration rather than an aggregate: model the arrangement as a small discrete dynamical system, and call
it a configuration when its cause-effect structure is irreducible under every partition of its elements. The
formal machinery is borrowed from integrated information theory (Φ, the minimum-information partition, the
complex), explicitly shorn of IIT's consciousness claims. From the criterion the essay derives three
conceptual packages: constitution (irreducibility, distinguished on small Boolean models from
interdependence, fit, wiring density, and co-occurrence), membership (the complex as a computed boundary,
with a graded core–periphery law tied to pivotality and the Shapley value), and dissolution (three
operators — substitution, bypass, constraint-lifting — with a distinction between necessary and contingent
irreducibility that gives power a formal seat). A closing section positions the criterion as a complement to
QCA and exhibits correspondences with cooperative game theory, platform economics, bargaining theory, and
communication theory.

The essay is a theory essay, not a methods paper, and it mostly holds that altitude; the models are framed
as instruments of theorizing in the Davis, Eisenhardt and Bingham (2007) sense. The fit with the C&C format
is good. The writing is unusually disciplined. My reservations are about the theory, and they are
substantial.

## Major issues

### 1. Thompson gave the tradition a within-case criterion, and the essay cites him only for the mediating technology

The gap claim in section 1 — "no within-case test decides, for a given arrangement, whether it is one
whole" — is the load-bearing sentence of the essay, and as written it does not survive contact with
Thompson (1967). Thompson did far more than name the mediating technology. Chapter 5 of *Organizations in
Action* is a within-case, ordinal account of exactly this question: pooled, sequential, and reciprocal
interdependence grade how tightly the parts of a single arrangement are bound, and each grade carries its
matched coordination mechanism (standardization, plan, mutual adjustment) and its design consequence (group
reciprocally interdependent units first). That is a within-case criterion of constitution, stated in 1967,
inside the canon the essay claims to extend.

What saves the essay is that its own results *refine* Thompson rather than merely rediscover him — but the
essay never says so, because it never engages the typology. Look at the manuscript's own examples through
Thompson's eyes. The quorum (section 3) is pooled interdependence coordinated by standardization: Thompson's
weakest grade, and Φ agrees, scoring interior thresholds at zero. The four-unit cycle is sequential
interdependence closed into a loop — which is to say, reciprocal at the level of the whole — and Φ agrees
again, reading it irreducible. The criterion recovers Thompson's ordering and then does something Thompson
could not: it puts a sharp boundary inside the pooled category (unanimity and any-one bind; every interior
threshold factors), and it computes the boundary of the interdependent set instead of assuming it. That is a
real contribution, and it is currently invisible because the ancestor is uncredited.

**Fix (paste-ready, for section 1, replacing the sentence at the end of the paragraph beginning "The gap
has consequences"):** "Thompson (1967) came closest to a within-case test: pooled, sequential, and
reciprocal interdependence grade how tightly a single arrangement binds its parts, and the grades carry
design consequences. But the typology is ordinal and coarse — it cannot say where inside the pooled category
binding begins, and it takes the roster of interdependent parts as given. A criterion that prices every
partition supplies both, and section 3 shows it recovering Thompson's ordering on the way."

### 2. The prior-art net misses two nearer rivals: Alchian–Demsetz nonseparability and epistemic interdependence

The essay checks its novelty claim against set-analysis, complementarity, fit, and NK — a fair list — but
two closer rivals go unmentioned, and a hostile referee will produce both.

First, Alchian and Demsetz (1972) *defined* team production by within-case nonseparability: output that "is
not a sum of separable outputs of each of its members." That is a criterion for when joint production is one
determination rather than an aggregate, and an entire theory of the firm stands on it. The essay's
"aggregate of smaller ones" language is the causal-dynamics rendering of the same idea. Second, Puranam,
Raveendran and Knudsen (2012) built the epistemic interdependence perspective on a within-case, formal,
organizational criterion: agent A is epistemically interdependent with B when A's optimal action requires
predictive knowledge of B's. That is recent, cited widely in the organization-design conversation this essay
wants to join, and it answers a version of "when are these parts jointly bound?" inside one arrangement.

The novelty claim survives, but it must narrow to what the borrowed formalism actually adds over these:
(a) gradation — Φ prices the cheapest cut rather than declaring nonseparability binary; (b) boundary-finding
— the complex computes *which* elements belong, which neither rival supplies; (c) exhaustive partition
search rather than pairwise dependence. Say this. A narrowed novelty claim that names its rivals is stronger
in this journal than a broad one that a referee can falsify from the classical reading list.

### 3. The redundancy problem: the criterion condemns exactly what organizations build on purpose

The quorum result is the essay's showpiece, and it cuts deeper than the essay admits. A
majority-rule committee has Φ of zero. But majority rule, redundancy, and slack are not incidental features
that organizations happen to have; they are what organizations deliberately build, because substitutability
is reliability. Landau (1969) made duplication and overlap a virtue of administrative systems; March and
Cyert made slack a condition of adaptation; and Perrow (1984) made the inverse point that tight coupling —
the essay's high-Φ regime — is where systems kill people. By the essay's criterion, virtually every large,
well-designed organization factors into small complexes, and "genuine configuration" becomes a property of
small, fragile, everyone-is-pivotal arrangements.

Is that a discovery or a reductio? I think it is defensible as a discovery — constitution and good design
are different properties, and the essay's own dissolution asymmetry (section 5) already implies that high
integration is high fragility — but the essay must own the inversion explicitly. As written, a reader
carrying the tradition's warm connotations for "configuration" (Miller's gestalts as achievements,
Siggelkow's fit) will conclude that the criterion misfires on the modal organization. The fix is a paragraph,
not a retreat: state that irreducibility is orthogonal to performance and often traded away on purpose, cite
Landau and Perrow, and reframe the rarity result of section 3 ("a tenth of the class") as consonant with
Simon's (1962) argument that near-decomposability is what survives. That last connection also earns Simon
properly: the essay's rarity finding is Simon's evolutionary conclusion, recovered computationally, and
crediting the convergence strengthens rather than weakens the borrowing.

**Fix (paste-ready, for the end of section 3):** "The rarity is not an embarrassment; it is Simon's (1962)
conclusion recovered from below. Near-decomposable arrangements dominate because substitutability is
reliability — Landau's (1969) redundancy, the slack that lets systems absorb failure — and full
irreducibility is the regime Perrow (1984) warned about, where every partition is expensive because every
part can bring down the whole. Constitution is not a virtue. It is a property, sometimes an achievement,
sometimes a vulnerability, and the criterion only reports which."

### 4. "Joint determination" holds the rules fixed; organizational actors rewrite the rules

The essay's answer to whether "joint determination" is a well-defined organizational concept is: it is
whatever the transition rules jointly fix. Within the model, that is well-defined. The organizational worry
is one level up. A partition "severs the constraints and replaces them with noise" — but in an organization,
severing a tie does not inject noise; it triggers mutual adjustment. Thompson made adaptive coordination the
residual mode precisely where programs fail; Weick made organizing the ongoing rebuilding of the rules, not
their execution. So a Φ verdict on a fixed transition system can misplace constitution in both directions:
an arrangement that factors on paper may be held whole by adaptive capacity the rules do not encode, and an
irreducible arrangement may dissolve gracefully because its members re-route. Section 7's encoding caveat
covers misdescription of the rules; it does not cover the endogeneity of the rules, which is a different and
deeper boundary. The essay needs one honest paragraph in section 7 conceding that the criterion evaluates
the coordination *regime in force*, a snapshot of programmed interdependence, and that constitution located
in re-programming capacity is outside the instrument's reach. This also sharpens what the criterion is good
for: platforms and algorithmic arrangements, where the rules genuinely are fixed on one side and enforced by
code, are the domain where the fixed-rule assumption is closest to true — which is a better argument for the
platform focus of sections 5 and 7 than the essay currently gives.

### 5. The Williamson mapping is off-target, and "search frictions" break the necessary/contingent dichotomy

Section 5 files make-versus-buy as a verbal sibling of constraint-lifting. The analogy is loose in a way a
transaction-cost reader will notice. Williamson's question is comparative governance cost under asset
specificity and opportunism; the essay's counterfactual — restore the forbidden tie, recompute, see whether
the intermediary stays — is closer to contestability and to the fundamental transformation than to the
make-or-buy margin. More damaging is the essay's own list of constraints that hold contingent conduits in
place: "franchise and licensing law, walled gardens and exclusive contracts, network standards, search
frictions." The first three are imposed constraints; search frictions are not. A middleman who economizes on
real search costs is doing integrating work in exactly Coase's and Williamson's sense — the direct tie is
logically available and economically ruinous. Filing that intermediary as a "contingent conduit held in the
core by an identifiable constraint" conflates computational necessity (the element computes a joint
condition no direct tie reproduces) with economic function (the element makes the tie affordable). The
retrodiction the essay is proud of — internet-era disintermediation — actually runs on the economic reading:
what the internet lowered was the *cost* of the direct tie, not a legal prohibition. The dichotomy should
become a relation: necessity is always relative to a stated counterfactual tie at a stated cost, and the
interesting empirical variable is the cost threshold at which a core member falls out. That is also where
the essay could finally meet Williamson squarely, since comparative cost of alternative governance is his
question, asked of the intermediary's position.

### 6. The essay's evidentiary spine is invisible

A theory essay may lean on models, but this one repeatedly cites specific computational results — ten
stylized cases with four verdict flips, an enumerated set of 660 strict-mediation forms, membership rising
from four in ten to nine in ten, a hand-assembled catalog of fifty-one intermediary types sorted into
quarters and halves — with no exhibit, no appendix, no citation, and no way for a reader or reviewer to
inspect any of it. The header promises every number "reproduces from the model's stated encoding," but no
encoding is stated for any of these aggregate claims (the section 3 vignettes are described qualitatively;
"strict mediation" is never defined). For this format the authors must choose: either (a) add one worked
figure — a single three-node transition table, its partitions, and its Φ — plus an online appendix carrying
the enumerations and the catalog, or (b) strip the counts and present the results as illustrative claims
about a model class. Option (a) is better and cheap; a C&C essay can carry one exhibit. As it stands, the
numbers perform precision the manuscript does not deliver, and that is the one place where the essay's
otherwise admirable candor about altitude slips.

## Minor issues

1. **Orton and Weick, engaged rather than conceded.** Section 3 concedes that Φ scales only the
   responsiveness axis of loose coupling. But Orton and Weick (1990) did not merely add a second dimension;
   they argued *against* collapsing coupling to a unidimensional scale, which is what "Φ prices the cheapest
   cut" does. One sentence acknowledging that the essay is doing the thing they warned about, and saying why
   it is worth doing anyway on the causal axis, would preempt the objection.
2. **"Dissolution by slack" wants Cyert and March.** The substitution operator's punchline uses "slack" as
   if coined; cite Cyert and March (1963) and gain an ancestor, since organizational slack as absorbed
   substitutability is exactly their concept.
3. **Thompson's buffering belongs in section 5.** Buffering the technical core is constraint-management in
   the essay's own sense — an organization maintaining the conditions of its own irreducible core — and would
   tie constraint-lifting back to the canon.
4. **March and Simon (1958) on coordination by plan versus feedback** is the programmatic ancestor of the
   fixed-rule modeling commitment in section 2 and should be cited where the commitment is introduced.
5. **The Miller "orchestrating themes" link overreaches.** Miller's central themes are interpretive
   (simplicity, focal commitments), not causal centrality; the Shapley reading in section 4 should be offered
   as an analogy, not an identification ("one formal rendering of what a central theme could be").
6. **"Strict mediation" (section 4) and the "transmit / transform / commit ladder" (section 6 table) are
   used before or without definition.** If the ladder is the authors' own construct, say so; Hancock et al.
   (2020) do not use that phrase.
7. **The microscope metaphor appears three times** (abstract, section 2, section 7). Once is a signature;
   three times is a drumbeat. Keep the abstract's.
8. **Abstract compression.** The sentence "The theory's complex names membership, pivotality grades cores
   and peripheries, and dissolution proceeds by substitution, bypass, or constraint-lifting…" is opaque to a
   reader who has not yet met the machinery; unpack it into two sentences or cut the middle clause.
9. **The five-move paragraph in section 5** (dashboard decoupling, contestability, extraction) is the
   densest in the manuscript and reads as compressed results from elsewhere; either give one move a full
   worked sentence or cut to three moves.
10. **References check out** where I can verify them (Thompson 1967, Simon 1962, Weick 1976, Orton & Weick
    1990, Williamson 1985, Lafontaine & Scott Morton 2010 are all accurately characterized in text). Add:
    Alchian & Demsetz (1972), Puranam, Raveendran & Knudsen (2012), Cyert & March (1963), Landau (1969),
    Perrow (1984), March & Simon (1958).

## Bottom line

The question is real, the borrowing is done with unusual care, and the dissolution section — especially the
necessary/contingent distinction and the self-liquidating *tertius iungens* — is the freshest configurational
theorizing I have read in some time. But the essay currently claims to fill a gap the canon partly filled:
Thompson graded within-case binding in 1967, Alchian and Demsetz defined nonseparability in 1972, and
Puranam and colleagues formalized within-case interdependence in 2012. The revision that would earn my
enthusiasm narrows the novelty claim to what Φ genuinely adds (gradation, computed boundaries, exhaustive
partitions), confronts the redundancy inversion head-on as a finding rather than leaving it as an
implication, repairs the necessary/contingent dichotomy where search costs break it, and puts one inspectable
exhibit under the numbers. All of that is achievable without changing the essay's architecture, which is why
this is major revisions and not rejection: the skeleton is sound; the lineage and the load-bearing joints
need the work.
