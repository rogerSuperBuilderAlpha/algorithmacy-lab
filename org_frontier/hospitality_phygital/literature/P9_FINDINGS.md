# P9 — the editor-grounding and final-corner research pass

Run 2026-08-11 as eight parallel refutation-default units against the rebuilt outline. Two purposes:
close every remaining corner of the argument, and build the material for the paper's revised
architecture — Pierre's claims, couched in the special-issue editors' own scholarship, then
professionalized by the general literatures.

Every item below is publisher- or Crossref-verified. Read depth is marked. Nothing here enters the
manuscript on a note alone. Crossref's API was proxy-blocked for several units; those verified
against publisher and institutional-repository pages instead, which is noted where it matters.

## Part 1 — what threatens the paper (act on these first)

### 1.1 Germann Molz 2026, and the end of the Riordan framing

Germann Molz, J. (2026), 'Guests without hosts: On the digital biopolitics of network hospitality',
*Hospitality & Society*, 16:1, pp. 63–82, doi 10.1386/hosp_00107_1. [abstract]

Airbnb read as "an inhospitable form of institutional and algorithmic governance" in which platform
systems render hosts visible, self-disciplining and ultimately erasable. Two figures carry it: the
absent Superhost and the mobile neighbour. The phrase also covers automated substitution of human
workers.

This is the most consequential finding of the pass. It sits in the target journal, in the issue
published five months before the submission window, written by the author of the journal's founding
network-hospitality line, and it occupies the paper's terrain. Riordan (2024) is no longer the
journal's nearest engagement with algorithmic mediation and the manuscript's framing sentences that
say so are now wrong.

The paper survives, and gains, on differentiation. Molz asks what becomes of hospitality when the
host is erased; the paper asks whether the party that took over the host's functions inherits the
host's obligation. Her title poses a question the paper answers. Her frame is biopolitical and
platform-specific; the paper's is architectural and normative, and it covers hotel and venue
intermediaries rather than network hospitality alone. Engagement belongs in §1 and §3, not in a
footnote.

Companion items from the same issue: Palmer, J. (2026), 'Afterword: Of thresholds and landslides in
an age of extremes', 16:1, pp. 99–112, doi 10.1386/hosp_00102_7 — engages Molz's account of
algorithmic hospitality and reads the threshold through Arendt; Salazar, N. B. (2026), 'Restorying
hospitality in times of biopolitics and polycrisis: A multi-scalar, more-than-human perspective',
16:1, pp. 83–97, doi 10.1386/hosp_00108_1 — hospitality as mutual care read up, down and sideways
across scales, which is the multi-scalar warrant §8's well-being levels have been missing.

### 1.2 The three near-misses on the host question

None kills it. Each must be cited and separated.

Lee, W. and Lu, L. (2024), 'The hospitable thought that counts: An emerging theory of "AI
consciousness" in genuine hospitality', *International Journal of Hospitality Management*, 123,
104928 (verify article number at proof), doi 10.1016/j.ijhm.2024.103928. [abstract] AI service
providers deliver genuine hospitality only insofar as guests attribute consciousness to them.
Attributional, not positional: it asks whether guests can experience the machine as hospitable, not
whether the machine occupies a position that owes.

Sharma, A. and Mattila, A. (2025), 'Rights and responsibilities of hospitality service robots',
*Journal of Hospitality & Tourism Research*, online 23 October 2025, doi
10.1177/10963480251393749. [full page] Puts robot responsibilities on the agenda as business
governance — liability, safety, data protection, consent — plus the ethics of human treatment of
robots. Same family as Kropf; no duty owed in virtue of arrival.

Liu, G. et al. (2026), 'Conceptualizing hospitableness in human–robot hospitality interactions',
*IJHM*, doi 10.1016/j.ijhm.2026.104640. [abstract] Robotic hospitableness as felt welcome produced
by design. Welcome as experiential output, not owed obligation. Already in the manuscript as the
construction that declines the position; the reading is confirmed.

Also verified: Mestre, J. (2025), 'Hospitality and the informational at-home', *Proc. ASIS&T*, doi
10.1002/pra2.1477 — Derridean hospitality in information ethics, with the *human* as host of the
informational at-home. The reversal Introna's machine-as-guest invites has not been made anywhere
located.

**Verified absence, multi-angle:** no source located 2010 to August 2026 theorizes the algorithmic
intermediary as occupying the host's position of obligation. Angles: machine/robot/artificial host;
Derrida plus AI, machine, welcome; algorithmic borders and thresholds; duty of care for service AI;
Telfer hospitableness plus AI; guest–host reconfiguration in the five hospitality and tourism
journals 2025–2026; full Crossref sweep of *Hospitality & Society* 2024–2026.

### 1.3 The seamlessness claim needs narrowing, twice

Batat's own corpus now contains a dark-side turn. Batat, W. (2026), 'The phygital intelligence value
experience (PHIVE) matrix: the good, the bad, and the ugly of AI marketing for customer experience
design', *Journal of Strategic Marketing*, doi 10.1080/0965254X.2026.2682414. [metadata only —
abstract unretrievable; **full text owed before submission**] The title alone establishes that the
lead editor theorizes negative valences of AI-driven phygital design. Add Batat, W. (2025), '"Binge
delivery" behaviors among digital natives: a phygital exploration of status, empowerment, and
vulnerability', *Qualitative Market Research*, 28:5, p. 941, doi 10.1108/QMR-08-2024-0153 [metadata]
— her own empirical evidence that frictionless phygital consumption produces vulnerability.

And Weaver, A. (2025), '"Fast hospitality" and technology: Contemporaneous connections between
"liquid" and "solid" in modern times', *Hospitality & Society*, doi 10.1386/hosp_00098_1
[abstract] — a Bauman-framed critique of technology-driven speed in hospitality, in the target
journal.

The claim survives in narrowed form, and the narrowing improves it: efficiency logics, technocentrism,
speed, and AI's harms have all been criticized, by Batat herself and in this journal. What has not
been questioned is seamlessness as a design value — the continuum, the fluidified journey, the
seamless merge, all of which remain stated goals in the frameworks even as their authors turn
critical about everything else. The manuscript should say that, and say it citing Batat against
Batat.

Confirming the gap from the newest framework: Mameli, E., Scarles, C., Stangl, B. and Frohlich, D.
(2026), 'A comprehensive framework for phygital tourism experiences', *Information Technology &
Tourism*, 28:1, doi 10.1007/s40558-026-00362-6 [full page] — 57 articles and 84 industry cases,
seamlessness treated as an unexamined goal, no friction discussion at all.

### 1.4 Mosca recommends concealment

Mosca, F. and La Rosa, E. (2019), '4.0 technology within fashion and luxury production',
*Symphonya*, 2/2019, doi 10.4468/2019.2.08mosca.larosa. [full] Managers see value in Industry 4.0;
customers hold negative attitudes toward technology in luxury production; the authors therefore
recommend keeping technological implementation undisclosed to preserve perceived authenticity. They
also insist Industry 4.0 "is not about complete automation but rather about human–machine
interaction."

An editor recommending invisibility is the live antagonist to the transparency affordance, and the
paper is better for meeting it. The turn is available in his own data: concealment is a response to
consumer distrust, which is a withholding of common understanding managed by hiding rather than by
answering. §6 should engage it directly.

### 1.5 A published competence decomposition

Zhou, L., Lei, X., Liu, M., Huang, X. and Hou, R. (2025), 'Algorithmic competency of on-demand labor
platform workers: scale development, antecedents, and consequences', *Asia Pacific Journal of Human
Resources*, 63:2, doi 10.1111/1744-7941.70004. Open access. [full, key sections]

The first validated scale of algorithmic competency, on five samples of Chinese platform workers,
with four dimensions: understanding, embracing, leveraging and remediating algorithmic management.
Understanding is folk theorizing in all but name ("I develop theories about task-allocating and
score-calculating algorithms based on my own experience"). Remediating includes using recorded
evidence "to appeal to the AM."

Must be cited. Three differentiators hold. Their construct is worker-only and gig-platform-specific.
Embracing is an attitude — willingness to trust — not a competence, which is why the scale cannot
carry a normative argument. And remediating folds appeal capacity into individual competency, which
is precisely the conflation §5 exists to undo: the capacity to lodge an appeal is not the standing to
be answered. The collision is usable as the foil that motivates coordinative sovereignty.

Supporting the differentiation: Gagrčin, E., Naab, T. K. and Grub, M. F. (2024), 'Algorithmic media
use and algorithm literacy: an integrative literature review', *New Media & Society*, doi
10.1177/14614448241291137 [abstract] — 169 studies, and the field still "lacks defined core
competencies," with a call for task- and domain-specific approaches. That call licenses a
hospitality-specific decomposition. Neither Dogruel's four components (awareness, knowledge, critical
evaluation, coping or influencing skills) nor Swart's cognitive-affective-behavioural triad nor
Cotter's practical-knowledge formulation names specification or tracking. Those two remain the
paper's own.

### 1.6 A published checkable condition, of a different kind

Hemmer, P., Schemmer, M., Vössing, M. and Kühl, N. (2025), 'Complementarity in human-AI
collaboration: concept, sources, and evidence', *European Journal of Information Systems*, doi
10.1080/0960085X.2025.2475962. [abstract] Formalizes complementary team performance: the human-AI
pair must beat both the human alone and the AI alone; sources are information and capability
asymmetry; the evidence review finds complementarity rare.

Cite and differentiate. Theirs is an ex-post performance test on organization-internal decision
tasks. §4's is an ex-ante diagnostic at the guest-facing touchpoint, built on coordination
requirements, and it turns on answerability to a party outside the organization — a dimension a
performance test has no place for.

Second-order: Yuan, Y., Hao, F. and Liu, S. (2026), 'Augment or replace? AI implementation and
employee job insecurity', *International Journal of Contemporary Hospitality Management*, 38:8, pp.
2503–2522, doi 10.1108/IJCHM-08-2025-1290 [abstract] — stipulates the augment/replace distinction as
an experimental manipulation rather than deriving it, and finds augmentation also raises insecurity,
which supports the non-separability the paper inherits from Raisch and Krakowski.

### 1.7 Two §2 absences must be narrowed

**Guest obligations.** Manfreda, A. and Harkison, T. (2025), 'Beyond exchange: decoding reciprocal
hospitableness in luxury lodge experiences', *Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Management*, 62,
pp. 173–187, doi 10.1016/j.jhtm.2025.01.011. [abstract; precursor conference paper read in full]
Multiple-case study of New Zealand luxury lodges proposing reciprocal hospitableness across guests,
hosts, staff, communities and environments, with concrete guest-side behaviours — tidying for
housekeeping, carrying glasses back to the bar, praising staff in reviews. The authors explicitly
frame these as gratitude-driven reciprocation rather than formal obligation or indebtedness.

The unqualified absence is dead. The narrowed one is stronger: no one theorizes what the guest
*owes*, as distinct from what the grateful guest happens to give back. Cite Manfreda and Harkison as
the nearest occupant of the ground.

**The instrument.** Shi, F., Han, X. and Samaniego-Chávez, C. E. (2025), 'Residents' perceived
benefits of host-guest interaction: scale development and validation', *Journal of Travel Research*,
64:4, pp. 950–965, doi 10.1177/00472875231224238. [abstract] A validated five-dimension scale —
emotional lift, local pride, altruism, destination attraction, self-development — measuring what
residents gain from interacting with tourists. That is a guest-to-host flow, measured.

Narrow to guest conduct toward a commercial host. Two further threats to watch: Liu, Y., Li, F. S.,
Zhang, G. and Zhang, J. (2025), 'Warm hearts, happy lodgings: how peer-to-peer accommodation
micro-entrepreneurs can foster sharing citizenship behavior', *Journal of Hospitality & Tourism
Research*, 49:6, pp. 1081–1095, doi 10.1177/10963480241265786 [abstract] — in peer-to-peer
accommodation the micro-entrepreneur is the host, so guest citizenship behaviour there is de facto
host-directed; item wording is owed before §2 makes any claim. And the customer-incivility
measurement tradition measures guest-to-employee conduct with validated instruments, so the sentence
must specify positive or obligational constructs, and the host rather than the employee.

Clean for the paper: Groth 2005 and Yi and Gong 2013 target the firm and other customers; the Blain
and Lashley hospitableness instruments, the Ariffin and Maghzi and Pijls scales, and Wu et al.'s
community citizenship scale all measure host disposition or guest perception of the host.

### 1.8 The §8 absence holds

No study located links hospitality automation or AI to both individual-level and collective or
community-level well-being. Angles: service robots and community well-being; AI hospitality and
resident well-being; automation and destination well-being; technology and host-community quality of
life; micro-meso-macro well-being under algorithmic management.

Three citations now carry the claim rather than assert it. Moganadas, S. R., Goh, G. G. G., Cheah, C.
S. and Shidik, G. F. (2026), 'Navigating employee well-being in the age of digital transformation: a
PRISMA-based systematic review', *Societies*, 16:7, 213, doi 10.3390/soc16070213 [full, open access]
— 57 articles, 2014 to 2025, covering AI and algorithmic systems, and staying entirely at the
individual level, with fragmentation named as the field's own diagnosis. Dar, H., Singh, K. and
George, B. (eds) (2026), *Tourism Technology, Sustainability, and Local Community Empowerment*,
Routledge [description] — smart tourism technology tied to community empowerment, with no individual
well-being outcome. Park, S., Lee, J. Z. and Lehto, X. Y. (2026), 'Transforming hotel lobbies via
community-centered design', *Journal of Hospitality & Tourism Research*, 50:2, pp. 170–187, doi
10.1177/10963480241305760 [abstract] — guest well-being and sense of community in one study, joined
by physical design and not by automation. The two levels can meet; automation is what has not joined
them.

## Part 3 — the general layer, section by section

New material that professionalizes the argument once it has been stated and couched.

**§3, discretion and authority.** Spektor, F., Fox, S. E., Min, S., Sarfo, G., Stringam, B., Riordan,
C. A., Rho, H. J., Begleiter, B. and Forlizzi, J. (2025), 'Working together: algorithmic management
and peer relationships in the hospitality industry', *Proc. DIS 2025*, doi 10.1145/3715336.3735704
[abstract] — unionized US hotel housekeeping; the tool disrupts coordination even when carefully
configured. Krzywdzinski et al. (2024), *New Technology, Work and Employment* — centralization of

'Algorithmic management in organizations? From edge case to center stage', *Annual Review of

ambiguously inside or outside organizational boundaries, which is the scaffolding for §4's claim that
the guest stands outside and so needs Bovens rather than Okhuysen and Bechky's colleague-facing
accountability.

**§4, predictability.** Hatherley, J. (2025), 'A moving target in AI-assisted decision-making: dataset
shift, model updating, and the problem of update opacity', *Ethics and Information Technology*, 27,
20, doi 10.1007/s10676-025-09829-2. [abstract] Names update opacity as a distinct kind: users cannot
know how or why an update changed the system's reasoning, and standard explainability remedies do not

A. K. (2026), 'Strategic responses to algorithmic recommendations: evidence from hotel pricing',
*Management Science*, 72:1, pp. 609–626, doi 10.1287/mnsc.2022.03740 [abstract] — the pricing

upgrade. Huang, Z. and Lo, A. (2025), *Information Technology & Tourism*, 27:2, pp. 417–448, doi
10.1007/s40558-025-00314-6 [full results] — human preference contingent on failure type, process

coordination-shaped. Schmidt, A. L. et al. (2025), *Strategic Change*, 34:4, pp. 559–575, doi
10.1002/jsc.2637 [abstract] — a rare field experiment, 200 guests, human and robot simultaneously
available at an information desk, and indifference between them: a touchpoint where all three
conditions survive.

**§5, competence and standing.** Lin, W., Zhang, M., Zhang, W. and Zhang, C. (2026), 'Will employees

10.1080/09585192.2026.2699267 [abstract] — enforced silence as the mechanism. Lin, H. (2025),

[abstract] — folk-theoretic competence not converting into power, the sharpest recent statement of

*Big Data & Society*, doi 10.1177/20539517241270663 [abstract] — resignation as structurally

**§6, the affordances.** Fink, M. (2025), 'Human oversight under Article 14 of the EU AI Act', SSRN

M. et al. (2025), 'Identifying algorithmic decision subjects' needs for meaningful contestability',
*Proc. ACM HCI* 9 (CSCW), doi 10.1145/3757415 [abstract] — 21 interviews with short-term rental

adjacent, and the strongest new ally. Choi, J. and Chao, M. M. (2024), *Personality and Social
