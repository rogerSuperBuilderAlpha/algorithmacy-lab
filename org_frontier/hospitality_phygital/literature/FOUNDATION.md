# Literature foundation — dissertation library pass

First research pass for the *Hospitality & Society* paper, run on 2026-08-07 against the private
dissertation library (`dissertation/research/library/`, 1,766 reference cards) with the CFP
([`../cfp/`](../cfp/)) and the locked abstract ([`../ABSTRACT.md`](../ABSTRACT.md)) as the targets.

**Method.** Thirteen theme patterns — hospitality, triad, algorithmacy, sovereignty, contestability,
transparency, discretion, algorithmic management, exclusion, seamlessness, personalization,
surveillance, service — scored across every card; the top-ranked cards in each theme read for their
relation-to-argument and verdict sections; transfer judged against what this paper must argue.

**Provenance rule.** Everything below cites published work. No private dissertation prose enters
this file — the library supplied the map, and the sources it points to are public. See
[`../DISSERTATION_REVIEW.md`](../DISSERTATION_REVIEW.md) for the log.

## Five findings that change the paper

### 1. The platform-hospitality gap is already closed — in hospitality journals

The gap flagged in [`field_map.md`](field_map.md) turns out to be stocked. The library holds eleven
cards on Airbnb-as-hospitality, and they sit in venues an *H&S* reviewer reads: *International
Journal of Hospitality Management* (twice), *Tourism Management*, *Geoforum*, *Socio-Economic
Review*, *Environment and Planning A*, *Philosophy & Technology*, *Marketing Letters*, and CHI.

Cheng and Foley (2019) name short-term-rental hosting as algorithmically managed work *in IJHM*.
Roelofsen and Minca (2018) theorize the Superhost badge as a device that grades the host and
withholds any seat at the table. Liang et al. (2017) measure why hosts chase the badge — it gates
search visibility and bookings. The paper does not need to import this literature from outside the
field; it needs to cite it.

### 2. The strongest evidence answers the title question from the *host's* side, not the guest's

This is the finding that should reshape the argument. The dissertation's Airbnb cluster documents
one structure repeatedly: the platform captures the listing, the calendar, and the guest
relationship, steers the host through pricing and ranking, and confers no standing — no employment,
no voice, no authority over the rules that govern the listing. Bosma (2022) traces hosts investing
labor and assets into a livelihood while gaining no claim on governance. Leick et al. (2024)
replicate it in rural markets. Zervas et al. (2021) and Teubner et al. (2019) show the reputation
that gates a host's visibility does not travel, so leaving is costly by design. Ladegaard (2021)
shows the platform offloading residual risk onto hosts, who absorb it through unpaid emotional
labor.

*Who hosts the guest?* has an evidenced answer for the case where the host is a person: the
algorithm has already taken much of the hosting function, and the human host holds competence
without standing. The guest-agency claim the abstract leads with is the *less* evidenced half.

That does not weaken the paper — it tells it where to put its weight. The triad's most defensible
empirical anchor is the redistribution of authority between algorithm and host, with guest agency
argued from that structure rather than asserted alongside it. It also strengthens the frontline
employee as co-equal with the guest, which [`../BRIDGE.md`](../BRIDGE.md) already commits to and
which the CFP explicitly invites ("power structures and guest-staff relationships").

### 3. Three of the five affordances have published nulls against their naive form

The design section cannot propose transparency, contestability, and explanation as though the
literature were silent about whether they work. It is not silent, and the findings are negative.

| affordance | what the literature already shows | what the paper must do |
|---|---|---|
| Transparency | Ananny and Crawford (2018): disclosure supplies visibility while leaving the forum absent; accountability is a relation, not a view. Burrell (2016) separates chosen secrecy from method-intrinsic opacity, so a property can withhold at one layer and blame another | Specify transparency **at the point of effect and paired with standing**, exactly as `design_principles.md` already hints — and cite the null rather than walking into it |
| Contestability | Vaccaro et al. (2020): an in-platform appeal, tested against a no-appeal baseline with outcome held constant, produced no lift in perceived accountability. Users contested the goal and the automation, not just the output | A hotel-internal appeal **cannot** be claimed to confer standing. Contestability has to reach the rule, or route outside the party that wrote it |
| Explanation | Edwards and Veale (2017) call the individual right to an explanation a "transparency fallacy" and push toward systemic, ex-ante instruments | Pitch the affordance as design obligation and audit, not as a guest's right to ask why |

Alfrink et al. (2023) and Almada (2019) supply the constructive alternative — contestability
designed across the lifecycle rather than bolted on — with the standing caveat intact.

This is the sharpest thing the library gives the paper. It converts section 6 from a plausible
checklist into an argument that survives a reviewer who knows the field.

### 4. Hospitality algorithmacy has already been observed, in a hospitality setting

Jhaver, Karpfen and Antin (2018) studied Airbnb hosts at CHI and found precisely the competence the
paper names: hosts reverse-engineer search ranking, build folk theories of the algorithm, compare
notes socially, and orient two audiences at once — the guest and the ranking system. They call the
affect that drives it algorithmic anxiety.

Around it sits the folk-theory cluster that gives the construct its general form: Eslami et al.
(2016) on how opacity provokes theory-building even with nothing economic at stake; DeVito et al.
(2017) on collective resistance when an opaque system changes; Bishop (2019) on algorithmic gossip
as semi-institutionalized reverse-engineering; Cotter (2019) on the visibility game, whose framing —
the algorithm "structures, but does not unilaterally determine" — matches this paper's altitude
exactly; Ytre-Arne and Moe (2021) on folk theories at representative-sample scale, showing the
response is a property of the mediation form rather than of precarity.

Hospitality algorithmacy is therefore not a construct the paper invents. It is a competence the
literature has documented under other names, which this paper specifies for the hospitality triad
and extends from hosts to guests. That is a stronger claim to make than novelty, and an easier one
to defend.

### 5. Lipsky inverts on the way into hospitality

The dissertation logs Street-Level Bureaucracy (Lipsky 1980) as **orthogonal**, with a precise
reason: the platform worker maps onto Lipsky's *client*, not onto the discretion-holding bureaucrat,
because the worker stands outside the organization whose rules bind them.

In hospitality that mapping reverses. A hotel's frontline employee *is* an employee of the
organization whose policy they implement, paid to exercise judgment on its behalf — Lipsky's
discretion-holder in the original sense. A source the dissertation had to rule out becomes
load-bearing here, and it supplies the vocabulary for what substitutive hospitality removes.
Christin (2017) then supplies the mechanism: an algorithmic instrument arriving as a substitute for
professional judgment, relocating the categories of judgment from the practitioner's discretion into
a vendor's model.

### Two constructs the library hands over intact

**Torque** (Bowker and Star 1999) names what happens to a person whose life does not fit the
category pressing on them. That is the guest whose needs fall outside algorithmically constructed
norms — the paper's own phrase — with thirty years of scholarship behind it.

**Voice as a separable good** (Folger 1977) is the empirical warrant for the anti-seamlessness
argument. Folger measured voice reducing experienced injustice *independent of outcome improvement*.
A frictionless encounter that produces good outcomes while removing the surface on which to be heard
is therefore not merely less relational as a matter of theory; withholding voice inflicts a
measurable harm that better outcomes do not offset. Section 7 should lead on this.

## What the library does not supply

The pass closes three of the four gaps in [`field_map.md`](field_map.md). One remains, and one new
gap opens.

- **Guest-side evidence is thin.** Edelman et al. (2017) is the principal guest-side hospitality
  study; Dujarier (2016) covers the customer as enrolled and working unpaid. Everything else reaches
  the guest through classification theory that is not hospitality-specific. **Acquisition target:**
  guest-facing studies of algorithmic mediation in accommodation — app check-in, facial recognition
  at the threshold, CRM-driven room assignment, reputation-mediated refusal.
- **Hospitality's own theorizing line stays outside this library.** The dissertation has no reason
  to hold Lashley, Derrida, or the *H&S* line, and it does not. Section 2 still needs its own
  acquisition pass; the seeds in [`references.bib`](references.bib) are the start, not the finish.
- **Employee-side hospitality specificity.** The algorithmic-management evidence is strong but drawn
  from ride-hail, freelancing, and short-term rental. Hotel PMS, revenue management, and scheduling
  need their own sources before the employee half can claim hospitality grounding rather than
  analogy.

## Citation provenance and verification status

Every source above carries a DOI or stable identifier from the library card. Four
hospitality-cluster entries — Cheng and Foley (2019), Roelofsen and Minca (2018), Liang et al.
(2017), Leick et al. (2024) — were spot-verified against Crossref on 2026-08-07 and matched exactly
on title, authors, journal, volume, and pages. The remaining entries inherit the library's own
verification and are marked `inherited` in [`references.bib`](references.bib); resolve each DOI
before manuscript lock, per [`../PLAN.md`](../PLAN.md) §5.B.5.

The library records a read-depth per card. Several load-bearing sources here are held at
`abstract_only` depth — Cheng and Foley, Roelofsen and Minca, Liang et al., Ytre-Arne and Moe,
Morrison, Folger, Cheney-Lippold, Burrell. **Read the full text of any source the paper leans on
before citing it for a specific claim.** The library's relation notes are a map, not a substitute.
