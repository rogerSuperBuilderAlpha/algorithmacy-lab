# Who Hosts the Guest? Reconfiguration of Hospitality in Phygital Spaces

*Short draft, 2026-08-09. One paragraph per section, with the sources each section leans on.*

## 1. Introduction

Hospitality now runs through hybrid physical-digital environments, the field has frameworks for
them, and service research has stopped modelling the encounter as a dyad: technology, employee and
customer as three roles; the service triad tested empirically; the encounter triad in this field's
own review of AI service; co-creation recast as a hybrid of customer, employee and AI system. That
the guest meets a third party is not in dispute. What the third party is called has held steady
across all of it — infused tool, automated social presence, mediator, facilitator, co-creator,
algorithmic actor — and each of those names something the technology does, while none names
something it owes. Hospitality does
not distribute its duties evenly among the parties present: a host owes a welcome, and no employment
relation creates that duty or discharges it. Service research filled the position marked employee
and left the one marked host empty. The consequence falls on the guest. Hospitality confers
standing, a position from which she may ask, decline, explain herself and be received as an
exception, and where an intermediary recognizes her, routes her, prices her stay and settles what
she may ask for, that standing has no counterparty. Is the third party a host? We take the triadic
structure as given and press that question, distinguishing augmentative from substitutive
hospitality as a condition a reader can check against a real touchpoint, separating the competence
guests and employees develop from the standing only institutions confer, deriving five affordances
from what the mediation withholds, and asking whether seamlessness is hospitable at all. The move
puts algorithmic systems in the position of constitutive mediator and gives operators a diagnostic
that classifies a touchpoint instead of an intention.

## 2. Hospitality beyond seamless service

Hospitality confers standing on the guest. Lashley's three domains put commercial hospitality
downstream of the domestic and the social, so a hotel inherits its obligations from the household
and from the rules governing how strangers are received, not from the service contract. What the
guest gets is a position she can act from: she may ask, decline, explain herself, be received as an
exception. Welcome, recognition, care, presence and negotiated access are the names the field gives
to the parts of that position. It would be easy to make this sound purely ethical, and Lynch
complicates the move: his
study of ordinary welcome finds the tactical and the ethical running together, and he ends by
calling himself
pessimistic about hospiety. The standing is real whichever register produces it, and it amounts to
negotiating room. Bulley locates where that room can be taken away. Reading Derrida, he argues the threshold is
the wrong place to look, because the host's authority is not spent at the door but exercised over
the guest continuously inside. A guest can be admitted and still be given no room at all.

## 3. Phygital hospitality as triadic mediation

Service research has modelled the encounter as three-party since 2017, and has rebuilt the model
several times since: technology, employee and customer as three roles (Larivière and colleagues);
the service triad tested empirically (Odekerken-Schröder and colleagues); the encounter triad in
this field's own review of AI service (Li and colleagues); co-creation recast as a hybrid of
customer, employee and AI system (Gursoy). We take the structure as given. What holds across all of
it is the name given to the third party: infused tool, automated social presence, mediator,
facilitator, co-creator, algorithmic actor. Each of those names something the technology does; none
names something it owes. Belanche and colleagues come closest by asking whether customers blame a
robot after a
failure, and blame assigned by a customer is a long way from a duty owed to one. Machine ethics has
come nearer still, arguing that care robots hold role responsibility from the social function they
occupy, and cataloguing which obligations fail to travel when agency moves to machines; the paper
stands on that work and marks the difference, since care is owed in virtue of need and welcome in
virtue of arrival. Hospitality has
never spread its obligations evenly across everyone present. A host owes a welcome; no employment
contract creates that duty and none discharges it. The literature compares machine and employee
constantly; what it has not examined is whether the employee was ever the right template, because
the host does not appear in its repertoire. Phygitalization meanwhile runs both ways,
digitalizing the physical encounter and physicalizing the digital one, and the guest crosses back
and forth many times in one stay. Where the phygital frameworks see fluidity we see a relay. Who
holds authority at each handover?

## 4. Augmentative and substitutive hospitality

The same mediation produces opposite outcomes. Augmentative hospitality extends the host's capacity
to notice and respond while leaving guest and employee room to shape the encounter. Substitutive
hospitality puts algorithmic direction where relational judgement used to be, and hospitality gets
performed as compliance. Stated that way the distinction is a mood. Stated as a conjunction of
withholdings it becomes checkable: an encounter runs substitutive where the guest cannot find out
what was decided, the rule moves without announcement, and nothing answers to her. Break any one of
the three and the encounter runs the other way. A diagnostic falls straight out of it. For any
touchpoint: what does the algorithm decide, what does the guest see of it, what can the employee
override, and what does refusal cost her? Mobile check-in, algorithmic room assignment, the AI
concierge and the reputation-mediated upgrade denial each answer differently, and most properties
mix both tendencies across their touchpoints.

## 5. Hospitality algorithmacy and coordinative sovereignty

Reading the screen is not enough inside a triad whose determinations bind. Guests and employees
develop a situated competence we call hospitality algorithmacy, the hospitality face of a broader
competency for coordinating through an algorithmic intermediary: inferring from a room assignment
what the system decided, compressing a real need into the fields a booking form will carry, noticing
that last stay's trick has stopped working. Platform hosts already reverse-engineer search ranking
and play to two audiences at once, and the folk-theory literature describes the general shape. We are specifying it for hospitality, not discovering it. The competence then
hits a wall that no amount of skill gets over. Accountability is a relation between parties, not a
state a party can be in, so an expert guest cannot conjure a forum where none exists. Hence the
second construct. Coordinative sovereignty is the standing a participant holds inside a mediation
they cannot exit, and institutions grant it. Our clearest case is a hotel where room attendants can
reorder their own assignments. That affordance is not a feature. The union bargained for it.

## 6. Five affordances of guest-centric phygital design

The affordances follow from what the mediation withholds. Any coordinating arrangement owes its
parties common understanding, predictability and accountability; transparency, adjustability,
contestability and human accessibility pay those debts, and bypass-ability is the exit case. Each
has to be specified in a form that survives its own published null. Disclosure delivers visibility
and leaves the forum empty. An internal appeal, tested against a no-appeal baseline, moved perceived
accountability not at all. The individual right to an explanation has been called a transparency
fallacy. So transparency comes paired with standing, contestability reaches the rule instead of
looping back to whoever wrote it, and explanation binds the designer rather than waiting on a guest
who thinks to ask. Human accessibility has a floor in the evidence: guests want a person for
emotional work and after service failure, though not uniformly. These affordances are not all bought
the same way. Transparency and human accessibility can
be designed and trained. Contestability cannot, because somebody has to constitute the forum, and a
hotel that treats it as an interface problem will ship an appeal button that answers to nobody.

## 7. Against frictionless hospitality

Seamlessness is a stated commitment of the frameworks this special issue builds on. Batat presents
phygital experience as fluidifying the customer's journey, and the newest statement of
transformative phygital service research aims its criticism at opacity and rigidity while leaving
smoothness alone. Lynch observes that welcome works like social oil and gets noticed only when it
fails. An encounter engineered never to fail is one nobody notices, and contestation of the
unnoticed attaches to no agreed point. The voice evidence is conditional in a way that serves the
argument. Voice keeps value even when it cannot change the outcome (Lind and colleagues), while an
invitation to speak that never bears on anything reads as pseudo-participation and backfires
(Folger; Goodwin and Ross). What frictionless design withholds is the occasion itself, and whether
an occasion pays depends on whether anything is disposed to listen. Infrastructure works by sinking out of sight, and seamlessness is that sinking pursued on
purpose. The guest whose requirements fall outside the categories then meets torque, with no surface
on which to be received as an exception, because the negotiation that would have surfaced her has
been designed away. The fix for algorithmic exclusion often runs through the instrument that caused
it: more reputational data reduces discrimination, deepens the mediation, and moves the disadvantage
onto the guest with no record at all.

## 8. Discussion and contributions

Algorithmic systems belong in the category of constitutive mediator, not neutral service technology.
The system recognizes the guest, routes her and admits her. What does it owe her? Three things
follow for operators. The section 4 diagnostic lets a manager classify an actual touchpoint instead
of debating whether the property is guest-centric. The section 6 derivation tells her which
affordances she can buy with design and training and which need an institution built, a distinction
phygital transition programmes currently miss. And the seamlessness argument names a design goal the
industry is chasing at its own expense. A transformation roadmap follows from those three, running
from touchpoint audit to affordance specification to constituting a forum. Well-being sits at two
levels: the affordances move agency, discretion and trust inside the encounter, and through those
they reach well-being at individual and collective scale, where belonging is one component. The
evidence carries positive, negative and conditional effects, and at population scale it reaches
labour displacement and exclusion. The argument is conceptual: no new field measurement, every
affordance-to-outcome link untested in hospitality, and a literature that documents the back of
house far better than the front desk.

## 9. Conclusion

Guest-centric phygital hospitality does not depend on making the technology invisible. It depends on
guests and employees keeping some say in how the technology takes part, and on organizations
accepting that a system now doing the host's work has not thereby taken on the host's obligations.
Somebody still owes the guest a welcome.
