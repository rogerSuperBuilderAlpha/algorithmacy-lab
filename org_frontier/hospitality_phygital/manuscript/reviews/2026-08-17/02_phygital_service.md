# Reviewer 02 — Davit Sarkisyan · phygital service research

## 1. Who I am

I work inside the frameworks this paper argues past. I publish on phygital customer experience and
extended reality in service settings, I sit on the reading committee for one of the agenda pieces
the paper cites, and I have written two of the sentences it is complaining about. I came to this
manuscript expecting the usual thing: a humanities argument that treats service research as a straw
man, quotes an abstract, and calls the field naive. That is not what I found, and I want to say so
before I start listing what I would change. The core claim of Section 7 is correct. I checked all
four of the works it names and the claim holds against every one of them. What I object to is
narrower and more fixable: the paper proves its point from the thinnest evidence available to it
when better evidence sits in the same documents, and it under-cites the one piece of my field's own
work that anticipates two of its three contributions.

## 2. The read

### [FIX] Shabnam et al. is cited once, in a subordinate clause, and it is the closest thing to a rival the paper has. Cost to fix: ~70 words added.

Section 7 introduces the transformative phygital agenda as background scenery: "the field's own
research agendas criticize the opacity and rigidity of algorithmic systems (Shabnam et al. 2026)".
That characterization is accurate — I verified it, see part 6 — but it is also the only appearance
Shabnam et al. make in the manuscript, and it badly undersells what they say. Their abstract states
that "key ambiguities persist around agency, governance and interpretive labour in algorithmically
mediated interactions" and that "[e]xisting frameworks address value co-creation but lack clarity
on how digitally enforced rules and redistributed labour drive transformation".

Read that next to Section 3 (a system redistributes knowledge, discretion and authority) and
Section 5 (employees lend guests an interpretive competence that the system's categories require).
A referee from my field will put those side by side within thirty seconds and ask why the paper
does not engage its nearest neighbour. The answer, I think, is that the paper is stronger for the
comparison and does not know it: TPSR names the ambiguity and stops, whereas this paper asks which
party inherits the obligation once the rules have done the redistributing. That is a real
difference and one sentence establishes it. Leaving Shabnam et al. in a §7 aside instead reads, to
me, like a paper that met the agenda piece late and slotted it where it fit.

Cite it in Section 3, where the paper states the gap, so that the gap belongs to my field rather
than to the author. Draft sentences in part 4.

### [FIX] The Mameli absence claim is true but proved the hard way. Cost to fix: ~30 net words (a swap, not an addition).

Section 7 asserts that "the most recent integrative framework for phygital tourism, built from 57
studies and 84 industry cases, contains no discussion of friction at all (Mameli et al. 2026)".
I searched the article. The claim is correct — "friction" and "frictionless" do not occur. But an
absence claim about an open-access article is the most checkable sentence a referee will ever be
handed, and it is doing work that a positive quotation would do better. Mameli et al. write that
"[p]hygital events employ technology to create seamless interactions between people and spaces,
enhancing the experience of moving between digital and physical settings". That sentence is your
evidence. It shows smoothness stated as an outcome to be engineered, in a 2026 integrative
framework, in the field's own words — which is exactly the currency argument the paragraph needs,
and it cannot be refuted by a reader with a search box.

I will add one thing a phygital referee would want noticed. Mameli et al. are not silent about
downsides: they report that "[o]nly 5 studies have discussed the potential risks and challenges
associated with phygital solutions", and they list implementation cost, novelty fatigue, privacy
and accessibility. The paper's hedge — "the field is far from uncritical" — already covers this, so
nothing breaks. Say it in the swap and the paragraph gets stronger rather than merely safer.

### [FIX] "It has not yet been examined as one" is refuted by your own reference list. Cost to fix: ~8 words.

The sentence reads: "Smoothness itself, however, has remained a stated goal, and it has not yet
been examined as one." Four lines later the paper cites Phillips et al. (2024), 'The Physical
Frictionless Experience: A Slippery Slope for Experience Memorability of Retail Services?', which
is an interview study of thirty customers in a simulated just-walk-out environment asking precisely
whether removing effort costs the experience something — and finding that brief transactional
contact with staff is what makes a service encounter memorable. Padigar et al. sit in the same
list, theorizing good and bad frictions in a marketing journal. Neither is phygital hospitality,
and the claim survives if it is scoped. Unscoped, it hands a referee a free objection with the
citation already supplied. Write "has not yet been examined within phygital hospitality" or
"as a design value in these frameworks" and the problem disappears.

### [CONSIDER] The friction argument is a transposition. It also has an increment, and the increment is unstated. Cost: ~55 words.

You asked me to judge whether Section 7 contributes or restates HCI. Honestly: as a design
argument, it restates. Chalmers and Galani made the case for revealing seams in 2004, and Ehsan et
al. (2024) already transferred it to algorithmic systems, arguing for "strategically revealing (and
concealing) seams to support user agency, re-configuration, and appropriation". A referee who knows
that literature will read Section 7's closing sentence — a four-citation bundle running Chalmers
and Galani, Ehsan, Padigar, Phillips — and conclude the author found the seamful line late and
appended it.

The increment is real and the paper nearly states it. Seamful design justifies the seam by what the
user can do with it: appropriate the system, foresee its harms, work with it rather than through
it. Section 7 justifies the seam by what someone else owes at it. The pause at the desk matters
even when the guest gains nothing instrumental from it, because that is where a person can
recognize her and make an exception, and the paper's own citation of Lind et al. and Folger
establishes that voice has value independent of outcome. Say that in two sentences and the
transposition becomes a translation with a reason. Leave it implicit and I score this section as
borrowed.

### [FIX] Padigar et al. carries the wrong year against its own volume. Cost: one character.

The reference gives "Padigar, Manjunath, Li, Yi and Manjunath, Chandana N. (2024) ... *Psychology &
Marketing*, 42:1, pp. 21–43". Crossref records the version of record as January 2025; August 2024
is the online-first date. Volume 42, issue 1, pages 21–43 is the 2025 issue. The reference as
printed points a reader at a volume that did not exist in 2024. Change both the in-text citation
and the list to 2025.

### [CONSIDER] Section 3 attributes a data-flow claim to a framework about customer movement. Cost: ~10 words or nothing.

"Physical interactions are continuously turned into digital records, and digital determinations are
continuously turned back into physical arrangements ... (Batat 2024)." PH-CX describes customers
shifting between physical and digital settings and the design problem of fluidifying that
movement. It does not describe a conversion of interaction into record and back. The formulation is
yours, and it is a good one; the citation makes it sound like Batat's. Either move the citation to
the following sentence, where the paper correctly attributes the fluidity language, or mark the
first sentence as the paper's own reading.

### [CONSIDER] Batat (2026) is the newest statement of the value and Section 7 does not use it. Cost: ~20 words.

The paper cites Batat's phygital service research paradigm in Section 1 and then leaves it. Its
abstract lists "contextual fluidity" among the paradigm's five emphases and states that "PSR
reframes service experiences and value creation as fluid, dynamically poly-constructed, ethically
and technologically mediated, and contextually adaptive". Published in 2026, by the framework's
architect, in a service journal. If the argument is that the value has escaped criticism while the
field criticized everything else, that is the sentence that dates the claim to now. Only spend the
words if they are still available after the Shabnam addition, which matters more.

## 3. Where it is strong

Section 4's inversion is the best paragraph in the paper and I would defend it at a panel. "An
opaque algorithm can still augment hospitality if its rules are reasonably stable and an empowered
employee can question or override its decisions. By contrast, a perfectly transparent algorithm can
still substitute for hospitality if nobody is required to answer for its decisions." My field has
spent a decade treating transparency as the master variable. That sentence pair explains why the
transparency literature keeps producing null results, and it does so in fifty words. Do not touch
it.

The Garcia et al. example is the single most useful thing in the paper for a service researcher. A
pricing system that biases its own recommendations because it anticipates the manager's deviation
is a case where nobody at the desk can honestly explain the decision, however willing they are, and
no amount of frontline training fixes it. Keep the whole passage.

The remedy paragraph in Section 7 — reputational records reducing discrimination while relocating
the disadvantage onto the guest with no record, plus Filippas et al. on inflation — is the most
sophisticated argument in the manuscript and the one my field is least likely to have made. It
earns its space three times over.

Section 5's competence/standing split is clean and correct, and the treatment of Zhou et al. is
fair to that scale: the paper says what it measures, says it is real, and locates the limit outside
the instrument rather than inside it. That is how to differentiate from a published construct.

Finally, the hedge at the end of Section 7 paragraph two — "the field is far from uncritical, and
the claim is only that one particular design value has so far escaped the criticism applied to
everything around it" — is why I am not writing an angry review. Keep that sentence exactly as it
stands. It is what makes the section survivable in front of this readership.

## 4. Best spend

I would put roughly 160 words in three places, weighted to the first.

**Section 3, after the Sharma and Mattila sentence (~72 words).**

> The phygital service programme has itself named the difficulty. Shabnam et al. (2026) report
> that ambiguities persist around agency, governance and interpretive labour in algorithmically
> mediated interactions, and that existing frameworks explain value co-creation without explaining
> how digitally enforced rules redistribute the labour of interpretation. Their agenda stops at
> naming the ambiguity. It does not ask which party, once the rules have done the redistributing,
> still owes the guest a welcome.

**Section 7, replacing the Mameli clause (~30 net words).**

> and the most recent integrative framework for phygital tourism, built from 57 studies and 84
> industry cases, records that phygital events employ technology "to create seamless interactions
> between people and spaces", treating smoothness as an outcome to be engineered rather than a
> value to be argued for (Mameli et al. 2026).

**Section 7, before the closing sentence (~57 words).**

> Seamful design already argues that revealing a system's joins restores a user's capacity to act
> (Chalmers and Galani 2004; Ehsan et al. 2024). Hospitality changes the reason for the seam. It
> matters here not because the guest can exploit it but because someone can meet her there, and
> that reason holds even when the seam wins her nothing.

## 5. Verdict

**Minor revision.** The paper is sound, the Section 7 claim is true, and everything I have listed is
a matter of sentences rather than structure.

The single most important fix is the Shabnam addition. Not because the paper is wrong about them,
but because a phygital service referee will find TPSR in ten seconds, will see agency, governance
and interpretive labour named there, and will ask what this paper adds. The manuscript has a good
answer and currently does not give it.

## 6. VERIFY

Everything below I checked myself against publisher records or full text on 17 August 2026.

- **Batat and Hammedi (2023), ERT.** Quotation verified verbatim from the published abstract: the
  framework's value is that these technologies "create a continuum in terms of customer value from
  physical to digital settings and vice versa". No occurrence of seamless, friction, smooth or
  fluid in the abstract. The manuscript's use is accurate. Minor: the manuscript writes "Extended
  reality frameworks describe their purpose" (plural) for a single source.
- **Batat (2024), PH-CX.** "Fluidifying" verified in the published abstract — the framework "helps
  managers design compelling customer experiences by fluidifying the journeys of customers from
  online to offline and inversely". Accurate. Note the manuscript renders this as the guest's
  journey; Batat writes customers, which is fine in paraphrase but the quoted word should stay
  isolated as it is now.
- **Mameli et al. (2026).** Verified against the open-access full text. "Friction" and
  "frictionless" do not occur. "Seamless" occurs, quoted above. Corpus figures of 57 articles and
  84 industry cases are correct. VERIFY once at proof by opening the article and running a find, so
  that the sentence you print is one you have personally searched; my check was a converted-text
  read and I would not stake a rebuttal on it alone.
- **Shabnam et al. (2026), TPSR.** The "opacity and rigidity" characterization is confirmed, and I
  was ready to object to it before I checked. The published version states that "[t]ransparent
  algorithms enhance autonomy and trust, while opacity reduces control" and that "[a]daptive
  governance fosters inclusion; rigid rules intensify exclusion". Nothing on seamlessness or
  friction that I could find. The Section 7 absence claim survives against this source. Full text
  is open at the O.P. Jindal repository if the author wants a page number.
- **Padigar et al.** Crossref gives volume 42, issue 1, pp. 21–43, published print January 2025,
  online August 2024. The reference list needs 2025.
- **Phillips et al. (2024).** Confirmed as an empirical study of frictionless retail service and
  memorability, thirty customer interviews, *The Service Industries Journal* 44:13–14. It examines
  smoothness as a value. This is the citation that forces the scoping fix above.
- **Batat (2026), PSR.** Abstract confirmed: "contextual fluidity" among five emphases, and "PSR
  reframes service experiences and value creation as fluid". No friction or seamlessness language
  in the abstract. I did not read the full text.
- **Not checked.** I did not verify the Weaver (2025) characterization of technology-driven
  acceleration, nor anything outside Sections 3 and 7 and the phygital cluster.
