# Reviewer 05 — Ana Beatriz Furtado

*Ethics of responsibility and machine agency*

## 1. Who I am

I work on responsibility gaps and machine agency, mostly on the question of what survives when a
duty's bearer is a socio-technical arrangement rather than a person. Bovens, Hart's role
responsibility, van de Poel on many hands, Santoni de Sio and Mecacci, and the care-robot literature
are my daily reading. I came to this paper expecting the usual borrowing: a hospitality argument
that reaches for "responsibility" as an honorific and then does nothing with it. That is not what
this is. The paper has a genuine normative thesis, it knows the difference between a capacity and an
obligation, and its central sentence is a real philosophical claim rather than a flourish. My
findings are almost entirely about that one sentence, which the paper asserts in a subordinate
clause and then asks to carry §8 and §9. It cannot carry them in its present form, and one
counterexample from my own field knocks it over. The good news is that the true version is stronger
than the stated version and costs under a hundred words.

## 2. The read

### F1. The load-bearing sentence is false as written, and the counterexample is the one my field reaches for first [FIX]

§3: "A welcome is owed simply because someone has arrived, and it is the only one of these duties
that defines the role of the party who owes it."

The first clause is right and it is the best sentence in the paper. The second clause is wrong.
Fiduciary duty is the standard case in law and in ethics of an obligation that constitutes the role
of its bearer. A trustee, an agent, a guardian: none of these is a person who happens to have taken
on a duty of loyalty; each is a role that exists only in virtue of that duty, and the word
"fiduciary" names the duty and the party interchangeably. The paper has just listed fiduciary duty
one clause earlier. Any referee with a philosophy or law background will stop at this sentence, and
will be right to.

Care and governance survive the test, for what it is worth. Duties of care attach to drivers and
manufacturers who are not carers, and governance duties attach to operators whose role is the
operating. So the paper has one clean counterexample and it is sitting inside its own list.

What the paper means is true and is a different claim. Welcome is the only one of the four whose
trigger is bare presence. Need is a condition in the other party, entrustment is an act by the other
party, risk-creation is a prior act by the duty-bearer. Arrival requires nothing of anyone. And the
second true half, which the paper needs and never states: the other three duties can be delegated to
a substitute without changing who the parties are. A hotel that appoints a processor to discharge
its data-protection duty is still the hotel. A hotel that hands the welcome to another party has
handed over the position of host. Performance is delegable; ownership is what being the host
consists in. That distinction is exactly what §1 needs when it insists that an employee is not a
host, and it is what makes the paper's question non-trivial.

Cost: about 80 net words, replacing roughly 28. Drafted in part 4. This is the single most important
fix in my report.

### F2. Kropf et al. is the paper's sharpest adversary and it is cited as a friend [FIX]

§3 attaches "(Kropf et al. 2026; Santoni de Sio and Mecacci 2021)" to the sentence grounding care in
need, fiduciary duty in trust, and governance in risk.

I have read the Kropf abstract in full. Its thesis is that AI-based care robots "cannot bear moral
responsibility due to their lack of consciousness, intention, and reason-responsiveness" but "can
still hold role responsibility based on their social function in caregiving contexts," and that
"they are appropriate targets for (thin) praise and blame, not because they are moral agents, but
because they operate within roles tied to morally relevant duties." Role responsibility there
descends from Hart, via Vincent.

Read that against the manuscript's thesis. The paper argues that a system taking the host's
functions does not inherit the host's obligations. Kropf argues that a machine occupying a social
function does acquire the duties tied to that role, without needing consciousness. If a care robot
can hold role responsibility for care, a check-in system can hold role responsibility for welcome,
and the paper's central negative claim fails. Citing Kropf in passing as support for a background
observation about the sources of duty leaves the paper's strongest objection unanswered on the same
line of the same page.

The objection is also easy to answer, because Kropf answers it. His conclusion is that recognizing
role responsibility "helps narrow existing responsibility gaps in care environments without
displacing human accountability." That is precisely the paper's position: the machine can occupy
the function, the answering still lands on a human institution. Fifty-eight words converts the
adversary into a supporter and pre-empts the referee who knows this literature. Drafted in part 4.

There is a second reason this matters. Without an answer to Kropf, the paper's thesis reads as true
by definition. If the welcome-obligation constitutes the host role, and systems cannot bear
obligations, then "the system does not inherit the host's obligations" follows analytically and the
paper has proved nothing. Kropf is what makes the claim contestable and therefore worth arguing. The
paper should want him in the room.

### F3. Santoni de Sio and Mecacci does not say what the citation implies [FIX, cheap]

I have read the paper. Its taxonomy is of gaps in kinds of responsibility, not of grounds of duty:
"the culpability gap, the moral accountability gap, the public accountability gap, and the active
responsibility gap." It says nothing about need as the ground of care and nothing about trust or
fiduciary duty at all. The one clause it does support is the governance clause, because the active
responsibility gap concerns "the obligation to ensure that these systems do not impact negatively on
the rights and interests of other persons."

So the citation half-lands, in a place where a referee who works on responsibility gaps will check
it. The project's own CLAIMS.md already recorded the honest position — "welcome is owed to the
stranger in virtue of arrival, not need (care) or trust (fiduciary); that transposition is ours" —
and the manuscript does not say so. Recast the citation as supporting the governance clause only, or
mark the trichotomy as the paper's own. Cost: zero to twenty words.

A related note the paper could use rather than fear. Santoni de Sio and Mecacci separate
responsibility along a temporal axis (backward-looking culpability, answerability, forward-looking
active responsibility) while §3 separates duties along an axis of triggering condition. Those are
different cuts through the same material, and saying so in half a sentence would make §3 look
deliberate rather than improvised.

### F4. §4's accountability condition rests on a Bovens forum the guest does not have [FIX]

§4: "Accountability weakens when there is no identifiable person or forum that is required to
provide an answer when a decision is challenged (Bovens 2007)."

Bovens's actor-forum relation carries three elements: an obligation on the actor to explain and
justify, a forum that can pose questions and pass judgement, and consequences the actor may face.
§4 keeps only the first. That is the smaller problem, and I return to it below.

The larger one is that Bovens's forums are institutions — political, legal, administrative,
professional — and the guest is none of them. His fifth type, social accountability, does reach
clients and civil society, but he characterizes it as the weak case: the hierarchical relationship
and the formal obligation to render account are generally absent, and account-giving happens on a
voluntary basis. Take that seriously and the condition is failed by every hotel on earth, always,
because no hotel is ever formally required to answer a guest. The augmentative/substitutive
distinction then collapses: everything is substitutive, and the paper's cleanest idea stops
discriminating between cases.

The paper already holds the answer and puts it in the wrong section. Table 2, stage 4, tells
organizations to "create a forum required to answer challenges, with genuine authority to act,"
which concedes that the forum has to be built rather than found. §4 should say so where the
condition is stated, not two sections later where it reads as a practical afterthought. And the
paper's own thesis licenses the transplant: if a welcome is owed in virtue of arrival, then arrival
is also what gives the guest standing to demand an answer. The guest does not need an antecedent
institutional relationship for the same reason she does not need one to be owed a welcome. That
reply is available, it costs about seventy-five words, and without it the condition looks borrowed
rather than argued. Drafted in part 4.

On the dropped elements: §6's fourth principle re-derives Bovens's third element from scratch, in
the observation that an employee who can listen but not act "becomes a place where frustration is
deposited rather than a meaningful source of accountability." That is exactly Bovens on
consequences. Naming all three elements in §4 costs about eighteen words and turns §6's fourth
principle from a fresh assertion into a corollary. Crisper, in the sense the brief means.

### F5. An absence claim a single well-known book refutes [FIX]

§3: "Philosophy of technology has considered the machine as a guest to whom hospitality might be
extended (Introna 2010); it has not yet considered the machine as a keeper of the threshold."

Introna is described accurately. I checked: he uses Derrida and Levinas, reads the Star Trek episode
on Commander Data, and argues for an ethos of hospitality extended toward technological artefacts.

The second half is refuted by James J. Brown's *Ethical Programs: Hospitality and the Rhetorics of
Software* (University of Michigan Press, 2015), which is a book-length treatment of networked
software as host — TCP as a protocol of admission, the machine that "never really gets to decide who
or what enters your space." That is the machine as keeper of the threshold, in those words, in a
title that pairs hospitality with software. The project's own audit flagged this as its
highest-variance unresolved item and I see no sign it was closed.

The claim is narrowable rather than lost, and the narrowed version is still novel. Brown's hosts
admit packets; nobody has asked what a commercial hospitality system owes a person standing at a
door. Roughly thirty words plus a reference entry, and the paper gains a citation that makes it look
better read rather than worse.

### F6. Ambiguous antecedent at the hinge of §3 [FIX, trivial]

"A related line of work catalogues the responsibilities of hospitality service robots, but frames
them as matters of governance: liability, safety, data protection, and consent (Sharma and Mattila
2025). Each of these responsibilities has a different source."

"These" reads as the four just listed, but the next sentence supplies a different three. The paper's
most important paragraph turns on a broken pronoun. "Duties of this kind differ in their source" or
similar. Three words.

### F7. The four-duty pattern is a real figure, and I would not touch it otherwise [CONSIDER]

Asked whether §3's pattern is a taxonomy or a rhetorical figure, my answer is: neither, and that is
fine. It is a contrast set built on one consistent axis — the condition that triggers the duty — and
consistency of axis is more than most such lists manage. It is not a partition, since a hotel owes
all four to the same guest at once, and it is not exhaustive. The paper never claims it is either.
Care, fiduciary duty and governance appear once and exist to set off welcome, which is legitimate
construction rather than padding.

So my verdict on the pattern is that it holds. My verdict on the uniqueness claim attached to it is
F1. Do not let a fix to the second dissolve the first: the four-part shape should survive, with the
distinguishing axis changed.

## 3. Where it is strong

Three things must survive any trim.

The first is the arrival trigger itself. "A welcome is owed simply because someone has arrived" is
the paper's thesis in nine words and I have not read it elsewhere in this form. It is also the
sentence that makes the transplant of Bovens defensible, once F4 is written.

The second is the competence/standing distinction in §5, and specifically "Lodging an appeal is an
action a person can learn to perform; being heard and answered depends on an institution agreeing to
recognize that appeal." That is a clean statement of something my field usually takes four pages to
say, and the Zhou et al. treatment around it is fair to the scale — it credits what the instrument
measures before naming what it structurally cannot.

The third is §6's division between principles that interface design can supply and principles that
only institutions can, closing on "Some problems require people to become more capable; others
require institutions to become more answerable." A responsibility-ethics reader will regard that
sentence as the paper's practical payoff. Do not cut it to make room for anything I have proposed.

I will also record that §4's counterintuitive result — an opaque algorithm can augment while a
transparent one substitutes — is correct and is the reason the framework is worth having. It
separates epistemic access from normative standing, which most of the transparency literature does
not.

## 4. Best spend

I would put my 150 on F1 and F2, in that order, both inside the §3 machine-ethics paragraph. If a
further 75 are available, F4 next.

**F1 — replacing "and it is the only one of these duties that defines the role of the party who owes
it" (about 110 words in, 28 out, net +82):**

> A welcome is owed simply because someone has arrived. The trigger is bare presence: the guest need
> not be in need, need not have entrusted anything, and need not have agreed to anything. The
> nearest rival is fiduciary duty, which likewise constitutes the role of the party who bears it,
> but a fiduciary is made by an entrustment the other party must first perform, and a hotel that
> appoints a processor to discharge its data-protection duties remains a hotel. A hotel that hands
> the welcome to another party has handed over the position of host along with it. The performance
> can be delegated. The ownership is what being the host consists in.

**F2 — immediately after, answering Kropf (+58):**

> Kropf et al. (2026) argue that a care robot can hold role responsibility grounded in its social
> function even where it cannot hold moral responsibility, and that recognizing this narrows
> responsibility gaps without displacing human accountability. The same holds at the threshold. A
> system can occupy the function of receiving without becoming the party who answers for the
> reception.

**F4 — in §4, after the accountability condition (+75):**

> Bovens's forum is ordinarily an institution: a court, an auditor, a professional body. He names a
> fifth relation in which agencies answer to clients and to civil society, but observes that it
> lacks the hierarchy and the formal obligation that give the others their force. The guest arrives
> with no such standing, which is the point rather than an objection to it. If a welcome is owed in
> virtue of arrival, arrival is also what founds the guest's claim to an answer, and the arrangement
> that makes the claim answerable has to be built rather than found.

Net across all three, +215, inside the headroom and leaving room for the other seven of us.

## 5. Verdict

**Minor revision.** This is a good paper with one broken load-bearing sentence and one uncited
adversary, and both sit in the same paragraph of §3.

The single most important fix is F1: change the axis of the uniqueness claim from "defines the role"
to bare-presence trigger plus non-delegable ownership. As written the sentence is false, the
counterexample is in the paper's own list, and every downstream claim in §8 and §9 leans on it.
