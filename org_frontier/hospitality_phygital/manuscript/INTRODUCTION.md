# Introduction

*Full draft on the 5Cs. Block labels come out at submission.*

Phygital hospitality scholarship frequently presents artificial intelligence, mobile applications,
automated interfaces, and immersive technologies as tools for delivering seamless, personalized, and
guest-centric experiences. Yet hospitality is not simply the efficient provision of services. It is
a relational and culturally situated practice involving welcome, recognition, care, presence, and
negotiated access. When algorithmic systems increasingly recognize guests, personalize environments,
mediate communication, allocate organizational attention, and determine available choices, they do
more than support hospitality: they participate in defining how hospitality is performed and
experienced. This conceptual paper therefore asks: How does algorithmic mediation reconfigure
relationships among guests, hospitality employees, and organizations, and under what conditions does
it enhance rather than diminish the human and relational qualities of hospitality?

### Common ground

The scholarship on hospitality's hybrid physical-digital encounters has expanded rapidly over the
past decade, organizing around a small number of frameworks that treat the blending of physical and
digital elements as a matter of experience design. Batat (2024, 2026) sets out the phygital customer
experience framework and the subsequent programme of phygital service research, placing guests,
hosts, employees and communities at the centre of experience design and directing the field towards
positive impact at individual, community and environmental levels; a systematic review of 105
studies published between 2017 and 2025 finds phygital experience at the core of the corpus and
characterizes the field as consumer-centred and technology-enabled (Brochado et al. 2026). Alongside
these frameworks sits a substantial body of research on how well the hybrid encounter can be built
and how readily guests will adopt it, addressing automation across tourism (Tussyadiah 2020), the
strategic potential of service robots and other digital service technologies (Wirtz et al. 2023),
and the willingness of guests to accept identification technologies such as biometric check-in (Xu
et al. 2020; Boo and Chua 2022). Service research has meanwhile ceased to model the encounter as a
dyad, developing three-party formulations in Service Encounter 2.0 (Larivière et al. 2017), the
service triad of robot, customer and frontline employee (Odekerken-Schröder et al. 2021), the
encounter triad in hospitality's own review of AI-based service (Li et al. 2021), and, most
recently, a hybrid triadic configuration of customer, employee and AI system through which
artificial intelligence is understood to redistribute agency and transform interpretive labour
(Gursoy 2026). Hospitality theory has held its own ground alongside these developments, insisting
that hospitality businesses are persistently misdescribed as service operations and better
understood through the host-guest relationship, generosity and the theatricality of the encounter
(Hemmington 2007), and locating hospitality itself within social control, within social and economic
exchange, and within metaphor (Lynch et al. 2011, 2021).

### Complication

The vocabulary in which the phygital and service-research literatures describe the third party has,
however, remained remarkably consistent across two decades of theorizing. Where the technology is
named, it is named as an infused tool (Bitner et al. 2000), an automated social presence (van Doorn
et al. 2017), a mediator or facilitator (Li et al. 2021), a co-creator (Xing and Zhang 2026), or,
more recently, an algorithmic actor bearing distributed agency (Gursoy 2026); the systematic review
of the corpus resolves the same tendency into the phrase technology-enabled (Brochado et al. 2026).
Each of these formulations describes what the technology is able to do in the encounter, but none of
them describes what the technology owes to the parties it now recognizes, routes, prices and admits.
Within that literature, Belanche et al. (2020) come closest to raising the question, examining
whether customers attribute
responsibility to a robot after a service failure and finding that they attribute it to the firm,
though attribution of responsibility by a customer is a rather different matter from an obligation
held by a host; machine ethics has come nearer still, assigning care robots a role responsibility
grounded in the social function they occupy (Kropf et al. 2026), and care is owed in virtue of need
where welcome is owed in virtue of arrival. This asymmetry between what a party does and what a
party owes is not incidental to
hospitality: the tradition has always distributed its duties unevenly among the parties present,
reserving a duty of welcome for the host that no employment relation can either generate or
discharge (Lashley 2000; Bulley 2015). When service research placed the technology in the third
position, it compared machine with employee constantly and filled the role accordingly; whether the
employee was ever the right template has gone unexamined, because the host does not appear in that
literature's repertoire.

### Concern

The consequence of the vacancy is that a good deal of what hospitality has always conferred on the
guest now has no counterparty. Hospitality confers standing, understood here as a position from
which the guest may ask, decline, explain herself, and be received as an exception; the field's own
instrumentation resolves the experience of hospitality into invitation, care and comfort, two of
which are constituted by the guest's sense of having been acknowledged rather than by anything an
operator delivers (Pijls et al. 2017), and studies of ordinary welcome caution that the standing so
conferred is a tactical achievement as much as an ethical one, closely bound up with the
self-interest
of those extending it (Lynch 2017). Where an algorithmic intermediary performs the recognizing, the
routing, the pricing and the settling of what may be asked, the guest retains the position but loses
the party who was formerly obliged to answer for it: she keeps an addressee for complaint and
compensation, and loses the counterparty with authority over the rules that govern her case. The
employee is exposed on the same axis, since a hotel room-assignment system
has been observed to fix the sequence of work, to withhold the day's full task list, and to offer a
rejection affordance that workers reported they could not use without fearing dismissal (Spektor et
al. 2023), while a study of employees in high-involvement phygital services finds resistance
concentrated where the connection between the technology and a higher organizational purpose is
obscured (De Vos et al. 2026). The prevailing design value in the field compounds the exposure,
since a journey made perfectly fluid across the physical-digital seam (Batat 2024) removes precisely
the occasions on which either party might have spoken, and the most recent programmatic statement of
transformative phygital service research directs its critical attention to opacity and to rigidity
while leaving smoothness itself unexamined (Shabnam et al. 2026).

### Course of action

This paper accordingly asks how algorithmic mediation reconfigures the relationships among guests,
hospitality employees and organizations, and under what conditions such mediation enhances rather
than diminishes the human and relational qualities of hospitality. Taking the triadic structure as
established rather than proposing it, we examine what is at stake in whether the third party
occupies, or fails to occupy, the position of host in hospitality's own sense of that term. The
paper first distinguishes augmentative hospitality, in which technology supports human recognition,
discretion, responsiveness and care, from substitutive hospitality, in which algorithmic direction
displaces relational judgment and restricts the capacity of guests and employees to shape the
encounter, and specifies the distinction as a condition on an arrangement that can be checked
against any particular touchpoint. It then introduces hospitality algorithmacy as the situated
capacity of guests and employees to recognize, interpret, engage with and, where necessary,
circumvent algorithmic mediation, and coordinative sovereignty as the practical ability to retain
meaningful influence over how hospitality is organized and enacted; the two are held apart because a
competence that a person can develop is not the same thing as a standing that only an institution
can confer. Five affordances of guest-centric phygital design follow from what mediation withholds,
transparency, adjustability, contestability, human accessibility and bypass-ability, each specified
in the form that survives its own published null.

### Contribution

The paper's contribution to hospitality theory is to reposition artificial intelligence from a
neutral service technology to a constitutive mediator of hospitality relationships, meanings and
power. Phygital scholarship has already registered that such transformations redistribute agency
across institutional and non-institutional actors, as the museum case shows most clearly, where
curatorial authority disperses across visitors, communities and the technology that mediates their
encounter (Mosca et al. 2025); what redistribution leaves unresolved is the obligation that travels
with the reallocated agency, and hospitality, unlike museum visitorship, carries a duty of welcome
that must land on some party if it is to be discharged at all. For hospitality organizations, the
framework yields principles that preserve human contact, explain consequential decisions, enable
employee intervention and provide meaningful alternatives to digitally prescribed encounters,
together with a distinction the practitioner literature has not made, between the affordances a
property can supply through design and training and the one it cannot, since contestability requires
that some party constitute a forum. The argument is conceptual, and every affordance-to-outcome
relationship proposed here awaits empirical test.
