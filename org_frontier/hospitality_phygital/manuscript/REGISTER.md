# Register ruling — measured, not inherited

The repo's `CLAUDE.md` mandates a Nagel-plain register with **no first person**, and this arm's
`AGENTS.md` re-bound that rule on 2026-08-07. The first full draft of the manuscript followed it, and
the result was wrong for the venue. This file records what the exemplars actually do, the ruling that
follows, and the scope of the suspension.

**Scope.** This ruling governs `manuscript/manuscript.md` and the submission front matter only. Every
other file in the arm — plans, agendas, bridges, this file — keeps repo house style. The suspension
expires with this submission.

## Exemplars read

Full texts, both from the target journal's own author community:

| source | why it counts |
|---|---|
| Lynch, McIntosh, Lugosi, Germann Molz and Ong (2021), 'Hospitality & Society: Critical reflections on the theorizing of hospitality', *Hospitality & Society* 11:3 | The target journal, by its editors, reviewing its own decade. Accepted manuscript via Oxford Brookes RADAR |
| Lugosi (2021), 'Exploring the hospitality-tourism nexus', *Tourist Studies* 21:1 | Single-author conceptual commentary by an *H&S* editor. Published version, open access |

## What they do

**First person is standard, and it is not hedged.** Lynch et al. write 'we first attempt a grounded
reading', 'the "creative, critical and interdisciplinary approaches to hospitality" we sought to
foster', 'Our analysis reveals thirteen themes', and 'we propose future directions'. Lugosi, writing
alone, states 'I argue that examining hospitality's abstract and concrete dimensions … helps to
better understand tourism's practices', and uses 'our understanding' and 'our conceptions' throughout.
A conceptual paper in this literature that refuses the first person is not being rigorous. It is
being conspicuous.

**Paragraphs are long and continuous.** Both run 150–250 words, eight to fifteen lines, developing a
single line of thought without visual breaks. Short paragraphs are used for transitions and almost
nothing else.

**Sentences subordinate heavily.** Lynch et al.: 'In what is in many ways a foundational paper, Bell
(2011) focuses upon theoretical issues and development contributing to broader critical and
theoretical agendas with a concern for hospitality work and hospitable spaces.' Thirty-five words,
three subordinate structures, no full stop where a lesser writer would put three.

**The cited author is the grammatical subject.** 'Bell examines the two spaces conceptually', 'Boudou
(2015) presents a political analysis', 'Kearney (2015) extends these debates', 'Shaul (2017) provides
a thought-provoking contribution'. Narrative citation is the default; trailing parentheses are for
background clusters, not for the paper being discussed.

**Evaluative language is normal.** 'a wonderfully rich and thought-provoking paper', 'an engaging
theoretical debate', 'usefully contextualises', 'creatively draw upon', 'a powerful contemporary
reflection'. The repo style would strike these as editorializing. This literature reads them as
scholarly engagement, and their absence reads as coldness.

**'Upon' rather than 'on'** in most prepositional uses: 'focuses upon', 'draws upon', 'reflection
upon', 'conditions placed upon'.

**Connectives the repo style suppresses are in ordinary use**: 'However,' 'Moreover,' 'Importantly,'
'Arguably,' 'In sum,' 'Consequently,'.

**Emphasis is italic and sparing**, applied to single words carrying an argumentative turn —
hospitality *is* society, the *only* ethical power, how to *inject* it into the law. Neither exemplar
uses bold in running text.

## Ruling

For the manuscript only:

1. **First person plural is permitted and expected.** Two authors, so 'we'. Each use should mark a
   decision the authors are making — a claim, a departure, a limit — and not decorate a sentence.
2. **Paragraphs target 150–250 words, and the spread matters as much as the mean.** That is the range
   both exemplars occupy, and the draft now sits at a mean of 173 and a median of 167 within it. Do
   not enforce the band mechanically. A paragraph should run long where it carries several sources
   through a single line of thought, and short where a section needs a closing beat or a conclusion
   needs to be tight. Prose in which every paragraph lands near the mean reads as machine-made, and
   neither exemplar reads that way. Three paragraphs in the current draft sit outside a 120–260 band
   by design; the reasons are recorded below and a later pass should leave them alone.
3. **Narrative citation is the default.** The cited author is the subject of the sentence wherever
   that author's argument is being engaged.
4. **Measured evaluative language is permitted** where it states a judgement the authors will defend:
   'usefully qualify', 'rich reading', 'arresting', 'rather starkly'.
5. **No bold in running text.** Constructs are named in plain prose on first use.
6. **British English with 'ize' endings**, per the Notes for Contributors, which overrides the -ise
   forms visible in the exemplars' accepted manuscripts.
7. **Retained from repo house style**, because the exemplars do not violate them and they improve any
   prose: the antithesis machine stays cut, self-narrating rigor stays at zero, and paragraph
   openings stay varied.

**On the spread, which is deliberate.** Twenty-two paragraph pairs were merged in a second pass, each
with a written seam rather than a splice, taking the mean from 102 to 173. Three paragraphs sit
outside a 120–260 band and should stay there. The discretion paragraph in section 3 runs to 264
because it carries five sources and a qualification that would lose its force if separated from the
claim it qualifies. The last of the three vignettes in section 4 runs to 95 as a short closing beat
after two longer ones. The conclusion's first paragraph runs to 102 because a conclusion should be
tight. Driving every paragraph to the mean would produce exactly the uniformity of shape that marks
prose as machine-made, and the exemplars do not have it either.
