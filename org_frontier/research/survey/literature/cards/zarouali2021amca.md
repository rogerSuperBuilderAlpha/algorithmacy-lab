## Summary
This paper develops and validates the Algorithmic Media Content Awareness Scale (AMCA-scale), a standardized self-report instrument for measuring whether online media users are aware of algorithmic content recommendation, a construct the authors note had no validated measure prior to this work. AMCA is defined as "the extent to which people hold accurate perceptions of what algorithms do in a particular media environment, as well as their impact on how users consume and experience media content." The scale was built in three phases (item generation, content/face validity with expert judges and a pre-test, and quantitative validation on a nationally representative Dutch sample of 2106 respondents). Although five dimensions were theorized a priori (content filtering, automated decision-making, human-algorithm interplay, algorithmic persuasion, ethical considerations), exploratory factor analysis recovered only four (dropping algorithmic persuasion), yielding a final 13-item scale explaining 74% of variance. Confirmatory factor analysis supported a hierarchical second-order model with good fit, and the scale showed strong reliability, convergent and discriminant validity, predictive validity (predicting algorithmic knowledge), and moderate-to-good test-retest reliability. The instrument was validated across three platforms (Facebook, YouTube, Netflix), demonstrating robustness across environments. Across the total sample, mean awareness scores were moderate-to-low (around 3 on a 5-point scale), consistent with prior findings of generally low algorithmic awareness.

## Key facts it relies on
- AMCA is defined as "the extent to which people hold accurate perceptions of what algorithms do in a particular media environment, as well as their impact on how users consume and experience media content."
- Five dimensions were theorized a priori (content filtering, automated decision-making, human-algorithm interplay, algorithmic persuasion, ethical considerations); the final validated scale has four dimensions and 13 items, after algorithmic persuasion failed to emerge as a distinct factor.
- Item pool funneled from 28 initial items (5-point scale, 1 = "not at all aware" to 5 = "completely aware") to 17 after expert content validity review (5 expert judges; I-CVI used, 11 items eliminated for I-CVI < 1), to the final 13 items.
- Validation sample: 2106 Dutch respondents (35% response rate from 6000 invited), representative of the Dutch population on age, gender, education, region; mean age 54 (SD = 15.59), 52% male; data collected July-August 2019.
- Three subsamples by platform served distinct roles: Netflix (n = 741) exploratory, Facebook (n = 688) confirmatory, YouTube (n = 677) replication; assignment random.
- EFA (Netflix, principal axis factoring, Promax rotation, parallel analysis) gave a four-factor solution explaining 74% of variance; KMO = 0.99, Bartlett's test χ² = 16461.24, df = 120, p < .001; item HAI3 dropped for low communality (0.49).
- CFA (Facebook) on a hierarchical second-order model showed good fit: χ²/df = 3.46, RMSEA = 0.060, SRMR = 0.02, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98; standardized loadings 0.69-0.92.
- Cronbach's alpha (Facebook): filtering 0.92, automated decision-making 0.92, human-algorithm interplay 0.86, ethical considerations 0.89; AVE for all factors between 0.70 and 0.79 (above 0.50 threshold); discriminant validity established via Fornell-Larcker.
- Predictive validity: AMCA significantly predicted algorithmic knowledge (7 true/false items, score 0-7); OLS b = 0.84, p < .001, F(1,685) = 190.17, R² = 0.22 (Facebook); replicated in YouTube (b = 0.83, R² = 0.21).
- Test-retest reliability (ICC, two-way random, re-administered four months later, n = 1288): content filtering 0.76, automated decision-making 0.75, human-algorithm interplay 0.72, ethical considerations 0.70 (moderate-to-good).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly note they cannot guarantee the four dimensions are an exhaustive list of the awareness construct; other relevant dimensions may exist and could be added in future work.
- The a priori "algorithmic persuasion" dimension lacked empirical support (items did not load cleanly on one factor and were omitted); the authors say this may mean the subscale is not distinctive enough or that they lacked appropriate items, and encourage future operationalization.
- The scale development was largely driven by a literature-review-based five-dimensional assumption; the authors recommend qualitative methods (in-depth interviews, focus groups, think-aloud) in future work to explore possibly missing dimensions.
- Validation was conducted only on a Dutch sample (items translated to Dutch) and three entertainment/social platforms; the authors flag that cross-cultural/cross-country applicability and other contexts (news, e-commerce, music streaming) remain to be tested, requiring adaptation of the "[platform name]" and "[media content]" placeholders.
- Some cross-loadings (FIL4, ADM3, ETC3) were retained rather than deleted to avoid leaving factors with fewer than 3 items, a deliberate tradeoff the authors acknowledge.
