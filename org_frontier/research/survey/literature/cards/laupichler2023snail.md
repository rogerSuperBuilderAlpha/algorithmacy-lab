## Summary
This paper develops and psychometrically explores the "Scale for the assessment of non-experts' AI literacy" (SNAIL), a self-report instrument for measuring AI literacy in laypeople without formal AI or computer-science training. The authors took a preliminary 39-item set (generated in an earlier Delphi study) formulated as "I can..." statements, administered it via an online questionnaire to a heterogeneous Prolific sample, and ran an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using an inductive, bottom-up approach with no a priori hypotheses. Three research questions structured the work: how many factors to extract and which items load on them (RQ1), whether the factors map onto interpretable content themes (RQ2), and which items could be dropped for efficiency (RQ3). After comparing one-, two-, three-, and four-factor models, a three-factor solution gave the best compromise between parsimony and avoiding underextraction, yielding factors named "Technical Understanding", "Critical Appraisal", and "Practical Application" (the TUCAPA model). Eight items were eliminated for cross-loading and/or low communality, leaving a final 31-item questionnaire with excellent internal consistency. The authors position SNAIL as a tool for assessing individuals or groups and for evaluating the teaching effectiveness of AI-literacy courses, while stressing the need for confirmatory replication.

## Key facts it relies on
- The instrument builds on a preliminary set of 39 content-valid items developed in a prior Delphi expert study (Laupichler et al., 2023) involving 53 AI-education experts across three iterative Delphi rounds; items are "I can..." statements rated on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree).
- 479 participants were recruited via Prolific (paid 1.80 GBP, English primary language, over 18, sampling balanced 50/50 by gender with n = 240 cap each); after three careless-responding exclusion criteria (attention check n = 9, bogus "top 10 AI researchers" item n = 16, completion faster than 1 SD below the mean n = 39), the final analytic sample was n = 415 (participant-to-variable ratio 10.6:1).
- Sample characteristics: average age 39.5 years (SD = 13.6), 208 (50.1%) identified as female, mostly from the United Kingdom (n = 316, 76.1%), South Africa (n = 32, 7.7%), United States (n = 27, 6.5%), Australia and Canada (each n = 9, 2.2%); mean completion time after exclusion was 5:39 min (SD = 2:19).
- EFA used common (maximum likelihood) factor analysis on the polychoric correlation matrix with promax (oblique) rotation; data appropriateness confirmed by Bartlett's test (p < .001) and KMO overall MSA = 0.97 (range 0.94-0.98); salience threshold for pattern coefficients set conservatively at 0.32 (Comrey & Lee, 1992).
- Factor-retention indicators disagreed: Horn's parallel analysis (20,000 iterations) suggested two factors while Velicer's MAP (minimum 0.0086) suggested three; the three-factor model was chosen for interpretability and parsimony.
- Variance explained: one-factor 48%, two-factor 54%, three-factor 57%, four-factor 58% (the four-to-three gain being less than one percent argued against four factors).
- Internal consistency for the three-factor model (full item set) was excellent: Cronbach's alpha = 0.94, 0.93, and 0.89 for the three scales; for the reduced 31-item set: alpha = 0.93 [0.92, 0.94], 0.91 [0.89, 0.93], and 0.85 [0.81, 0.88].
- Eight items were eliminated (five for diffuse/cross-loadings, two for low communalities, one for both), where low-communality cutoff was 2 SD (0.08) below the mean communality (0.57); the final SNAIL has 31 items across the three factors.
- The three TUCAPA factors map approximately onto prior frameworks: Ng et al.'s "know and understand" to Technical Understanding, "use and apply" to Practical Application, and "evaluate and create" plus "ethical issues" combined into Critical Appraisal; the authors explicitly exclude programming skills from their AI-literacy definition.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors flag that self-assessment questionnaires are vulnerable to conscious/unconscious biases and recommend SNAIL not be used where results carry direct consequences (e.g., grades, job applications); they call for objective performance tests as a complement.
- No separate "AI ethics" factor emerged, unlike Wang et al. (2022) and Carolus et al. (2023); the authors attribute this partly to few ethics-focused items being generated in the source Delphi study and call for CFA to test whether ethics is distinct or subsumed under Critical Appraisal.
- The non-probabilistic consecutive sampling (paid, anonymous Prolific participants) may introduce sampling/response bias (e.g., self-selection of AI-interested people who rate themselves higher); the authors recommend replication in a more representative or differing sample to enable confirmatory factor analysis.
- The study is exploratory (EFA only) and provides only "initial evidence"; factor structure has not been confirmed, the questionnaire exists only in English, and applicability across subject domains and cultures remains untested.
- Parallel analysis and MAP disagreed on two vs. three factors, so the three-factor choice rests substantially on interpretability/content judgments rather than a clean statistical consensus.
