## Summary
This single-author PRISMA 2020 systematic review (preregistered at OSF) asks which AI literacy measurement instruments exist and how psychometrically sound they are, to guide researchers and educators in choosing scales. Searching Scopus and arXiv on June 18, 2024 yielded 5574 records (5560 after de-duplication); after screening and reverse searches, 22 studies validating or revalidating 16 scales were included. The author assessed each scale against COSMIN measurement properties (content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, cross-cultural validity, reliability, measurement error, construct validity, responsiveness) plus interpretability and feasibility, then graded scale-level evidence using GRADE. Overall, scales showed good structural validity and internal consistency, but only a few were tested for content validity, reliability, construct validity, and responsiveness; none were tested for cross-cultural validity or measurement error. Most studies reported no interpretability indicators and almost none shared raw data. The review found 3 performance-based scales versus 13 self-report (Likert) scales, and notes that no single scale showed positive evidence across all COSMIN properties. It offers population-specific recommendations (e.g., AILS for general population; AI literacy test and ChatGPT literacy scale for higher education; AI-CI/AILQ/Kim & Lee for secondary; Intelligent TPACK for teachers).

## Key facts it relies on
- Search (Scopus + arXiv, June 18, 2024) returned 5574 records (n_Scopus = 5335, n_arXiv = 239); 5560 remained after duplicate removal; 5501 excluded at title/abstract; 59 full texts assessed; 44 excluded; 1 added from reference lists and 6 from reverse searches, yielding 22 studies validating/revalidating 16 scales.
- Scale composition: 3 performance-based scales (AI literacy test, AI-CI, SAIL4ALL) versus 13 self-report scales; most use self-assessment Likert items; all scales were constructed in the last three years, the oldest being MAIRS-MS from 2021.
- Target populations vary: general population, higher education students, secondary education students (three scales: AI-CI, AILQ, Kim & Lee), and teachers (one scale: Intelligent TPACK).
- COSMIN methodology was used (originally a health/medical tool, also applied in psychological and educational research); criterion validity was not assessed because as of January 2024 there was no gold-standard AI literacy instrument; floor/ceiling effects were flagged when >15% of respondents hit the lowest/highest possible score.
- Across scales: good structural validity and internal consistency overall, but few tested for content validity, reliability, construct validity, responsiveness; none tested for cross-cultural validity or measurement error.
- Virtually all scales recognize three consistently shared core competencies: technical understanding of AI, societal impact of AI, and AI ethics; scales differ on whether higher-order skills (creation and evaluation of AI) are core components.
- Many conceptualizations draw on Long & Magerko (2020) and Ng et al. (2021); Ng et al.'s exploratory review proposed four aspects: know and understand, use, evaluate, and ethics.
- Population recommendations: AILS has the most robust evidence for general population; AI literacy test and ChatGPT literacy scale for higher education (both with content validity evidence plus good structural validity, internal consistency, construct validity); AI literacy test is the only performance-based scale for higher education; SAIL4ALL is the only performance-based scale for general population.
- MAILS is the only scale with evidence of minimal floor and ceiling effects; SNAIL shows an indication of floor effect with almost half its items having >15% lowest-score responses; only Laupichler et al. provided open data.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The author explicitly states no scale showed positive evidence for all COSMIN measurement properties and that most studies suffered from poor methodological rigour.
- Self-acknowledged limitation: the review was conducted by a single author, which may introduce bias in quality assessment (though COSMIN criteria are quantitatively defined).
- Self-acknowledged limitation: the search was limited to Scopus and arXiv, so scales in grey literature may have been missed (mitigated by reverse searches in Scopus and Google Scholar).
- The author warns that lack of open data prevents calculation of some quality indicators and may contribute to the replicability crisis; most studies did not report missing-data percentages or handling strategies.
- The author notes content of some performance-based items (e.g., typical AI use in practice) may become obsolete due to rapid AI developments, and questions whether create/evaluate-AI skills should be considered core AI literacy competencies going forward.
