## Summary
The paper pursues two goals: to collect validity evidence for the Meta AI Literacy Scale (MAILS; Carolus, Koch et al. 2023), a 34-item self-assessment instrument for AI literacy plus supplementary psychological competencies, and to develop a 10-item short version. The authors formulate two intended measurement purposes (explaining individual AI literacy and evaluating interindividual differences), derive eight empirically testable axioms (1a-1e, 2a-2c) about the scale's nomological network, and test them across three studies using previously published and newly collected German- and English-speaking samples analyzed in R. Study I (N=300) confirmed axiom 2a (prior use of AI correlated positively with all MAILS scales/subscales); Study II (N=149) confirmed axiom 1b (positive attitude toward AI) and 1c (correlation with three other AI-literacy self-report scales); Study III (N=120) confirmed axioms 1d (correlation with an objective AI-literacy knowledge test), 2a, and 2b (learning about AI). A fourth study (combined N=653) selected 10 items representing all subscales and obtained good confirmatory-factor-analysis fit. Overall the authors find supportive evidence for the MAILS' validity, with the AI self-competency scale (emotion regulation, persuasion literacy) showing deviating patterns that they flag for further research. Axioms 1e (divergent validity) and 2c (pre-post learning differences) remained untested.

## Key facts it relies on
- The MAILS has 34 items: 18 on AI literacy, 4 on the ability to create AI, and 6 each on AI self-efficacy and AI self-competency; AI literacy subfactors are Use AI (6), Know & understand AI (6), Detect AI (3), and AI Ethics (3); items use an 11-point Likert scale (0-10).
- Validation follows three steps (state interpretive purpose, derive axioms, collect evidence); eight axioms were derived: 1a (factorial structure), 1b (convergent with other AIL scales / attitude), 1c (medium correlations with attitude and openness to technology), 1d (correlation with AI-related knowledge), 1e (divergent validity from IT/data literacy, intelligence), 2a (prior use of AI), 2b (formal/informal learning), 2c (pre-post learning differences).
- Prior factorial-structure evidence: original sample N=300 gave CFI=.93, RMSEA=.06 [.05,.06], SRMR=.08; second sample N=219 gave CFI=.95, RMSEA=.05, SRMR=.06.
- Study I (N=300 German-speaking, mean age 32.13, SD 11.66): prior use of AI at work, at school/university, and in everyday life correlated significantly (small-to-moderate) with all MAILS scales/subscales, with one exception (use at school/university not correlated with emotion regulation, r=.09).
- Study II (N=149 English-speaking, mostly UK): positive attitude toward AI correlated with all MAILS scales except persuasion literacy (e.g., AIL r=.55***); negative attitude correlated negatively only with AI self-efficacy, Use AI, emotion regulation, and problem-solving (small effects). MAILS correlated with the AI Literacy Scale (Wang et al.), MAIRS-MS (Karaca et al.), and AI Literacy Scale (Pinski & Benlian).
- Study III (N=120 German-speaking): an objective AI literacy test (Hornberger et al., 29 single-choice items + 1 sorting item, 3-PL IRT, guessing rate .25) correlated with total AIL (r=.21*), AI self-competency (.24**), Know & understand AI (.25**), and emotion regulation (.41***); prior use and learning about AI correlated with all scales except AI self-competency and its subscales.
- Short version (Study IV, combined N=653): 10 items were chosen so each lower-level scale is represented by one item and Create AI (a level-two scale) by two; items 10, 13, 20, 28, 30, and 33 were selected across all three selection methods. The chosen model (item 3, CFA loading directly on superordinate scales) gave chi2(29)=154.750, p<.001, RMSEA=.090, SRMR=.038, TLI=.936, CFI=.958.
- Funding: German Federal Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs [DKI.00.00030.21]; authors declare no generative AI was used in writing.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors acknowledge axioms 1e (divergent/discriminant validity from IT literacy, data literacy, intelligence) and 2c (pre-post differences after learning) remained untested, so validation is explicitly incomplete and ongoing.
- The AI self-competency scale (and subscales emotion regulation, persuasion literacy) showed deviating correlation patterns (e.g., unrelated to prior use/learning in Study III), leading the authors to recommend these skills be viewed separately from the rest of AI literacy; persuasion literacy had questionable internal consistency (alpha=.66 in Study I).
- Convergent-validity tests (axiom 1c) are limited because the comparison instruments (Wang et al., Karaca/MAIRS-MS, Pinski & Benlian) have themselves only been validated for factorial structure; some of their subscales had low reliability (e.g., Wang Ethics alpha=.35, Awareness alpha=.50).
- The authors note the AI literacy test captures only the knowledge component of AI literacy; no objective test exists for the broader abilities/competencies, limiting axiom 1d testing.
- English and German versions were translated jointly by two researchers without structured translation methods, so cross-language equivalence is unverified; "use of AI" was flagged as semantically vague, and the short version still requires independent validation.
