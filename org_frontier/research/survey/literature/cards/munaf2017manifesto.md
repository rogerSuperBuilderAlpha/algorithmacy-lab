## Summary
This Perspective ("manifesto") argues that the reliability and efficiency of scientific research can be improved by adopting concrete, evidence-informed measures targeting specific threats to reproducibility. It frames the problem in terms of cognitive biases (apophenia, confirmation bias, hindsight bias) and structural threats (low statistical power, P-hacking, HARKing, publication bias, analytical flexibility, lack of data sharing) that inflate the rate of false findings. The authors organize proposed solutions into five themes — methods, reporting and dissemination, reproducibility, evaluation, and incentives — and summarize each with example initiatives and estimated current adoption levels in Table 1. Proposed measures include blinding, methodological training, independent methodological support, team science, study pre-registration, reporting guidelines (e.g., CONSORT, ARRIVE, TOP, PRISMA), open data/materials/code, Registered Reports, diversified pre- and post-publication peer review, and incentive reforms (badges, replication funding, hiring/promotion changes). The paper stresses that all measures require iterative evaluation and improvement and that none is guaranteed to be effective, closing with the Feynman dictum that "you must not fool yourself."

## Key facts it relies on
- One cited analysis estimates that 85% of biomedical research efforts are wasted; 90% of respondents to a Nature survey agreed there is a "reproducibility crisis."
- In a systematic review of fMRI studies, Carp found nearly as many unique analytical pipelines as studies; applying almost 7,000 analytical pipelines to a single fMRI dataset produced significant activation in over 90% of brain voxels in at least one analysis.
- Studies of statistical power persistently find it below (sometimes well below) 50% across time and disciplines; low power increases both false-positive and false-negative results.
- Disclosure example: the probability of a single variable reaching P < 0.05 by chance is 5%, but the probability of one of fifteen variables reaching P < 0.05 is 54%.
- Even with clinical trial pre-registration, one study found just 13% of trials published outcomes fully consistent with pre-registered commitments (COMPare); Franco et al. found ~40% of published psychology reports failed to mention one or more experimental conditions and ~70% failed to mention one or more outcome measures.
- As of November 2016, Registered Reports had been adopted by over 40 journals (including Nature Human Behaviour); the TOP guidelines had 757 journal and 64 organization signatories.
- Table 1 estimates current adoption as: * = <5%, ** = 5–30%, *** = 30–60%, **** = >60%; pre-registration is **** for clinical trials but * for other studies, and disclosure of conflicts of interest is ***.
- Open data badges (introduced by Psychological Science in January 2014) increased data sharing more than tenfold; by October 2015, 38% of Psychological Science articles had open data (Fig. 2, adapted from Kidwell et al.).
- A simulation optimality model (Higginson & Munafò) showed researchers maximizing "fitness" should run small studies with statistical power of only 10–40%; metascience produces over 2,000 relevant publications annually.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly state the proposed measures are not exhaustive, that offering a solution does not guarantee effectiveness, and that some solutions "may be ineffective or even harmful," requiring iterative evaluation (ideally via randomized trials, as proposed for ARRIVE).
- They note empirical evidence that reporting guidelines "may be necessary, but will not alone be sufficient" to address reporting biases (e.g., low compliance documented in COMPare and Franco et al.).
- They acknowledge that whether "crisis" is the appropriate term for the state of science is debatable, while maintaining there is substantial room for improvement.
- The paper concedes uncertainty about which studies deserve replication and the most efficient replication strategies, and cites a simulation (Smaldino & McElreath) suggesting replication alone may not suffice to eliminate false results.
- Several authors declare relevant competing interests (e.g., Nosek as executive director of the Center for Open Science; Percie du Sert leading the NC3Rs work that produced ARRIVE; Ware and CHDI links), which is relevant context for an advocacy/manifesto piece.
