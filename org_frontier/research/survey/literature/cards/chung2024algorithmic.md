## Summary
The study asks how "algorithmic knowledge" (understanding of how social media algorithms filter and present information) is distributed within and between countries, and whether it shapes how people respond to misinformation. The authors ran national quota-sampled surveys in the United States (N = 1,415), the United Kingdom (N = 1,435), South Korea (N = 1,798), and Mexico (N = 784) between April and September 2021, measuring algorithmic knowledge with nine yes/no questions about Facebook and X/Twitter feed mechanics (index range 0–9) and using structural equation modeling (path analysis) to relate sociodemographic predictors and corrective-action intentions. Within every country algorithmic knowledge was unevenly distributed across sociodemographic factors, but the specific predictors differed by country (e.g., political ideology dominant in the US/UK, gender most powerful in South Korea, only education and social media use in Mexico). Between countries, knowledge differed significantly (F(3, 5428) = 107.28, p < .001), with the US scoring highest, followed by the UK, Mexico, and South Korea—despite South Korea having the highest internet penetration (98%). In all four countries higher algorithmic knowledge predicted greater intention to take corrective actions against misinformation, providing empirical evidence for real-world (third-level digital divide) outcomes of the knowledge gap.

## Key facts it relies on
- Sample sizes: US N = 1,415, UK N = 1,435, South Korea N = 1,798 (Methods text states 1,718; Table B4 totals 1,798), Mexico N = 784; non-probability quota samples collected April–September 2021.
- Algorithmic knowledge index = number of correct answers to nine yes/no questions on Facebook/X-Twitter feed operation (range 0–9), adapted from Brodsky et al. (2020) and Powers (2017).
- Mean algorithmic knowledge: US M = 6.12 (SD = 1.73), UK M = 6.05 (SD = 1.74), Mexico M = 5.70 (SD = 1.49), South Korea M = 5.16 (SD = 1.76); cross-country difference F(3, 5428) = 107.28, p < .001, with Bonferroni post-hoc showing US/UK not different from each other but both higher than Mexico and South Korea (all ps < .001), and South Korea lowest of all.
- SEM (SPSS AMOS 28) standardized betas for algorithmic knowledge: age US −.01*, UK −.01**, Korea −.02***, Mexico −.00; political ideology US .15***, UK .11*** (top predictor in both); gender Korea .23** (top predictor); education Korea .17***, Mexico .05*; social media use UK .08*, Korea .09*, Mexico .11*; ethnicity US −.04* (not measured in Korea/Mexico).
- Outcome path (corrective actions) was positive and significant in all four countries: US 2.93**, UK 1.43***, Korea .07***, Mexico 1.11*.
- Country-specific significant predictors: US = political ideology and ethnicity; UK = age, political ideology, social media use; South Korea = age, gender, education, social media use; Mexico = education and social media use only.
- Internet penetration (2020): US 91%, UK 94%, South Korea 98%, Mexico 64%; social media access (2021): US 70%, UK 66%, South Korea 83%, Mexico 67%; over 90% of respondents in all four countries had social media access.
- Corrective-action intention measured on a 1–5 scale (adapted from Barnidge & Rojas 2014; Lim 2017): US M = 2.82 (α = .84), UK M = 2.58 (α = .83), Korea M = 2.19 (α = .85), Mexico M = 3.58 (α = .77).
- By age group, 18–24-year-olds had the highest algorithmic knowledge in the US (6.36) and UK (6.53), and 25–34-year-olds highest in South Korea (5.58); younger respondents generally scored higher.
- SEM model fit met criteria in all countries (CFI ≥ .981, RMSEA ≤ .050; e.g., US χ²=10.84, df=4, CFI=.991, RMSEA=.035).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors note their algorithmic-knowledge measure covers only two specific applications (Facebook and X/Twitter) and a single type of algorithmic operation (content recommendation for personal feeds), so it may not represent the breadth/depth of users' algorithmic knowledge or generalize to other platforms/algorithm types.
- They caution against assuming algorithmic knowledge is inherently tied to particular sociodemographic features or nationalities, since understanding of other algorithm aspects could yield different patterns; they point to more comprehensive scales (e.g., Dogruel et al., 2022) for future work.
- Samples are non-probability quota samples, and the authors acknowledge the anchor population statistics (Table B1) were the latest available but somewhat dated (e.g., 2011 UK Census, 2017 ACS), so more current statistics would better assess representativeness.
- The between-country knowledge ranking does not perfectly track social media use (Figure 2 vs Figure 1); the authors concede South Korea's low knowledge despite high access may partly reflect limitations of their measurement instrument rather than a pure substantive gap.
- There is an internal inconsistency in the reported South Korea N (1,798 in the abstract and Table B4 vs. 1,718 in the Methods section).
