## Summary
This review surveys the state of the art of statistical and survey-methodological research on measurement (non)invariance (MI), framed as a core prerequisite for valid comparison in the social sciences: only if a latent construct is measured "in the same way" across contexts can means and associations be compared rather than comparing "chopsticks with forks" (Chen, 2008). It traces the historical roots from early factor-analytic work through Jöreskog's (1971) multiple group confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA), which remains the predominant tool, and lays out the hierarchical levels of invariance (configural, metric/weak, scalar/strong, strict) and partial invariance. Because full exact invariance is rarely supported in real survey data, the paper's central contribution is reviewing the "next generation" of approaches developed in roughly the last ten years that relax exact MI while still permitting valid latent-variable comparisons: Bayesian approximate measurement invariance (BAMI), the alignment method, multilevel/MLSEM MI testing (random intercepts and random slopes), mixture multigroup factor analysis (MMG-FA), the Measurement Invariance Explorer (MIE), and the response-shift-true-change (RSTC) decomposition for longitudinal data. It then highlights the often-underemphasized survey-methodological side—how rating-scale design, survey mode/device, item wording, translation (TRAPD), and pretesting (cross-cultural cognitive interviewing and web probing) shape achievable MI before any data are analyzed. It closes with four future directions: conceptual/theoretical clarification of MI, statistical advances (ESEM, machine learning), methodological advances (combined cross-group/cross-time testing), and survey-methodological advances (supervised ML on existing comparative data to identify item features driving noninvariance).

## Key facts it relies on
- MI was defined by Horn and McArdle (1992, p. 117) as "whether or not, under different conditions of observing and studying a phenomenon, measurement observations yield measures of the same attribute"; MI implies comparability, not equality, of scores across groups.
- The MI hierarchy (after Meredith, 1993) is summarized in Table 1: configural (same items/constructs, no comparisons allowed), metric/weak (equal factor loadings; permits comparing unstandardized associations), scalar/strong (equal loadings and intercepts; permits comparing latent means), and strict (additionally equal error variances).
- MGCFA (Jöreskog, 1971; Sörbom, 1974) within SEM is the predominant testing tool; invariance levels are assessed by nested model comparison, with a common criterion that CFI should not deteriorate by more than approximately 0.01 (Chen, 2007), since the chi-square difference test was later shown to be too restrictive and sensitive to sample size.
- Partial invariance: Byrne et al. (1989) and Steenkamp and Baumgartner (1998) argued it may suffice if only two items have equal loadings and intercepts; recent simulations by Pokropek et al. (2019) concluded partial invariance is sufficient under various conditions and performs as well as more recent approaches.
- BAMI (Muthén and Asparouhov, 2012; van de Schoot et al., 2013) replaces exact zero constraints with a small-variance normal prior on cross-group parameter differences, N(0, σ0); the worked example shows that with group variances of 0.5 and covariance 0.495, V(λpg − λpg') = 0.01, giving the prior λpg − λpg' ~ N(0, σ0 = 0.01) so that λpg ≈ λpg' rather than exactly equal.
- The alignment method (Asparouhov and Muthén, 2014 for continuous; Muthén and Asparouhov, 2014 for categorical) automates the partial-invariance approach of Byrne et al. (1989) in a single-stage estimation with the same fit as the configural model; a rule of thumb is that it works when fewer than 20% of parameters are noninvariant (spread across groups), though Asparouhov and Muthén (2022) show it can work even when half of all measurement parameters are noninvariant.
- MLSEM treats group membership as random for MI testing across many groups (e.g., 50 countries); suggested minimum cluster counts are 50 (Maas and Hox, 2005) and 30 (Muthén and Asparouhov, 2018); Jak et al. (2013) define "cluster bias," and the random-intercept-and-slope framework (requiring Bayesian estimation) can distinguish uniform from nonuniform bias.
- MMG-FA (De Roover, 2021; De Roover et al., 2022) combines multigroup factor analysis with mixture modeling (Eq. 14) to classify groups into subsets sharing a user-specified invariance level; class number is chosen via BIC plus the CHull method, and it is implemented in Latent GOLD 6.0 ("emfa") and the mixmgfa R package; the Measurement Invariance Explorer (MIE; Rudnev, 2018–2022) instead quantifies pairwise noninvariance (e.g., dMACS effect size of Nye and Drasgow, 2011; ΔCFI) and visualizes group subsets as network graphs.
- The RSTC decomposition (Oort, 2005; Leitgöb et al., 2021) links response shift theory's three processes—scale recalibration, reprioritization, and reconceptualization—to MI levels and decomposes observed indicator mean change (Eq. 15) into true change, recalibration (Δνp), and reprioritization (Δλp), using Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition principles, with a fourfold extension separating pure reprioritization from an interaction term (Leitgöb and Seddig, forthcoming).
- Survey-design choices demonstrably cause noninvariance: varying rating-scale points (5, 7, or 11) and verbalization led to rejections of metric/scalar MI (Menold and Kemper, 2015; Menold and Tausch, 2016; Menold and Toepoel, 2022); web probing of the ISSP "civil disobedience" item across six countries (n = 3695; Behr et al., 2014) and the ISSP National Identity module across five countries (Meitinger, 2017) revealed cross-country differences in item interpretation underlying scalar noninvariance.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper states that, per a review by Davidov et al. (2014), although MI's importance was established more than 30 years ago (Meredith, 1993), only a very small share of comparative social-science studies (especially in sociology and political science) test MI before comparing constructs; when MI fails, equal scores can be artefacts and true differences can be masked.
- The authors stress that full exact metric and especially scalar invariance is rarely supported with real survey data (Byrne and van de Vijver, 2010), motivating the relaxed approaches—but they caution (quoting Davidov et al., 2015) that "no method to deal with noninvariance performs magic; there is a point at which one must conclude that MI simply does not hold."
- BAMI is cautioned against when differences are systematic or relatively large: the small-variance prior pulls strongly deviating estimates toward the group average and inflates invariant parameters above their true values (Muthén and Asparouhov, 2012); the alignment method is "not universally applicable" and may fail to recover data-generating parameters when a simpler, more-invariant representation exists (e.g., random intercepts/loadings).
- The authors note acknowledged scope limits of newer tools: MIE is a young heuristic (not a formal model) lacking extensive simulation evaluation and gives only model-level, not item-level, noninvariance; MMG-FA is currently limited to maximum-likelihood estimation assuming continuous, multivariate-normal items and an equal number of factors across subsets.
- The paper restricts itself to the MGCFA/SEM tradition and explicitly limits its presentation of the IRT/DIF framework (item bias, differential item functioning), pointing readers elsewhere; it also flags an ongoing conceptual debate in Sociological Methods & Research (Welzel et al., 2021/2022; Fischer et al., 2022; Meuleman et al., 2022) over the relevance of MI and how it is routinely assessed.
