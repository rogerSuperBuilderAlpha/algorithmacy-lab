## Summary
This single-author systematic review (preregistered at OSF, conducted per PRISMA 2020) asks which AI literacy measurement instruments exist and how psychometrically sound they are, to help researchers and educators choose scales for assessing AI literacy. Searching Scopus and arXiv (search run June 18, 2024) plus reference-list and reverse-citation searches, the author screened 5560 deduplicated records down to 22 studies validating or revalidating 16 distinct AI literacy scales. Each scale's quality was assessed against the COSMIN measurement properties (content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, cross-cultural validity, reliability, measurement error, construct validity, responsiveness) plus interpretability and feasibility, with overall evidence graded via the GRADE approach. The headline finding is that scales generally show good structural validity and internal consistency, but few have been tested for content validity, reliability, construct validity, or responsiveness, and none have been tested for cross-cultural validity or measurement error. Most studies report no interpretability indicators and almost none provide raw/open data. The review gives population-specific recommendations (e.g., AILS for general population; AI literacy test and ChatGPT literacy scale for higher education; AI-CI, AILQ, and Kim & Lee's for secondary; Intelligent TPACK for teachers) and notes that of the 16 scales only 3 are performance-based versus 13 self-report.

## Key facts it relies on
- The initial search yielded 5574 results; after deduplication 5560 studies remained; 5501 were excluded at title/abstract screening, leaving 59 full texts; 44 were excluded, and additions from reference lists (1) and reverse searches (6) yielded a final 22 studies validating/revalidating 16 scales.
- Of the 16 scales, only 3 are performance-based (AI literacy test, AI-CI, SAIL4ALL) versus 13 self-report scales relying mostly on Likert items.
- All scales were constructed in the last three years; the oldest is MAIRS-MS from 2021. Only MAIRS-MS, SNAIL, and AILS have been revalidated by another study.
- Target populations: most scales target the general population or higher education students; three target secondary education students (AI-CI, AILQ, Kim & Lee's) and one targets teachers (Intelligent TPACK).
- Overall, scales demonstrated good structural validity and internal consistency, but only a few were tested for content validity, reliability, construct validity, and responsiveness; none were tested for cross-cultural validity or measurement error.
- Across scales, three core competencies recur: technical understanding of AI, societal impact of AI, and AI ethics; authors diverge on whether higher-order skills (creating and evaluating AI) are core components. MAILS treated "create AI" as a separate construct, whereas AILQ includes creating AI as core.
- COSMIN considers content validity the most important measurement property and requires both experts and the target population to be involved for content validity to be adequate; the author did not evaluate criterion validity because as of January 2024 there was no gold-standard AI literacy instrument.
- Floor/ceiling effects were flagged when more than 15% of respondents achieved the lowest or highest possible score; SNAIL shows a floor effect (almost half of items had >15% lowest-score responses), while MAILS is the only scale with evidence of minimal floor and ceiling effects.
- Feasibility/completion times where reported: MAILS ~16:05 min (most within 20 min), SAIL4ALL true/false 8:51 and Likert 9:38, SNAIL 5:39 (original; most within 10 min); Laupichler et al. (SNAIL) was essentially the only group providing open data.
- The conceptual backbone draws on Ng et al.'s four-aspect AI literacy conceptualization (know and understand, use, evaluate, ethics) and Long & Magerko's 17 competencies across five areas (What is AI? / What can AI do? / How does AI work? / How should AI be used? / How do people perceive AI?).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The author explicitly notes the review was performed by a single person, which "might have caused some bias" in quality assessment, though COSMIN criteria are quantitatively defined.
- Search scope was limited to Scopus and arXiv (English papers in Scopus), so scales in grey literature may have been missed; the author argues reverse searches in Scopus and Google Scholar mitigated this.
- The author flags a replicability concern: almost no studies provided open raw data, which both prevents computing some quality indicators (e.g., multiple-group factor analyses need raw data) and may contribute to the replicability crisis; most studies also did not report missing-data percentages or handling strategies.
- No scale showed positive evidence across all COSMIN measurement properties, and most studies suffered from poor methodological rigour; interpretability and feasibility remain largely unknown due to under-reporting.
- The self-efficacy vs. self-reported AI literacy distinction is acknowledged as "heavily blurred," leading the author to adopt a deliberately inclusive relevancy criterion (including GSE-6AI and Wang & Chuang's AI self-efficacy scale).
