## Summary
This is a methodological primer (explicitly not a systematic review) that distills the technical literature and the authors' own scale-building experience into a structured, jargon-reduced guide for developing and validating multi-item scales of latent constructs in health, social, and behavioral research. The authors organize scale development into three phases spanning nine steps: (1) item development — domain identification/item generation and content validity; (2) scale development — pre-testing questions, survey administration and sample size, item reduction, and factor extraction; and (3) scale evaluation — tests of dimensionality, reliability, and validity. For each step they define key concepts, describe required tasks, flag common pitfalls, and give a published example of best practice. The primer covers both Classical Test Theory (CTT) and Item Response Theory (IRT) approaches, though the authors note it leans toward CTT. Its stated goal is to make scale development more approachable and transparent so researchers can purposively choose and rigorously execute the steps appropriate to their study and resources.

## Key facts it relies on
- The framework is three phases / nine steps: item development (steps 1-2), scale development (steps 3-6: pre-testing, survey administration/sample size, item reduction, factor extraction), and scale evaluation (steps 7-9: dimensionality, reliability, validity).
- Content validity is assessed by typically 5-7 expert judges using the content validity ratio, content validity index, or Cohen's kappa; the authors recommend Cohen's kappa as most efficient, and note an increase in the number of experts increases robustness of ratings.
- Recommended pre-testing uses cognitive interviews with 5-15 interviewees in 2-3 rounds (or until saturation); the initial item pool should be at least twice as long as the desired final scale (some recommend five times).
- Recommended sample size is about 10 respondents per item (10:1 ratio) and/or 200-300 observations; Comrey and Lee's graded scale: 100 = poor, 200 = fair, 300 = good, 500 = very good, >=1,000 = excellent; Guadagnoli and Velicer suggest a minimum of 300-450 with replication required if n < 300.
- Response-scale guidance (Krosnick and Presser): 2-3 point responses have lower reliability than 5-7 point Likert scales, with gains leveling off after seven points; five points recommended for unipolar items, seven for bipolar items.
- Item reduction thresholds: inter-item and adjusted item-total correlations below 0.30 are flagged for potential deletion; general-purpose scales focus on medium-difficulty items (item difficulty proportion 0.4-0.6 under CTT, which ranges 0.0-1.0).
- Factor extraction: items with factor loadings below 0.30 contribute <10% variation and are inadequate; retain items loading 0.40 and above; Rasch single-factor fit uses infit/outfit mean-square residuals >0.4 and <1.6.
- Model fit thresholds for dimensionality (Table 2): RMSEA <= 0.06, TLI >= 0.95, CFI >= 0.95, SRMR <= 0.08, WRMR < 1.0; chi-square is noted as overly sensitive to sample size.
- Reliability: Cronbach's alpha of 0.70 is often accepted as satisfactory, but Nunnally recommends >=0.90 (with 0.95 desirable); other statistics include Raykov's rho, ordinal alpha, McDonald's Omega, Revelle's beta, and test-retest reliability.
- Validity types covered: content (step 2), criterion (predictive and concurrent), and construct (convergent, discriminant, differentiation by known groups, correlation analysis); the authors prefer bivariate regression over correlation for quantifying validity, and note scale validity is supported if at least two forms of construct validity are examined in addition to predictive validity.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly state this is a primer / "jumping off point," not a systematic review, and that it is an amalgamation of technical literature plus the authors' own experiences rather than an exhaustive treatment.
- The review acknowledges it leans toward the CTT approach and that a comprehensive review of IRT modeling would be complementary; several topics are deliberately omitted, including interpreting scale output, designating cut-offs, when indices (rather than scales) are more appropriate, and principles for re-testing scales in new populations.
- The authors stress that not all nine steps are needed for every study (e.g., validating existing scales may use only the last four steps) and that they cannot say which steps are most important; resource constraints (time, money, participant attention) are acknowledged as real limits to rigor.
- The authors note a concurrent validity limitation: it fails with small sample sizes due to large sampling errors, and appropriate "gold standard" criterion variables often are not available, which may explain its omission in many validation studies.
