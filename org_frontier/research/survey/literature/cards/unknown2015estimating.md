## Summary
The Open Science Collaboration conducted direct replications of 100 experimental and correlational studies sampled from three 2008 psychology journals (Psychological Science, JPSP, and JEP:LMC), using high-powered designs and original materials when available, with protocols reviewed by original authors and pre-registered. The question was how reproducible current published psychology is. Whereas 97% of the original studies reported statistically significant results, only 36% of replications produced significant results in the original direction, and replication effect sizes were about half the magnitude of original effects (mean r = 0.197 vs 0.403). Across five complementary indicators (significance/P values, original effect size within replication 95% CI, effect-size comparison, meta-analytic combination, and subjective "did it replicate?" judgments), reproducibility was modest: 47% of original effect sizes fell within the replication 95% CI, 39% were subjectively judged to have replicated, and combining original plus replication results left 68% with significant effects (assuming no bias in originals). Correlational analyses found that replication success was better predicted by the strength of the original evidence (e.g., lower original P value, larger original effect size) than by characteristics of the original or replication teams. The authors frame the results as showing room to improve reproducibility while also reflecting science behaving as it should.

## Key facts it relies on
- 100 replications were completed by 270 contributing authors; studies were drawn from a quasi-random sampling frame of 2008 articles in PSCI, JPSP, and JEP:LMC. 84 of 100 (84%) replications were of the last reported study in the article; to-be-replicated articles averaged 2.99 studies (SD = 1.78).
- 97 of 100 (97%) original studies reported positive/significant results (four had P just above 0.05: 0.0508, 0.0514, 0.0516, 0.0567, but were interpreted as positive). Original mean P value = 0.028; replication mean P value = 0.302.
- Only 35 of 97 (36.1%, 95% CI 26.6%–46.2%) replications were significant in the original direction, versus ~89 expected given average replication power of 0.92 (median 0.95); McNemar χ²(1) = 59.1, P < 0.001.
- Replication effects were about half the magnitude of originals: original mean effect size r = 0.403 (SD 0.188) vs replication r = 0.197 (SD 0.257), Wilcoxon W = 7137, P < 0.001; 82 of 99 (82.8%) study-pairs showed a stronger original effect (binomial P < 0.001); original and replication effect sizes correlated Spearman r = 0.51.
- Coverage/effect-size indicator: 47.4% replication success rate overall (30 of 73, 41.1%, of replication CIs contained the original effect size for the meta-analytic subset, vs 78.5% expected; plus 68.2% of 22 studies using other test statistics).
- Meta-analysis combining original and replication effects (fixed-effect): 51 of 75 (68%) effects had 95% CIs excluding 0. Subjective assessment: 39 of 100 judged to have replicated.
- Reproducibility was higher in cognitive than social psychology by significance testing: 21 of 42 (50%) cognitive effects replicated vs 14 of 55 (25%) social; effect-size declines were similar across journals/disciplines (χ²(3) = 2.45, P = 0.48). Main/simple effects replicated more than interactions: 23 of 49 (47%) vs 8 of 37 (22%).
- Initial strength of evidence predicted replication: 20 of 32 (63%) originals with P < 0.001 replicated; original P value correlated negatively (r = -0.327 with significance replication), larger original effect sizes positively (r = 0.304); team experience/expertise, importance, and self-assessed quality showed little association.
- Effect sizes were converted to correlation coefficients (r) with Fisher transformation; five reproducibility indicators were positively correlated with each other (r from 0.22 to 0.96, median 0.57). Of 488 articles in the 2008 issues, 158 (32%) became eligible, 111 (70%) were claimed, producing 113 replications, of which 100 (88%) were completed.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors state explicitly that the project established neither which effects are true (zero) nor which are false (zero); single replications give "broad-and-shallow" evidence with little precision about any individual effect, contrasting with "narrow-and-deep" Many Labs approaches.
- Stated limitations: it is unknown how far findings extend to the rest of psychology or other disciplines; not all eligible articles were replicated, only one study per article and one statistical result per study were tested, and more resource-intensive studies were under-sampled, so residual selection-bias impact is unknown.
- A failure to replicate does not establish the original was a false positive: unanticipated differences in sample, setting, or procedure could moderate effects; conversely, low-powered original designs plus publication bias favoring positive results are offered as a plausible explanation for systematically smaller replication effects (replications had no publication/selection/reporting bias due to pre-registration and confirmatory analysis plans).
- The meta-analytic "68% significant" figure assumes no bias in the original results; if publication/selection/reporting biases fully explain the differences, the replication estimates (not the meta-analytic or original ones) would be the better effect-size estimates.
