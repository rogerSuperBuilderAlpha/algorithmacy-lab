## Summary
The paper, a sequel to Rogosa's (1980) critique of cross-lagged correlations, argues that the widely used cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) fails when constructs have trait-like, time-invariant stability: its autoregressive parameters only control for temporal (rank-order) stability, so the cross-lagged estimates do not reflect the true within-person process and can yield erroneous conclusions about the presence, predominance, and sign of causal influences. The authors propose the random intercepts cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM), which adds person-specific random intercepts (factor loadings fixed to 1) to partial out stable between-person differences so the lagged parameters pertain to within-person dynamics. They derive analytically how the CLPM standardized cross-lagged parameters relate to the RI-CLPM parameters (Appendix B / Equation 5), then use Mplus simulations (four handpicked scenarios, N=200, 1,000 replications, two-wave data generated by an RI-CLPM and fit with the CLPM) to show the CLPM produces spurious results on all three objectives of cross-lagged panel research. They situate the RI-CLPM relative to the STARTS, ALT, LCS, and (modified) LST/TSO models, and demonstrate a modeling strategy on three-wave empirical data from Soenens et al. (2008) on parenting and adolescent depression, where the CLPM and RI-CLPM lead to substantively different conclusions. They conclude many published CLPM lagged parameters likely fail to reflect actual within-person mechanisms.

## Key facts it relies on
- The CLPM is just identified (saturated, perfect fit) with only two waves of data; a PsycINFO search of 2012 cross-lagged publications found 115 peer-reviewed articles, 111 applications reporting 117 datasets, and 45% of the datasets used only two waves.
- In the CLPM the autoregressive parameters capture only "temporal stability" (rank-order stability that decays over time), not trait-like time-invariant stability; the RI-CLPM adds random intercepts (latent factors with all loadings constrained to 1) to separate within-person dynamics from stable between-person differences.
- The CLPM is nested under the RI-CLPM (obtained by fixing the variances and covariance of the random intercepts to zero); the RI-CLPM needs at least three waves for identification (1 df with 21 observed statistics vs. 20 covariance-structure parameters), and the nesting comparison follows a chi-bar-square (not regular chi-square) distribution because two variances are fixed at the parameter-space boundary.
- Equation 5 (Appendix B) expresses the CLPM standardized cross-lagged parameter as a complex function of the RI-CLPM cross-lagged parameter, the within-person autoregressive parameter, the within-person deviation covariance and variance, the trait-component variance var(omega), and the between-trait covariance cov(omega, kappa).
- Simulations used Mplus, N=200, 1,000 replications, two-wave data generated by an RI-CLPM with within-person variances set to 1, between-person variances of 2 or 3, and autoregressive parameters of .5; each scenario instantiated Simpson's paradox (within- and between-person covariances of opposite sign).
- Simulation Model 1 (no true cross-lagged effects, between-person variance 3, between-person covariance -2): average estimates were about -.118 and -.120, with coverage rates of .105 and .103 — about 90% of cases falsely detected a significant negative cross-lagged effect.
- Simulation Model 2 (true cross-lagged parameters of .3): average estimates about -.003, coverage rates .951 and .945 — about 95% of cases failed to detect the substantial true effects.
- Simulation Models 3 and 4 (asymmetric true effects, e.g., beta=-.3, gamma=.1) showed the CLPM identifies the wrong variable as causally dominant; Model 4 additionally produced a significant cross-lagged estimate of opposite sign to the generating parameter.
- In the empirical Soenens et al. (2008) data (396 students for the first analysis, three waves one year apart), the RI-CLPM fit well while the CLPM fit poorly (e.g., RMSEA .113); for parental responsiveness vs. depression the CLPM implied a significant negative effect of responsiveness on later depression whereas the RI-CLPM found no reciprocal influence, with the trait-like components negatively correlated (-.443, SE .067, p<.001).
- The bias from ignoring the within-/between-person distinction does not vanish as sample size increases (only the variability of estimates and standard errors decreases).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors stress the RI-CLPM is "a rather restrictive model" and not necessarily the best alternative; notably it includes no measurement error, and they note that once stable between-person variance is partialed out, measurement error accounts for a larger share of the remaining variance, increasing its distorting effect.
- They acknowledge the four simulation scenarios were handpicked to illustrate contradictory cases and are not claimed to represent realistic or typical psychological processes; true RI-CLPM parameter values for real data are unknown since the model is not yet used in practice.
- They flag alternatives with their own costs: the STARTS model often needs 10+ waves and per Cole, Martin, and Steiger (2005) frequently yields improper solutions; the ALT and LCS models have a recursiveness problem; continuous-time SEM (Oud and colleagues) better handles varying lags; and BHOUM allows random/varying-lag bivariate effects but cannot handle asymmetric within-person cross-lagged effects.
- The paper inherits Rogosa's (1980) framing that strong causal claims require experimental designs; the authors confine themselves to Granger causality and note researchers often use softer terms (reciprocal relationship, influence, impact) that still imply a causal mechanism.
