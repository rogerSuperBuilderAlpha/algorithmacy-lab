## Summary
The study asks how prevalent algorithmic misconceptions are in the general population, how they are distributed across demographic groups, and what information sources are associated with them. Using a representative survey of the Dutch population (N = 2,106, fieldwork July 19–August 9, 2019), the authors measured belief in five true/false misconception statements about media algorithms (e.g., algorithms are independent of human influence, are unbiased, can solve any problem, reason like humans, will replace media workers). A large share of respondents endorsed each misconception, ranging from 43.64% to 63.96%, with a mean of 2.31 of 5 misconceptions held per person. Misconceptions were significantly more common among older, lower-educated, and female respondents. Respondents reporting no information source, or relying on friends/family, held more misconceptions, whereas own experience, media, and school were associated with fewer. A multiple regression confirmed age, gender, and education (block 1) plus information sources (block 2) as predictors, explaining 24.5% of variance total. The authors frame these gaps as a potential "algorithmic divide" and a new digital divide, and call for algorithmic literacy initiatives.

## Key facts it relies on
- Representative survey of the Dutch population, N = 2,106 (net), from a gross sample of 6,000 contacted, i.e., a 35% response rate; data collected July 19–August 9, 2019 (21 days); mean age 54.18 (SD = 15.59), 48% women.
- Five misconceptions measured as true/false items (correct answer always "false"), format adopted from Taylor and Kowalski (2004): (MC1) algorithms completely independent of human influence; (MC2) always neutral/objective and free of bias; (MC3) can solve every problem in society; (MC4) same critical reasoning/intelligence as humans; (MC5) will replace human workers in the media sector.
- Total-sample prevalence of holding each misconception: MC1 53.66%, MC2 52.85%, MC3 43.64%, MC4 54.23%, MC5 63.96% (MC5 most widespread).
- Aggregated misconception index (0–5, one point per incorrect answer) had M = 2.31, SD = 1.75.
- Age was significantly associated with all misconceptions except MC5 (χ² for MC5 = 3.64, ns); older groups held more. Gender significantly associated with all five (women more than men). Education significantly associated with all five (lower-educated more).
- Information sources by misconception index: respondents with more misconceptions relied less on own experience, media, and school, and were especially likely to report no information source (up to 74.48% at index = 5).
- Multiple regression: Block 1 demographics explained 11% of variance; age (β = 0.09, p < 0.001), gender (β = 0.11, women more, p < 0.001), moderate education (β = −0.08, p < 0.01), high education (β = −0.15, p < 0.001). Block 2 information sources added incremental R² = 13.5%; own experience (β = −0.12, p < 0.001), media (β = −0.09, p < 0.001), no information source (β = 0.25, p < 0.001), friends/family (β = 0.04, p < 0.05), school (β = −0.02, ns). Total R² = 24.5%.
- Five misconceptions were derived from a literature review (e.g., de Saint Laurent, 2018; Emmert-Streib et al., 2020; First, 2018; Roffel & Evans, 2018); list described as non-exhaustive.
- Framing draws on "algorithmic knowledge gap" (Cotter & Reisdorf, 2020) and "algorithmic divide" (Carmi & Yates, 2020); region (Nielsen division) was described for the sample but excluded from statistical analyses.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly call the analysis "very exploratory and descriptive" and note many unanswered questions (root causes of misconceptions, cross-country comparability, whether driven by technological solutionism vs. lack of critical thinking, downstream effects on trust/use).
- They acknowledge the five-misconception list is not exhaustive and that some of these beliefs (e.g., MC5 about replacing media workers) might not be misconceptions in a far-distant future, making the "incorrect" coding time-dependent.
- The study did not measure familiarity with technology / digital media or digital literacy directly, limiting ability to disentangle these from demographic predictors.
- Information sources are self-reported (perceived) attributions and the design is cross-sectional, so source–misconception links are associational, not causal; the "other" source category was folded into the five and dropped.
- The Netherlands ranks among EU leaders in digital skills (Eurostat, 2020), so the authors note prevalence may be even higher in lower-literacy countries; generalizability beyond the Dutch context is untested.
