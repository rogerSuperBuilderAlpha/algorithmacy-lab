## Summary
This is a short "Discussion" article in Business & Information Systems Engineering that reports on a panel held at the 19th International Conference on Wirtschaftsinformatik (WI 2024) in Würzburg, Germany, on September 18, 2024, devoted to the open questions surrounding algorithmic literacy. (Note: the article's actual authorship is a nine-person group led by Irina Heimbach et al., not a single author named Marc Schmitt as listed in this card's frontmatter.) Rather than presenting an empirical study, it synthesizes the views of five IS scholars (panelists Jussupow, Legner, Meythaler, Müller, and Pinski) around three themes: how to define algorithmic literacy and distinguish it from related concepts, how to measure it, and how to enhance it. The paper adopts a working view of algorithmic literacy as "the capability of humans to understand and effectively interact with algorithmic systems" and notes there is no clear, agreed definition in IS research. Key panel conclusions are that algorithmic literacy is an emerging, multifaceted, context-dependent construct that exists on a spectrum (not a binary), builds on related literacies (statistical, graph, computer, and especially data literacy), and must be adapted to different user profiles. On measurement, the panel favored objective, scenario-based and behavior-based assessments over subjective self-report scales, while acknowledging all measurement approaches face challenges. On enhancement, it advocated a balance of structured education and general awareness, integrating coding/algorithmic thinking into school curricula early, and supporting transparency through regulation.

## Key facts it relies on
- The article is a panel-discussion synthesis from WI 2024 (Würzburg, Germany, September 18, 2024); it summarizes views on three themes — conceptualization, measurement, and education of algorithmic literacy.
- Five IS scholars served as expert panelists: Ekaterina Jussupow, Christine Legner, Antonia Meythaler, Oliver Müller, and Marc Pinski; Legner and Müller provided the organizational perspective while Pinski, Jussupow, and Meythaler offered an individual-user perspective.
- Working definition used: algorithmic literacy is "the capability of humans to understand and effectively interact with algorithmic systems" (Benbya et al. 2024); the communication-science definition (Dogruel et al. 2022, p. 118) frames it as being aware of algorithm use, knowing how algorithms work, critically evaluating algorithmic decision-making, and having skills to cope with or influence algorithmic operations.
- The paper cites a survey of more than 6,000 people in the USA, Canada, the UK, Germany, and Australia (nationally representative panels) where only 62% had seen, read, or heard anything about AI, and the majority self-reported low understanding of how these systems work (Curtis et al. 2023).
- It traces a literacy progression: computer literacy → internet literacy → information privacy literacy → data literacy → AI literacy / algorithmic literacy, noting the shift toward AI literacy especially after the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022.
- Two main measurement approaches are identified — subjective scales and simple objective measures (Dogruel et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022); subjective scales are prone to over-/underestimation (Mabe and West 1982), and objective scales tend to be oriented toward IT professionals.
- Panelist Pinski lists three measurement challenges: construct complexity (subjective measures distorted by biases like the Dunning-Kruger effect; objective measures too narrow), rapid technological progress rendering tools obsolete (e.g., prompting skills with ChatGPT), and the broad/context-dependent nature requiring precise definition and domain context.
- Legner distinguishes three organizational user profiles ("personas"): general users (amateurs), domain-specific professionals (e.g., marketing or supply chain analysts), and data experts (e.g., data engineers, analytics specialists), each with distinct learning needs.
- Müller proposes conceptualizing algorithmic literacy as two dimensions — interpreting/evaluating system inputs and outputs (as in data literacy) and understanding procedural/internal aspects — and extending "literacy" beyond reading to "writing" (building or designing algorithmic systems).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper itself states there is no clear definition of algorithmic literacy in IS research and no mature instruments to measure AI literacy due to high variation in conceptualizations; it is a discussion/opinion piece, not an empirical study, and offers no new data or validated instrument.
- Panelist Jussupow cautions that a universal framework for algorithmic literacy is appealing but risks overlooking the nuances of specific socio-technical systems and their affordances; different systems (e.g., conversational vs. high-stakes predictive AI) demand different literacies.
- Panelist Pinski warns against overextending the concept — including advanced programming (e.g., coding in C++) under "algorithmic literacy" stretches it beyond its intended lay audience; the takeaways advise caution against conflating basic literacy with expert programming skills.
- The paper notes a risk that disparities in algorithmic literacy could create a new form of digital divide (Gran et al. 2021), and that many algorithmic systems are opaque/secretive, making transparency efforts (potentially via regulation) necessary but challenging (Meythaler).
- Scope is narrow: the panel featured scholars from Germany and Switzerland only, reflecting a regional/European IS perspective rather than a globally representative or systematically sampled view.
