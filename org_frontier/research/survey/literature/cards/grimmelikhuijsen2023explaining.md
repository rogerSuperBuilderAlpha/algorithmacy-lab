## Summary
The paper asks how algorithmic transparency affects the perceived trustworthiness of automated street-level decision-making. Drawing on procedural fairness theory, the author distinguishes two core dimensions of algorithmic transparency—accessibility (public/expert availability of source code, model, and data) and explainability (giving a human-understandable reason for a decision)—and tests their effects on trust in both the algorithm and the human bureaucrat. Two scenario-based survey experiments, each a 2×2 between-subjects design, were fielded in the representative Dutch LISS panel (897 respondents): a low-discretion denied visa application and a high-discretion welfare-fraud house search. Results show that explainability has a more pronounced effect on trust than accessibility, that transparency effects extend partly to trust in the human decision-maker, and that effects are not robust across the two decision contexts. In the visa experiment only explainability raised trust in the algorithm, whereas in the welfare-fraud experiment both accessibility and explainability raised trust in the algorithm and in the bureaucrat. A formal three-way interaction test, however, found no significant interaction with study context, so the context difference cannot be confirmed. The author concludes that transparency-as-accessibility alone is insufficient and governments must address explainability to foster citizen trust.

## Key facts it relies on
- Two survey experiments used a between-subjects 2×2 factorial design, independently manipulating accessibility (high/low) and explainability (high/low); each participant did both experiments with randomized order and a distraction task between them.
- Data came from the Dutch LISS panel (a true probability sample); 1,125 members were approached and 897 completed the survey (79.7 percent response rate); pooled across both experiments the sample size is 1,803.
- The dependent variables were perceived trustworthiness of the "computer system" and of the human employee, each a 4-item 7-point scale; Cronbach's alpha was 0.92/0.92 for the algorithm (exp 1/exp 2) and 0.95/0.94 for the bureaucrat.
- Study 1 (visa): explainability significantly raised trust in the algorithm (Std. Beta = 0.33, p < .001), while accessibility was non-significant (Std. Beta = 0.16, p = .090); no significant transparency effects on trust in the bureaucrat. R²/adjusted R² = 0.018/0.015 (algorithm), 0.009/0.006 (bureaucrat).
- Study 2 (welfare fraud): both accessibility (Std. Beta = 0.27, p = .003) and explainability (Std. Beta = 0.38, p < .001) significantly raised trust in the algorithm; explainability also raised trust in the bureaucrat (p = .002) and accessibility was borderline (p = .051). R²/adjusted R² = 0.030/0.027 (algorithm), 0.014/0.011 (bureaucrat).
- Across the conditions, mean trust in the algorithm rose from the no-access/low-explainable cell to the access/high-explainable cell (Study 1: 3.23 → 3.69; Study 2: 2.90 → 3.61), and the human bureaucrat was generally trusted more than the algorithm.
- Hypothesis summary: H2 (explainability → trust in algorithm) supported in both studies; H1 (accessibility → trust in algorithm) rejected in visa, supported in welfare; H3 (accessibility → trust in bureaucrat) rejected in both; H4 (explainability → trust in bureaucrat) rejected in visa, supported in welfare; H5 (stronger effect in high-discretion) inconclusive.
- For H5, a three-way interaction with "Study" showed no significant interaction effects; the pooled design (n = 1,803) could detect effect sizes of f = 0.076 at p < .05 and power = 0.90, considered relatively large for an ordinal interaction.
- The framing is grounded in the Dutch SyRI welfare-fraud risk system (17 datasets, 12,000 addresses in Rotterdam on 25 June / 7 July 2018), which a Dutch court banned in 2020 partly for lack of algorithmic transparency.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The author flags low external validity: these are hypothetical "clean" survey vignettes, and effects may be weaker in the real world (Gaines, Kuklinski and Quirk 2007); field experiments with real or mock algorithms are recommended.
- The transparency manipulations are "rather crude" (all-or-nothing accessibility/explainability); the paper calls for testing more subtle, gradual transparency treatments.
- The experiment tested a "positive" scenario in which transparency revealed a clear, apparently non-biased reason for a negative decision; it did not reveal algorithmic bias, leaving open how transparency about biased/discriminatory algorithms would affect trust.
- The R² values are very small (≤ 0.030), indicating transparency explains only a modest share of variance in trust; and because the cross-context interaction test was non-significant, the claimed context-dependence of effects cannot be asserted with certainty.
- The same 4-item scale was applied to both the algorithm and the human; the author acknowledges (citing McKnight et al. 2011) that trustworthiness of technology and of humans may be conceptually distinct, so applying identical measures could be questioned.
