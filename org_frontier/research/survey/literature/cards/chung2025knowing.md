## Summary
This survey study asks how U.S. young adults' algorithmic awareness and knowledge relate to their attitudes and behaviors toward social media content, what predicts that awareness/knowledge, and how much algorithmic-literacy education they received in secondary school. Using a non-probability quota sample of 348 U.S. adults aged 18-25 recruited via Prolific in May 2025, the author measured algorithmic awareness/knowledge with the Zarouali et al. (2021) AMCA scale (plus an added fifth dimension) and modeled relationships using bivariate correlation, OLS regression, and structural equation modeling (SEM in SPSS AMOS 28). Higher algorithmic awareness and knowledge were associated with greater concern about content (lower perceived reliability, lower perceived exposure to diverse views), but paradoxically with weaker intentions to correct misinformation or to engage in perspective-taking. Frequent social media use and conservative political orientation predicted lower algorithmic awareness and knowledge, and nearly 40% of respondents reported little-to-no formal education about algorithms in secondary school. The author proposes "algorithmic cynicism" (a resignation/lack-of-agency response, paralleling privacy cynicism) as a possible explanation, but explicitly frames the cross-sectional findings as correlational rather than causal.

## Key facts it relies on
- Sample: national non-probability quota sample of U.S. young adults ages 18-25, N = 348 (48.3% female, M age = 22.6), via Prolific in May 2025, quota-matched on age and gender from simplified U.S. Census data.
- Algorithmic awareness/knowledge measured with Zarouali et al.'s (2021) Algorithmic Media Content Awareness (AMCA) scale across four dimensions (content filtering, automated decisions, human-algorithm interplay, ethical considerations) plus an added fifth dimension (motivations behind algorithms); M = 3.99, SD = .65, α = .80 (subdimensions α = .79-.83).
- Correlations (Table A1): algorithmic awareness/knowledge negatively associated with perceived content reliability (r = -.40, p < .01), perceived exposure to diverse views (r = -.20, p < .01), and corrective action (r = -.23, p < .01), but not with perspective taking (r = -.08, n.s.).
- OLS regressions: algorithmic awareness/knowledge significantly predicted lower perceived content reliability (β = -.438, p < .001) and lower corrective action (β = -.172, p < .05), but not perceived exposure to diverse views or perspective taking.
- Predictors of algorithmic awareness/knowledge (multiple regression): higher SNS use predicted lower awareness/knowledge (B = -.228, SE = .050, p < .001) and more conservative political views predicted lower awareness/knowledge (political views B = .059, SE = .017, p < .001); age, gender, income, education, ethnicity, and developmental-disability status were not significant (all ps > .05).
- SEM (SPSS AMOS 28) model fit: χ²(40) = 1.928, p < .001; CFI = .977; RMSEA = .052 (90% CI: .034, .069); thresholds used were CFI ≥ .95 and RMSEA ≤ .06.
- Algorithm education: respondents reported low formal education about social media algorithms in middle/high school (M = 2.86, SD = 1.36); almost 40% indicated they had received little to no education on the topic.
- Other measures: perceived content reliability (M = 2.97, SD = .95, r = .67); perceived exposure to diverse viewpoints (M = 3.33, SD = .92, r = .54); corrective action intention (M = 3.34, SD = 1.01, α = .83); perspective-taking intention (M = 3.70, SD = .84, α = .78); composite social media use (M = 3.34, SD = .71).
- Algorithmic literacy defined (Dogruel et al., 2022, p. 4) as awareness of algorithm use, knowing how algorithms work, ability to critically evaluate algorithmic decision-making, and skills to cope with/influence algorithmic operations; framed as multidimensional (cognitive, affective, behavioral; Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2023).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The author states the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference; the key sequential-ignorability assumption of mediation analysis cannot be verified, so reported path coefficients and indirect effects should be interpreted as correlational, not causal (citing Rohrer et al., 2022, on pitfalls of popular path models).
- Analyses were exploratory and not pre-registered, and rely on a convenience crowdwork sample (Prolific); the author notes mediational claims are especially fragile in observational research where the no-omitted-variables assumption rarely holds.
- "Algorithmic cynicism" is proposed as an interpretive concept but was not directly measured in the study; the author explicitly cannot confirm whether it is part of algorithmic literacy and leaves this to future work.
- Measurement focused specifically on content-recommendation algorithms for personal feeds; respondents' awareness of other algorithm types (facial recognition, automated moderation, targeted advertising) was not captured, limiting generalizability.
- The sample is somewhat more educated than the U.S. average (99.7% with at least a high school diploma, 44.3% with a four-year degree vs. national figures); region data were not collected, limiting representativeness assessment.
