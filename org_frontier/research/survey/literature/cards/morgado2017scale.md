## Summary
This paper is a systematic review of scale-development practices in human and social sciences, aiming to (a) catalog current practices, (b) assess the limitations authors themselves report, and (c) offer recommendations for best practice. Papers were retrieved in September 2015 from Scopus, PsycINFO, and Web of Science using the search terms "scale development" and "limitations," with no time restriction; four reviewers screened blindly. The final corpus was 105 studies published 1976-2015 (88.5% from 2005-2015). Practices were analyzed across three standard steps: item generation, theoretical analysis, and psychometric analysis. The review identified ten recurring limitation types, the most common being sample-characteristic limitations (81% of studies), methodological limitations (33.2%), and psychometric limitations (30.4%). A key finding is that many studies showed methodological weaknesses (e.g., undersized psychometric samples) but few authors explicitly recognized or recorded them. The authors conclude with recommendations to help future researchers make better-informed methodological choices.

## Key facts it relies on
- 105 studies published between 1976 and 2015 were evaluated; 88.5% were published 2005-2015, and only two predate 2000 ("the last century").
- Item generation: 35.2% (n=37) used only deductive methods, 7.6% (n=8) only inductive, and 56.2% (n=59) combined both; literature review was the most common deductive method (84.7%, n=89), and interviews the most common inductive method (26.6%, n=28).
- Theoretical analysis: 74.2% (n=78) used expert opinions, 43.8% (n=46) used target-population opinions, and 63.8% (n=67) used only one of these two approaches.
- Psychometric analysis: EFA was used by 88.6% (n=93), CFA by 72.3% (n=76), convergent validity 72.3% (n=76), discriminant validity 56.2% (n=59); 65.7% (n=69) combined EFA and CFA; only 4.7% (n=5) used no factor analysis. Internal consistency was used by all studies; test-retest was second most common (22.8%, n=24).
- The ten limitation types and their prevalence: sample-characteristic (81%), methodological (33.2%), psychometric (30.4%), qualitative-research (5.6%), missing data (2.8%), social desirability bias (1.9%), item limitations (1.9%), brevity of scale (1.9%), difficulty controlling variables (0.9%), and lack of manual instructions (0.9%).
- 50.4% (n=53) of studies used sample sizes smaller than the rule-of-thumb minimum of 10 participants per item (10:1); 49.6% (n=52) lost more than 50% of their initial item pool during validation.
- The paper invokes the rule of thumb of at least 10 participants per item (10:1), with an "ideal" of 15:1 or 20:1 (Clark and Watson 1995; DeVellis 2003; Hair Junior et al. 2009).
- Minimum acceptable Cronbach's alpha is cited as at least 0.7, with 0.8-0.9 considered ideal (DeVellis 2003); two studies (Negra and Mzoughi 2012; Tombaugh et al. 2011) flagged brevity, both ending with only five items.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The corpus is selection-biased by design: inclusion required that articles explicitly declared their limitations, so the review describes self-reported limitations rather than all latent methodological flaws; the authors note many studies had weaknesses but only few recognized/recorded them.
- Scope restrictions: only self-administered measurement scales for humans were included; studies only assessing reliability/validity/revisions of existing scales, instrument translations to other languages, unvalidated scales, and non-English/Portuguese/Spanish papers were excluded.
- Search was a single time point (September 1, 2015) across three databases with English-only search terms, which can miss relevant non-English-indexed work.
- The authors concede some limitations are intrinsic and not fully remediable (e.g., subjectivity/researcher bias in qualitative work "will remain a common problem in any qualitative study"), and that detailed treatment of missing-data statistics and social-desirability control methods is beyond the paper's scope.

## Key topics covered
Scale development; psychometrics; item generation (deductive/inductive methods); content validity / theoretical analysis (expert and target-population judges); construct validity; exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis (EFA/CFA); convergent and discriminant validity; reliability (internal consistency, test-retest, Cronbach's alpha); sample size rules of thumb (10:1, 15:1, 20:1); missing data; social desirability bias; reverse-scored items; scale brevity; systematic literature review methodology.
