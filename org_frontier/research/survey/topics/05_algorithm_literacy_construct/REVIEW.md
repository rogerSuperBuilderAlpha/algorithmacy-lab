# Conceptualizing Algorithmic Literacy

Algorithmic literacy emerged as a distinct construct from a recognition that opaque, "black-boxed" recommender and decision systems mediate everyday access to information, yet most users hold only partial, experience-based understandings of them (Klawitter & Hargittai, 2018; Swart, 2021). Early empirical work framed the construct primarily through awareness — whether users recognize that an algorithm is operating — and treated awareness as a stratifying resource, with Gran, Booth and Bucher (2021) arguing that uneven algorithm awareness constitutes a new digital divide. Qualitative studies established that users develop "folk theories" of algorithmic operation that guide engagement even absent technical knowledge (Dogruel, 2021; Swart, 2021).

A more dimensional account consolidated around the definition advanced by Dogruel, Facciorusso and Stark (2022): being aware of algorithm use across applications, knowing how algorithms work, being able to critically evaluate algorithmic decision-making, and having skills to cope with or influence algorithmic operations. Their validated scale operationalized two of these facets — awareness and knowledge — through 11-item subscales, leaving critical evaluation and coping comparatively under-measured. Parallel instrument development by Zarouali, Helberger and de Vreese (2021) decomposed algorithmic media-content awareness into content filtering, automated decision-making, human–algorithm interplay, and ethical considerations, validated across multiple platforms.

Synthesizing reviews map the construct's structure and its gaps. Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2025) catalogue definitions, measures, and theorizing, noting heavy concentration on the cognitive facet and a comparative neglect of affective and behavioral dimensions; they call for balancing user literacy against developer transparency and for addressing the emerging algorithmic divide. Gagrčin, Naab and Grub (2024) review 169 studies, reframe literacy acquisition through experiential learning cycles, and prioritize defining core competencies, standardizing measures, and integrating subjective with factual assessment. A business-systems treatment positions algorithmic literacy as a navigational compass spanning cognitive, affective, and behavioral components (Schmitt, 2025).

The construct increasingly intersects with adjacent literacies. Systematic reviews of AI literacy converge on overlapping constructs — recognize, know and understand, use and apply, evaluate, create, and navigate ethically (Almatrafi, Johri & Lee, 2024) — and health-domain syntheses situate algorithmic literacy within functional, critical, and communicative tiers of digital health literacy, observing that communicative competencies remain rarely assessed (Iyamu et al., 2026). Across this literature the recurring tension concerns whether self-report subjective measures adequately capture the critical-evaluation and coping facets that distinguish literacy from mere awareness, and whether task- and domain-specific instruments are needed.
