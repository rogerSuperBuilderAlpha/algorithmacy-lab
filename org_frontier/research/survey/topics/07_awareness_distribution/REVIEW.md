# Social Distribution and Knowledge Gaps

Survey research treats awareness of platform algorithms as a socially distributed resource that maps onto existing inequalities. The foundational claim is that algorithm awareness functions as a "new digital divide": a population survey of Norway found clear demographic gradients and an awareness typology spanning the unaware, uncertain, affirmative, neutral, sceptic, and critical (Gran et al., 2020). United States survey data tied algorithmic knowledge to socioeconomic advantage, framing algorithms as "experience technologies" understood through use that is itself unequally distributed (Cotter & Reisdorf, 2020). Validated instruments distinguishing awareness from knowledge let later work measure these gaps comparably (Dogruel et al., 2021).

The recurring demographic signature is robust. Age, education, and frequency of platform use predict awareness, and a Dutch survey located misconceptions disproportionately among older, lower-educated, and female respondents, alongside the information sources that mitigate them (Zarouali et al., 2021). Slovenian survey data integrated awareness and knowledge into a three-level digital-inequality model, where access shapes skills, skills shape awareness and knowledge, and the latter shape tangible online outcomes, moderated by age, education, and income (Petrovčič et al., 2024). A Czech study reading personalization knowledge across social classes reached parallel conclusions about disparities and targeted education (Moravec et al., 2025). An integrative review consolidates these endogenous, exogenous, and personal predictors (Gagrčin et al., 2024).

Cross-national comparison sharpens the picture. A two-country survey showed the same predictors of awareness operating in the United States and Germany, while overall levels differed, attributable largely to higher U.S. platform usage (Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2023). A four-country study (United States, United Kingdom, South Korea, Mexico) documented an algorithmic knowledge gap both within and between nations, with the United States highest and South Korea lowest (Chung & Wihbey, 2024). Persuasion-knowledge segmentation of advertising audiences likewise split consumers by awareness and coping ability along demographic lines (Voorveld et al., 2023).

The awareness-behavior link is less straightforward than divide framing implies. Higher algorithmic knowledge predicted corrective and restrictive responses to misinformation across four countries, though the strength varied by national context (Chung, 2023). The same knowledge gap carried protective behavioral consequences, since more knowledgeable individuals acted against misinformation (Chung & Wihbey, 2024). Yet a survey of U.S. young adults found that greater awareness coincided with disengagement, with knowledgeable users less likely to correct falsehoods or engage opposing views, possibly reflecting limited algorithmic agency (Chung, 2025). Knowing how algorithms work, this evidence suggests, does not reliably translate into the civic behavior literacy interventions assume.
