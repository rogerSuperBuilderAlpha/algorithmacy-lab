# Panel and Latent Growth Methods for Skill Acquisition

Separating learning trajectories from stable individual differences across waves is the central modeling problem in skill-acquisition research, and two related structural-equation traditions address it. The latent growth curve model (LGCM) decomposes repeated performance measures into a between-person intercept (initial standing) and slope (rate of acquisition), with their variances indexing inter-individual differences in intra-individual change (McArdle, 2009). Applied directly to skill data, this framework reframed the long-disputed ability–acquisition relationship: reanalyzing air-traffic-controller practice data, latent slope factors revealed associations between cognitive ability and learning rate that between-subject correlations had obscured (Voelkle et al., 2006). Comparable LGCM treatments of cognitive-training curves separate baseline performance from learning rate and test individual-difference predictors of each, with baseline ability emerging as the most consistent correlate of gain (Bürki et al., 2014). In medical education, growth slopes from progress tests predict later licensing performance, supplying validity evidence that intercept and slope are distinct, meaningful components of skill development (Karay & Schauber, 2018).

The cross-lagged tradition addresses a different question: reciprocal influence between an evolving skill and a co-developing construct. The standard cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) conflates within-person dynamics with stable trait-like between-person differences, so its lagged coefficients can misstate the presence, sign, or direction of effects when constructs carry time-invariant stability (Hamaker et al., 2015). The random-intercept CLPM (RI-CLPM) adds person-level random intercepts to isolate within-person carry-over and cross-lagged paths, and extensions accommodate person-level covariates, multiple groups, and multiple indicators (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). A closely related alternative, the latent curve model with structured residuals (LCM-SR), embeds autoregressive and cross-lagged structure in the residuals of a growth model, separating between-person trajectory from within-person fluctuation (Curran et al., 2014). Applied to the reciprocal-effects model of self-concept and achievement, the RI-CLPM attenuates the inflated reciprocal paths that the CLPM reports, shifting support toward a skill-development pathway (Burns et al., 2020).

The choice among these models remains contested. Several authors argue the CLPM almost never targets the relevant estimand once trait-like stability is plausible (Lucas, 2023), while others caution that RI-CLPM and CLPM answer conceptually distinct between- versus within-person questions and that model selection should follow the research question, timescale, and design (Orth et al., 2020; Hamaker, 2023). Direct comparisons on the same data show that the magnitude, and sometimes the direction, of cross-lag estimates is model-dependent (Grimm et al., 2021). For skill acquisition specifically, this implies that claims about whether practice drives a correlate, or a stable aptitude drives both, hinge on whether between-person standing is partitioned from within-person change.
