# Interpersonal Coordination via CRQA

Cross-recurrence quantification analysis (CRQA) characterizes the shared dynamics of two behavioral time series by reconstructing their joint trajectory in phase space and quantifying recurrent states along and around the line of synchrony. The foundational application showed that two people unintentionally share more postural phase-space states while solving a puzzle together than when addressing different partners, establishing posture as a measurable cross-person coordinative structure (Shockley, Santana, & Fowler, 2003). A theoretical review extended this to the claim that conversing dyads form temporarily coupled neuromuscular units, with gaze and shared common ground modulating the strength of postural coupling (Shockley, Richardson, & Dale, 2009).

Methodological consolidation has driven the program. An R package made categorical and continuous CRQA broadly accessible (Coco & Dale, 2014), and a tutorial systematized CRQA, diagonal cross-recurrence profiles for leader–follower asymmetry, and multidimensional RQA for multivariate signals (Wallot & Leonardi, 2018). These tools support analysis of recurrence rate, determinism, and diagonal-line structure as indices of coupling strength and stability.

Empirical work spans conversational, gestural, and joint-action settings. Multimodal behavior matching in dialogue is temporally synchronized rather than merely co-occurrent (Louwerse, Dale, Bard, & Jeuniaux, 2012). Conversational dyads behave as interpersonal synergies whose coordinated variability predicts collective task performance (Fusaroli & Tylén, 2015), and movement coordination drops during argument relative to affiliative talk (Paxton & Dale, 2013). CRQA also captures coupling in collaborative music improvisation, where interaction stabilizes joint timing (Walton et al., 2017), and categorical CRQA indexes caregiver–infant communicative dynamics during the Strange Situation (Lira-Palma et al., 2018).

Recent studies (2023–2026) push the program toward naturalistic and applied contexts. Postural CRQA tracks how conversational coordination degrades under background noise, linking coupling to (mis)communication (Miles et al., 2023). Postural and gestural synchronization measures predict observers' perceived coupling of dancing dyads (Hartmann, Carlson, Mavrolampados, Burger, & Toiviainen, 2023). Nonverbal coordination dynamics distinguish casual chat from conflict (Bente, Schmälzle, Jahn, & Reimers, 2025), and CRQA extends to face-to-face collaboration through gaze, pointing, and verbal reference (Haraped et al., 2025). The method has migrated to hyperscanning, where inter-brain CRQA on fNIRS data shows that balance, delay, and entropy of neural coupling predict negotiation outcomes (Goldstein, Burns, Peck, Dale, & Lieberman, 2026). Open challenges include standardized reporting, theory-driven index interpretation, and extension beyond dyads.
