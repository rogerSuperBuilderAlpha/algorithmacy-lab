## Theories of mind and coordination

The program inherits a long contest over where cognition lives and how minds model one another. Symbolic computationalism framed intelligence as rule-governed manipulation of representations (Newell and Simon, 1976). Ecological and embodied responses relocated cognition into agent-environment coupling and enacted activity (Varela, Thompson, and Rosch, 1991), and the extended-mind thesis pushed the boundary of the cognitive system into the world, treating external resources as constituents of thought (Clark and Chalmers, 1998). Distributed-cognition fieldwork showed coordination achieved across people, instruments, and procedures rather than inside any single head. Predictive processing supplies a unifying mechanism: brains as hierarchical prediction engines minimizing error against generative models (Clark, 2013; Friston, 2010; Hohwy, 2020). Theory of mind names the capacity to attribute mental states (Premack and Woodruff, 1978), and shared intentionality grounds human coordination in jointly held goals (Tomasello et al., 2005). These commitments matter for a lab studying coordination through opaque third parties because they specify what a person must model to act with, or through, a system whose internal states are hidden.

## Modeling an opaque, interested third party

A second literature documents how ordinary users reason about algorithmic systems they cannot inspect. Bucher (2017) named the algorithmic imaginary, the affect-laden ways people picture what algorithms are and do, and showed that these pictures feed back into behavior. Eslami et al. (2015) found that most Facebook users were unaware of feed curation and reacted with surprise and anger on discovery. Folk-theory research catalogues the lay models users build: structural and abstract theories of feed operation (DeVito, 2017), and digital irritation crystallizing into theories of algorithms as confining, reductive, and exploitative (Ytre-Arne and Moe, 2021). Awareness is unevenly distributed and tracks a reinforced digital divide (Gran, Booth, and Bucher, 2021), with literacy emerging through situated experience rather than instruction (Swart, 2021; Gagrčin, Naab, and Grub, 2024; Ridley and Pawlick-Potts, 2022). Influencers and creators treat the system as a strategic adversary, playing a visibility game against rules inferred from outcomes (Cotter, 2019). Anthropological work cautions that algorithms are enacted cultural objects, not stable mechanisms (Seaver, 2018), and that transparency can backfire by producing an illusion of understanding (Ostinelli, Bonezzi, and Lisjak, 2024). Folk theories also extend to the labor and data that feed such systems (Fink and Goldkind, 2025). Vignette and scenario methods help surface these otherwise inarticulate models (Das et al., 2024).

## Communicating with and through machines

When a machine is itself a communicator or a mediator, traditional source models strain. The media-equation and computers-as-social-actors programs established that people apply social rules to machines automatically (Nass, Steuer, and Tauber, 1994; Reeves and Nass, 1996). Human-machine communication formalizes machines as communicative subjects and asks how people draw ontological boundaries between humans and computers (Guzman, 2020; Guzman and Lewis, 2020). AI-mediated communication studies the case where an agent modifies, augments, or generates messages on a communicator's behalf (Hancock, Naaman, and Levy, 2020). Empirical findings include a trust penalty when text is believed to be AI-written (Jakesch et al., 2019), shifts in language and interpersonal perception from suggested replies (Mieczkowski et al., 2021), and measurable effects on relationships and cooperation (Hohenstein et al., 2023). Large language models now pass several theory-of-mind tasks while failing others, raising the question of how people model machine minds (Strachan et al., 2024). Integrated information theory and its Phi measure, the lab's analytic instrument, supply a quantitative account of when a system forms an integrated whole (Tononi et al., 2016).
