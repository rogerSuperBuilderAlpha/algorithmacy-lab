# Strategic Visibility Games

Strategic visibility games describe a recurring structure on algorithmically curated platforms: creators cannot observe an interested third party's ranking rules, so they infer those rules from partial feedback and act on the inferences, while the platform retains authority over both the rules and their interpretation. Cotter (2019) names this the "visibility game," showing that Instagram influencers treat algorithmic outcomes as encoded "rules" and mechanize their conduct around contested interpretations of authenticity and reach. The inferential substrate is folk theorization: intuitive causal models users build from everyday use, media, and peer talk to decide how to behave under opacity (DeVito et al., 2018). Because platforms change, theorization is recursive, and DeVito (2021) frames adaptive re-theorization as a route to "algorithmic literacy," modulated by a creator's read of "platform spirit."

Inference is social rather than solitary. Bishop (2019) documents "algorithmic gossip"—communally circulated, directive theories among beauty vloggers that stabilize practices such as upload cadence and genre conformity. Marginalized creators extend this into "collaborative algorithm investigation," jointly testing and proving suppression they cannot otherwise observe (Delmonaco et al., 2024), and folk theories of identity-based suppression shape both content and resistance (Karizat et al., 2021). Visibility itself becomes a trap, where actionable theories guide approach and demotivational theories flag risks too costly to spring (DeVito, 2022).

The coordination problem is asymmetric. Platforms govern visibility unevenly through formal and informal means, disadvantaging stigmatized creators (Duffy & Meisner, 2023). Shadowbanning sharpens the asymmetry: a possibly nonexistent mechanism propagated through "algorithmic folklore" (Savolainen, 2022), against which platforms deploy "black box gaslighting" to discredit creators' situated knowledge (Cotter, 2021). Petre et al. (2019) show that the boundary between legitimate strategy and illegitimate "gaming" tracks platform interests, a dynamic they term platform paternalism.

Formal models recast these dynamics as games with an interested leader. Hron et al. (2022) model an "exposure game" in which algorithmic design choices determine the existence and character of creator-strategy equilibria, supporting pre-deployment audits of incentive misalignment. Khadka (2025) casts the algorithm as a Stackelberg leader setting exposure and reward rules that creators best-respond to. Fedorova et al. (2025) extend the frame to collective user action, deriving conditions under which coordinated interaction shifts recommendations and welfare. A through-line for an integrated-information program: visibility games couple opaque, interested algorithmic mediation with distributed inference and coordination, where the rules, the feedback, and the authority to interpret them are themselves stakes.
