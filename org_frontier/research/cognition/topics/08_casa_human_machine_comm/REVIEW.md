# CASA and Human-Machine Communication

The Computers Are Social Actors (CASA) paradigm originates in experimental demonstrations that people apply human social rules (politeness, reciprocity, gender stereotyping) to computers despite knowing the machines lack feelings or selves (Nass et al., 1994). The framing held that social responses arise from mindless application of overlearned scripts triggered by minimal social cues, with the machine occupying an ambiguous status as both tool and apparent interlocutor. Three decades on, the paradigm faces empirical and conceptual strain. A direct replication finds that participants no longer treat desktop computers as social agents, suggesting CASA effects attach to emergent technologies and fade for familiar ones (Heyselaar, 2023). Theoretical revisions accommodate such drift: a stronger CASA proposes that people develop and apply human-media scripts distinct from human-human ones (Gambino et al., 2020), while the Media Are Social Actors extension distinguishes primary from secondary cues and pairs mindless with mindful anthropomorphism (Lombard & Xu, 2021). Multidimensional-scaling evidence continues to favor mindlessness as the dominant mechanism for social presence and trust (Xu et al., 2022).

Human-Machine Communication (HMC) reframes the question by treating the machine as a communicative subject, a message source rather than a channel (Guzman & Lewis, 2019). Its agenda foregrounds functional, relational, and metaphysical dimensions, the last concerning blurred ontological boundaries among human, machine, and communication. Interview work shows people distinguish humans from computers along origin of being, autonomy, tool status, intelligence, emotion, and flaws, boundaries that erode as systems emulate human qualities (Guzman, 2020). The field's self-definition has moved toward a trans-ontological framing in which communicative presence and legitimacy are constituted relationally, captured by the Act–Mean–Relate paradigm and a view of design as a third party in communicative triads (Edwards & Etzrodt, 2025).

Bridging work imports interpersonal and computer-mediated theory into HMC, arguing that CASA licenses such transfer while machines occupy positions ranging across Buber's I-It and I-Thou (Westerman et al., 2020). Agent-agnostic models reject the exclusive assignment of sender and receiver roles to humans, evaluating participants by agency, interactivity, and influence (Banks & de Graaf, 2020). Domain studies extend these moves: automated journalism repositions software as communicator rather than mediator (Lewis et al., 2019), and disaster human-AI teaming locates communication in the scripts annotators invoke when training and correcting models (Stephens et al., 2023). Editorial syntheses connect CASA's old-brains formulation to hermeneutic and semiotic readings of machines as narrative and symbolic others (Fortunati et al., 2021).
