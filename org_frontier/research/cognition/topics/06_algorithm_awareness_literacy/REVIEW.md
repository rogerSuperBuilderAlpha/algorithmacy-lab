# Algorithm Awareness and Literacy

Algorithm awareness names users' recognition that opaque systems filter what they see, while algorithm literacy adds knowledge, attitudes, and behavioral competence (Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2021). Foundational qualitative work established that users build "folk theories" and an "algorithmic imaginary" to reason about hidden curation, often after a seam or expectancy violation reveals the algorithm's presence (Eslami et al., 2016; Bucher, 2017). These intuitive accounts shape behavior even when users cannot articulate them.

Population-level surveys document an uneven distribution amounting to a candidate digital divide. Mapping Norwegian users into six awareness types, from the unaware to the critical, Gran, Booth, and Bucher (2021) tied awareness to demographics and to attitudes toward recommendations. Cross-national replication finds the same predictors—age, education, social-media frequency—operating in both the United States and Germany, with higher overall usage lifting U.S. awareness (Oeldorf-Hirsch & Neubaum, 2023). Zarouali et al. (2021) show misconceptions concentrate among older, lower-educated, and women respondents, and that having no informational source compounds them. Comparative survey work across four countries confirms an algorithmic knowledge gap within and between nations, with more knowledgeable individuals more inclined to act against misinformation (Chung et al., 2024). A validated two-dimensional scale separating awareness from knowledge supports such measurement (Dogruel et al., 2021), and a model disentangling the two locates each within wider digital inequalities (Reisdorf et al., 2024).

Experiential learning, accumulated through everyday platform encounters, drives literacy more than formal instruction. An integrative review frames acquisition as experiential learning cycles (Gagrcin et al., 2024), and interviews with young people show that intuitive, experience-based insight into news personalization frequently outpaces the vocabulary to express it and does not reliably translate into intervention (Swart, 2021). Gruber and Hargittai (2023) position algorithm skills as a distinct internet competence shaping informed use.

Awareness does not guarantee benign outcomes, and transparency interventions can backfire. Greater algorithmic knowledge among young adults correlates with less correction of misinformation and less engagement with opposing views, a knowing-yet-disengaged pattern (Chung, 2025). Experimentally, the mere prospect of an explanation fosters an illusory sense of understanding and unfounded reliance on algorithmic outputs, even when the explanation goes unread (Ostinelli et al., 2024). These findings caution that disclosure and literacy interventions interact with motivation and agency in ways that can dampen, or even invert, their intended civic effects.
