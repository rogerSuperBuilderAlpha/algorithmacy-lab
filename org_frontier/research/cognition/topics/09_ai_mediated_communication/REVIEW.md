# AI-Mediated Communication

AI-mediated communication (AI-MC) names interpersonal communication in which an intelligent system modifies, augments, or generates messages on a communicator's behalf (Hancock, Naaman & Levy, 2020). The foundational concern is that delegating expression to an opaque algorithmic third party reshapes how senders are perceived. Early work isolated a perception penalty: Airbnb hosts whose profiles were thought to be AI-written were trusted less, though the penalty surfaced mainly when human- and AI-written profiles were mixed, a pattern termed the Replicant Effect (Jakesch, French, Ma, Hancock & Naaman, 2019).

The effects on language and relationships are double-edged. Algorithmic "smart replies" speed communication, raise positive-emotion language, and lead partners to evaluate each other as closer and more cooperative, yet senders suspected of using them are judged more negatively (Hohenstein, Kizilcec, DiFranzo, Aghajari et al., 2023). Suggested replies carry a positivity bias and can erode social attraction while restructuring conversational turns (Mieczkowski, Hancock, Naaman, Jung & Hohenstein, 2021). When interactions fail, the AI absorbs blame as a "moral crumple zone," lessening responsibility assigned to the human partner (Hohenstein & Jung, 2020).

A consistent finding is that disclosure of AI involvement, and the interpersonal stakes of the message, govern the response. Trust in email writers fell when AI was disclosed, though it rose for interpersonal rather than transactional emails (Liu, Mittal, Yang & Bruckman, 2022). In e-commerce, AI-assisted and AI-authored service messages lowered perceived benevolence and integrity relative to employee-written ones (Hennighausen, Yarza Navarro-Schär & Eller, 2025). Across seven experiments, emotional marketing copy believed AI-authored reduced positive word of mouth and loyalty through diminished authenticity and moral disgust, an effect that vanished for factual messages or when AI only edited (Kirk & Givi, 2025). Disclosing AI authorship generally erodes trustworthiness, caring, and competence, most steeply for social writing, though AI literacy buffers these declines (Nakano, Takezawa, Matulic, Yang & Yatani, 2026). Professionals nonetheless rate AI-assisted writing as professional and effective while marking sincerity slightly lower under disclosure (Coman & Cardon, 2024).

The authenticity cost concentrates in emotionally loaded, identity-relevant messages. Heavier drafting reduces perceived ownership and authenticity, and competence gains rarely compensate, while a sender-voiced co-sign can restore authenticity for tone rewrites (Fan et al., 2026). Senders themselves report guilt, "ghostwriting remorse," when delegating heartfelt messages to AI (Hass, Kirk & Givi, 2025). Counterevidence exists: in one-shot incentivized trust games, predictive-text assistance left trust largely intact while improving efficiency (Purcell, Jakesch, Dong & Nussberger, 2025). At the system level, AI-MC may depress epistemic trust online, posing a dilemma between warranted skepticism and unjust discrimination against suspected AI users (Sahebi & Formosa, 2025).
