# Theory of Mind and Shared Intentionality

Theory of mind (ToM) names the capacity to attribute unobservable mental states such as beliefs, desires, and intentions to agents in order to explain and predict behavior (Spaulding, 2019). The standard developmental marker is false-belief understanding; meta-analysis of 178 studies shows a robust shift from below-chance to above-chance performance across the preschool years, consistent with genuine conceptual change instead of task artifact (Wellman et al., 2001). Rakoczy (2022) synthesizes the cognitive, linguistic, social, and neural foundations of this trajectory and frames the contested distinction between implicit and explicit ToM. Two classic accounts of the underlying machinery persist: theory-theory, treating mental-state attribution as folk-psychological inference, and simulation theory, treating it as modeling another mind with one's own; interaction-theoretic and pluralist views argue that much everyday social understanding proceeds through embodied, directly perceived, or context-sensitive routes (Gallagher & Varga, 2015). A deflationary current proposes that script-based, non-mentalistic event schemata explain apparent ToM in preverbal infants and great apes without metarepresentation (Taylor et al., 2023).

Shared intentionality designates the species-typical skills and motivations to share goals, attention, and intentions during joint activity (Tomasello et al., 2006). On this account, gaze following becomes joint attention, group activity becomes collaboration, and social learning becomes instructed learning, distinguishing human children from other great apes. Tomasello (2018) extends the framework to belief understanding itself, arguing that the social and mental coordination of conflicting perspectives, expressed through joint attention and language, drives the construction of a false-belief concept and reconciles infant, ape, and preschooler findings. O'Madagain and Tomasello (2021) trace how escalating levels of these coordination skills, culminating in coordinating on reasons, underwrite cumulative cultural evolution.

Joint action supplies the mechanistic complement: co-actors form shared task representations, predict one another's contributions, and monitor joint outcomes through dense perceptual coupling (Sebanz et al., 2006). Recent work foregrounds debate over minimal forms of shared intentionality and whether dominant theories overstate the cognitive demands of acting together (Crone et al., 2025).

These foundations now set the baseline for modeling machine minds. Large language models pass many false-belief and higher-order ToM items, sometimes at adult levels (Strachan et al., 2024), and ToM-like behavior has been reported to emerge with scale (Kosinski, 2023). Trivial perturbations to such tasks collapse performance, indicating reliance on shallow heuristics (Ullman, 2023), and reviews caution that behavioral parity may produce an illusion of understanding absent the developmental and embodied mechanisms of human ToM (Marchetti et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025).
