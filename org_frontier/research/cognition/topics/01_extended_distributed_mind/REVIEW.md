# Extended and Distributed Cognition

The extended-mind thesis holds that cognitive processes can be constituted, and not merely caused, by resources distributed across brain, body, and environment, so that a person coupled to an external resource forms a single cognitive system (Clark & Chalmers, 1998). Hutchins' cockpit fieldwork supplied the complementary empirical program, showing that remembering and computation are achieved by socio-technical assemblies of people and artifacts whose representational state no single component carries (Hutchins, 1995). Together these frames license a question central to the algorithmacy program: when does an opaque algorithmic intermediary count as a constituent of a coupled cognitive system rather than an external object merely interacted with?

Membership has been tied to integration conditions. Clark and Chalmers' original glue-and-trust criteria (reliable availability, automatic endorsement, easy accessibility) were systematized by Heersmink into a multidimensional taxonomy of information flow, reliability, durability, trust, transparency, individualization, and transformation that grades how deeply an artifact is woven into a cognitive system (Heersmink, 2014). Sutton and colleagues recast the case for inclusion around complementarity, where external resources earn constituent status by contributing properties the brain lacks rather than by mimicking neural ones, and ground this in the psychology of socially distributed remembering (Sutton et al., 2010).

The internet and AI sharpen the application. Smart argued that internet-extended cognition is possible while flagging that diffuse, commercially mediated systems strain the classical criteria (Smart, 2017). Hernández-Orallo and Vold placed AI extenders on a continuum from loosely coupled tools to internalized processes, noting that learning, opacity, and interest-bearing operation distinguish them from notebooks (Hernández-Orallo & Vold, 2019). Recent work treats AI as a pre-cognitive substrate ("System 0") that shapes the informational ground of thought while satisfying reliability, trust, and individualization conditions yet risking sycophancy and bias capture (Chiriatti et al., 2025). Grumbach generalizes the picture to cognitive assemblages of humans, infrastructures, and algorithmic co-agents, where intermediation reconfigures autonomy and accountability (Grumbach, 2026).

Opacity and interestedness motivate qualifications. White and colleagues find that glue-and-trust and phenomenal transparency fit ambient, agenda-setting smart environments poorly, proposing a process-ontology account of shifting cognitive boundaries (White et al., 2025). Tomczyk grants that artifacts can partly constitute mental states while denying that the coupled human-plus-algorithm is itself an autonomous agent, locating agency in conscious human intentionality (Tomczyk, 2023). Extensive enactivists relocate the debate from constitution to interaction dynamics (Sganzerla et al., 2025), Farina and Lavazza argue the embedded/extended boundary matters more ethically than metaphysically (Farina & Lavazza, 2022), and Loock's extracted-mind counter-thesis warns that intermediaries may displace rather than extend competences (Loock, 2025).
