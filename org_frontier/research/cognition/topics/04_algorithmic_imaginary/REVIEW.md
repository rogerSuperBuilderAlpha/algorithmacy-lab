# The Algorithmic Imaginary

The concept of the algorithmic imaginary originates with Bucher's study of Facebook users, defined as "ways of thinking about what algorithms are, what they should be and how they function" (Bucher, 2017). The central claim is generative: affect-laden pictures of the algorithm are productive of moods and sensations and play a role in moulding the algorithm itself, since the conduct those feelings provoke becomes the data the system learns from. This positions the imaginary as a dynamic loop, where opacity and partial visibility (encounters with glitches, suppression, or "happy accidents") provoke speculation that then reshapes behavior (Stanusch, 2024).

A first lineage develops the user side through "folk theories," lay accounts of how opaque systems work. Early elicitation showed that disclosing algorithmic "seams" lets users rapidly form theories and make plans contingent on them (Eslami et al., 2016), and that rumored algorithmic change triggers expectation-violation backlash framed by those theories (DeVito et al., 2017). Survey and interview work catalogs recurring theories and locates the affective register, arguing that digital irritation rather than resignation tends to dominate (Ytre-Arne & Moe, 2021). The imaginary also acquires political weight where users theorize suppression: identity-based "strainer" theories on TikTok and shadowban folklore both show speculation hardening into accounts of governance and bias (Karizat et al., 2021; Savolainen, 2022).

The behavioral feedback that closes the loop is documented across creator and user populations. Instagram influencers treat algorithmic "rules" as a visibility game and mechanize their conduct accordingly, evidencing interdependency among users, algorithms, and platform owners (Cotter, 2018). On #BookTok, imaginings extend from critique to playful, anthropomorphizing strategies that shape cultural production (Low et al., 2023). Douyin users oscillate between resistance and compliance as their imaginaries evolve, so feedback registers as continuous negotiation rather than subversion (Lin, 2025).

A second lineage corrects the user-centric framing. Schulz (2023) draws on Castoriadis to argue that designers and machine-learning systems also imagine future user behavior, making the imaginary reciprocal across both sides of the interface. Platforms increasingly furnish the discourse that users draw on: official "algorithm explanation" material on Douyin shapes vernacular sensemaking, and platform messaging functions as an algorithmic pedagogy that constructs creators' imaginaries (Lin, 2025; Liang & Ye, 2025). The trajectory thus moves from a one-directional account of user affect toward a coupled system in which imaginaries, conduct, and platform self-presentation co-produce one another.
