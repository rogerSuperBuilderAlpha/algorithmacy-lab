## Summary
This conceptual philosophy paper asks where autonomous agency is located within a single-person extended cognitive system composed of one human and a cognitive artifact. Tomczyk grants the extended mind thesis (Clark and Chalmers 1998) — that mental states responsible for autonomous action can be partly constituted by cognitive artifacts — but argues that this does not commit one to an "extended agent thesis." Her method is to survey the main accounts of autonomous agency (internalist coherentist, externalist responsiveness-to-reasons and responsiveness-to-reasoning, relational/relationist), and the phenomenology of agency, then deploy both theoretical and practical arguments against treating the whole human-artifact system as an autonomous agent. The central claim is that autonomous agency is essentially tied to conscious experience (phenomenal and access consciousness) and intentionality, which on Chalmers' own argument can be attributed only to the human (biological) part of the system. Theoretically she rejects externalism about the self, treating the self as a non-spatial higher-level emergent property of a physical system; practically she points to the unacceptable consequences for moral and epistemic responsibility (e.g., the "Otto-plus-his-notebook" who commits a crime). She concludes against the extended agency thesis for one-person systems, noting that artificial systems reaching such complexity would instead constitute a more-than-one-subject (group-like) system requiring separate analysis.

## Key facts it relies on
- The extended mind thesis (Clark and Chalmers 1998) holds that in "continuous reciprocal causation" a human and an external artifact together constitute one cognitive system; the Otto example (Alzheimer's patient using a notebook for memory) and the "parity principle" (Clark, Chalmers 1998, 8) ground this.
- Clark and Chalmers impose four "glue and trust" conditions on extended dispositional belief: constancy of use, facility of access, trust, and prior endorsement (the information was consciously endorsed in the past).
- Sterelny (2010) adds an "entrenchment and personalization" condition; Clowes (2015) adds an "epistemic possession" condition (the artifact must be minimally cognitively penetrable, policable, and revisable) — yielding "six conditions" total in the author's framing.
- Causal Theory of Action (CTA): "behavior counts as action if and only if it is caused in the right kind of way by mental antecedents which constitute the agent's own reasons for the action" (Bishop 1997, 251); its key problem is deviant causal chains.
- Myles Brand's solution to deviant causal chains requires action to be proximately caused by the mental event of intending; the author uses this to argue that when an artifact (not human intending) is the proximate cause of behavior, there is no action and the human is not an agent.
- The MAIA project (Mental Augmentation through determination of Intended Action; Vanacker et al. 2007) on brain-computer-interface wheelchair control is cited as a real case where human-device interaction errors can produce deviant causal chains and shared control.
- Chalmers' (2019) argument: phenomenal consciousness requires information to be directly available for global control; extended/perceptual information must pass three stages (object to eye, eye to visual cortex, visual cortex to loci of control) while internal neuronal correlates traverse only part of the final stage — so extended consciousness is impossible.
- Two accounts of the experience of agency are distinguished: the mental causation thesis (Wegner 2002; Hohwy 2004) and the agent causation thesis (Chisholm 1976; Taylor 1966; Horgan, Tienson, Graham 2003); Bayne (2008) is cited for two functions of agentive experience (enabling free decision between alternatives; providing knowledge of one's own agency).
- The paper engages Farina and Lavazza (2022a, 2022b), who argue (via the "necklace with chemical sensor" / drinking example) that treating the artifact as part of the agent is morally preferable because a person devoid of the deeply integrated device is a different moral agent.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Scope is explicitly restricted: the author addresses only single-person, artifact-enhanced cognitive systems with unintentional (non-agent) artifacts; group systems and second-agent ("heteromesial"/prosthetic) deviance cases are set aside as topics "for another article."
- The argument is conceptual/philosophical, not empirical; it relies on thought experiments (Otto, Otto-committing-a-crime per Swallow 2013, Harbisson's "eyeborg") and acknowledges that such practical thought experiments "cannot constitute the argument" against extended agency — they only motivate the search for theoretical arguments, the real load being borne by the consciousness/intentionality claim.
- The paper notes the extended-consciousness counter-position (enactivists Noe 2004, O'Regan 2011; predictive processing Kirchhof, Kiverstein 2019) and concedes conscious experience causally depends on social/environmental factors, but treats the stronger constitutive claim as "much more radical" and "challenged as not well grounded" (citing Prinz 2009; Chalmers 2019), and that those analyses concern mainly perceptual (visual) consciousness rather than agency.
- The author acknowledges relationist/social-wave accounts (Menary, Cash, Meyers, Gallagher and Crisafi) that strongly socially constitute the self, but argues these still presuppose an individual who decides and is credited/blamed, and so do not support extended agency more strongly than Clark and Chalmers' original view.
- The final positive theory leans on contested commitments — non-reductive physicalism and the self as a non-spatial emergent property — and the author concedes that whether one can experience oneself as an "unmoved mover" undetermined by one's mental states "lies beyond the scope of this paper."
