## Summary
Premack and Woodruff ask whether the chimpanzee has a "theory of mind," defined as imputing unobservable mental states (purpose/intention, knowledge, belief, etc.) to oneself and others and using that system to predict behavior. Rather than testing problem-solving directly (as Kohler did), they showed an adult chimpanzee, Sarah, 30-second videotapes of a human actor struggling with problems (e.g., bananas out of reach, a locked cage, a malfunctioning heater, an unplugged phonograph) frozen on hold in the last five seconds, then offered photographs of which she had to choose the one constituting the solution (a stick, a key, a lit wick). Sarah chose the correct photograph consistently (e.g., 21 of 24 trials, p < .001, in the first test), with her errors concentrated on the block-laden-box problem that also defeated Kohler's apes. The authors argue this is best explained by Sarah imputing the actor's purpose and knowledge, and they weigh this "theory of mind" account against simpler alternatives (physical matching, associationism, empathy). The bulk of the paper is a program of further (largely proposed, not yet completed) experiments to distinguish these accounts and to test richer states such as guess vs. know, pretend vs. real, deception, and agent-specific belief; the commentary-style article spawned the modern theory-of-mind research program.

## Key facts it relies on
- Definition: an individual has a theory of mind if he imputes mental states to himself and others; it is a "theory" because such states are not directly observable and because the system can be used to predict the behavior of other organisms.
- Subject: Sarah, a fourteen-year-old African-born chimpanzee, received before age one, taught a simplified visual language between 4.6 and 6.5 years of age, and tested on cognitive tasks five days a week for the previous ten years; she had prior experience with commercial television.
- First test (inaccessible-banana problems): four 30-second videotapes of four problem types; the last five seconds were put on hold and Sarah chose between a pair of photographs (one solution, one not).
- First-test result: Sarah was correct on 21 of 24 trials (p < .001); all errors were confined to one problem (removing blocks from the block-laden box), the same problem that most defeated Kohler's apes; she was correct on the other three problems 6 of 6 times each (p = .013, binomial test).
- Three candidate explanations are weighed: (1) physical matching (ruled out by including every physical element in every videotape, per Figure 1), (2) associationism/generalization, and (3) theory of mind; plus an empathy alternative.
- Richer-problem test (locked cage, malfunctioning heater, unplugged phonograph, unattached hose): with gross alternatives Sarah made no errors; key/locked-actor and lit-wick/unlit-heater pairings have no physical match, so cannot be explained by matching.
- Refined-alternatives series (three versions of each of four cases, e.g., key intact/twisted/broken): Sarah made one error in twelve choices, attributed to photographic quality (photos reduced from 8"x10" to 3"x4").
- "Good" vs. "bad" actor test: with Keith (Sarah's favored trainer) she chose the "good" alternative eight of eight times, but with Bill (toward whom she displayed no affection) only two of eight (z = 2.58, p < .01), indicating her choices depend on who the actor is.
- The paper lays out proposed experimental programs for embedded videotape (observing a viewer observe), guess vs. know, pretend vs. real, deception/lying (longitudinal study of four sexually immature chimpanzees, in preparation as Woodruff & Premack), and self-knowledge.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors themselves stress this is a position/commentary paper: most of the proposed experiments "have been done" only "in principle," many tests "waiting in the rather long line of studies that remain to be done," so the theory-of-mind thesis is defended more in principle than in fact.
- They explicitly cannot rule out associationism/generalization for these results because the problem sequences lack well-defined structural descriptions (unlike language), leaving "just enough vagueness" for the generalization proposal.
- The empathy alternative is acknowledged as not radically different from theory of mind (both impute purpose); the "good/bad actor" result is offered as evidence against pure empathy, but the authors note the actor-identity effect could instead reflect preference for particular photographs and add controls.
- Findings rest on a single chimpanzee (Sarah) with extensive atypical training (language, television, daily cognitive testing for ~10 years), and the authors concede results may have limited generality and that the theory, if learned, would carry species-specific biases and errors.
- This 1978 BBS target article is the origin of the term "theory of mind" and launched decades of debate (the deception, false-belief, and guess/know programs it proposes were largely pursued by later researchers).
