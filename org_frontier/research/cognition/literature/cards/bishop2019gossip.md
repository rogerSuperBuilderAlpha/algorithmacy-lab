## Summary
Bishop asks how professional beauty vloggers on YouTube formulate and sustain expertise about the platform's opaque recommender algorithms, given that detailed information on how visibility is determined is unavailable to creators. Drawing on a three-year multi-platform ethnography and semi-structured interviews with ten British full-time beauty/lifestyle vloggers (plus nine industry intermediaries), she develops the concept of "algorithmic gossip": communally and socially informed theories and strategies about recommender algorithms, shared and implemented to engender financial consistency and visibility on algorithmically structured platforms. She argues gossip is productive — talk supports practices such as uploading frequently and producing feminised beauty content because creators believe these perform better algorithmically. The paper identifies three functions of algorithmic gossip: a collaborative resource for knowledge production (via the Internet Creators Guild changelog, closed Facebook groups, and intimate requests to fans); a subversive tool for marginalised creators (especially vloggers of colour) to expose perceived platform discrimination; and a method researchers can use as data about how algorithms work. Bishop positions this between two existing research camps — studies of "technical" sites and studies of everyday users — arguing platform producers (neither employees nor ordinary users) are a distinct, under-studied source of algorithmic knowledge.

## Key facts it relies on
- Algorithmic gossip is defined as "communally and socially informed theories and strategies pertaining to recommender algorithms, shared and implemented to engender financial consistency and visibility on algorithmically structured social media platforms."
- Method: three years of immersion in a "messy web" of vloggers' participation across social networks and offline spaces; combined semi-structured interviews, online ethnography across platforms, offline ethnography at vlogging events, and qualitative analysis of YouTube's promotional materials; online ethnography conducted 2015-2018.
- Sample: ten British beauty and lifestyle vloggers interviewed, plus nine vlogging industry intermediaries (including talent agents, Twitter's UK/Europe community manager, and the then-Director of the Internet Creators Guild); each interview lasted between thirty minutes and three hours.
- Three interviewees had previously vlogged full time but currently earned a part-time wage; one was an ex full-time vlogger who had ceased producing on YouTube.
- The ethnography followed upwards of 30 UK-based beauty vloggers with a minimum of 10,000 subscribers; 10,000 subscribers is the minimum requirement for YouTube creator services (e.g., YouTube Space Creator Café, Creator Days) and signals "A List" monetizable visibility.
- The Internet Creators Guild (ICG) is a non-union, non-profit founded in 2016 by vlogger Hank Green; membership ranges $75-$500 per year and includes a "Platform Changelog" of gathered, unconfirmed creator experiences.
- The paper draws on Cotter's (2018) "playing the visibility game," Bucher's (2017) "algorithmic imaginary," Eslami et al.'s (2016) "folk theories," Sandvig et al.'s (2014) "algorithmic auditing," Kitchin's (2017) "reverse engineering," and Nieborg & Poell's (2018) "platformization."
- Worked examples of gossip-as-data: Zoe London's tweet that "the algorithm rewards you for uploading 15 min of content a week"; Rachel Levin's 2017 tweet request for subscription-box screenshots returned 41 fan images used as evidence of unannounced changes to YouTube's subscriber inbox; Carly Rowena's Instagram poll crowdsourcing whether her video appeared in subscribers' "sub box."
- Subversive function: mixed-race vlogger Grace Victory's video "Does YouTube Discriminate?" argues algorithms reinforce white visibility (linked to Noble's Algorithms of Oppression, 2018, and a body of work on YouTube's tendency to promote white creators, e.g., Gaunt 2015; Oh & Oh 2017).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Bishop explicitly flags methodological limitations of gossip: it is closed, intimate, and hard to track, so the ethnographer's exposure is limited to snippets confided in interviews; vloggers' theories were often hedged with precursors like "I feel like" and "in my opinion."
- Gossip is "arguably not static or reproducible," due to the changeable nature of YouTube's algorithm, complicating its use as reproducible evidence.
- The paper acknowledges that creators (and academics) "cannot know for certain why videos are (or are not) widely promoted"; claims such as Grace Victory's racial-discrimination theory "cannot truly evidence" discrimination — they reflect experience and affect rather than proof.
- Bishop notes the social-justice potential of gossip is limited, since gossip (and creators who voice racism/inequality) risk being positioned as "irrational" against YouTube's "meritocratic framework" and self-presentation as a rational, objective actor.
- Closed gossip spaces reinforce boundaries between insiders and outsiders; "full time" status skews toward white actors with intersecting privilege, so the gossip networks studied are themselves exclusionary (Bishop 2018; Duffy 2017; Waddington 2012).
