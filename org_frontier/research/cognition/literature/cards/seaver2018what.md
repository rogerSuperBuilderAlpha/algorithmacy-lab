## Summary
This essay asks what an anthropology of algorithms should do, and argues that algorithms are not autonomous technical objects but complex sociotechnical systems—"culture," another name for collections of human choices and practices. Drawing on ethnographic fieldwork among music-recommendation engineers (the composite figure "Brad" on the radio team at "Whisper"), Seaver portrays algorithmic systems as fabrics of feedback loops "held together by a moment of human response," where every algorithmic decision conceals many human ones. He contrasts this with what Malte Ziewitz calls the "algorithmic drama," in which critics figure algorithms as powerful, inhuman, and obscure, casting anthropology as the human defender of the analog against the digital. Seaver names this trap the "analog slot"—after Trouillot's "savage slot"—a position nominally opposed to the digital but dependent on it for coherence, which grants computing definitional supremacy. He urges anthropologists to refuse the human-versus-algorithm dichotomy, find the people within these systems (including underpaid data and content-moderation workers), and supply much-needed empiricism and particularism to critical discourse. The ultimate contribution he proposes (after Alfred Gell on art) is the "corrosive potential of anthropological attention," which dissolves the apparent solidity and coherence of "the algorithm."

## Key facts it relies on
- The paper opens with Conway's Law, "first formulated... in the late 1960s," using Eric S. Raymond's (1996, 124) New Hacker's Dictionary formulation: "If you have four groups working on a compiler, you'll get a four-pass compiler."
- Facebook's open-office workspace, a Frank Gehry-designed hangar, "when it opened, held 2,800 people" (Frankel 2015).
- Ethnographic vignette: "Brad" sits with "the five other members of the radio team" building playlist-generating algorithms for "Whisper," a music recommendation company; he evaluates his "algorithmic DJ" by listening, then escalating to teammates, product manager, internal company feedback, and user tests.
- Central thesis: "algorithms are not autonomous technical objects, but complex sociotechnical systems"; reframing reverses supposed essential qualities—the idealized algorithm is "generic, stable, and rigid" while the actual algorithmic system is "particular, unstable, and malleable" (Seaver 2013).
- Case of programmer Jacky Alciné (2015), whose photo of himself and a friend (both African American) was auto-tagged by Google Photos as "gorillas"; a Google manager, Yonatan Zunger (2015), responded within "a few hours" and "by the next day, the objectionable tag was gone." Note 3 reports (via Simonite 2018 in Wired) that Google engineers "simply blocked the system from labeling most nonhuman primates."
- The "analog slot" is theorized as the anthropology of computing's version of Michel-Rolph Trouillot's (1991) "savage slot": "a position nominally opposed to the digital, but which depends on it for coherence."
- Key supporting quotes/sources: a former head of recommendation at Spotify said such systems are "human all the way down" (Heath 2015); Malte Ziewitz (2016, 3) names the "algorithmic drama"; Bryan Pfaffenberger (1988, 241) states the classic lesson that technology is "a set of social behaviors and a system of meanings."
- Closes by adapting Alfred Gell (1992, 41): "the ultimate aim of [the anthropology of algorithms] must be the dissolution of [algorithms]."

## Critical notes from the literature
- This is a short "Openings and Retrospectives" essay (pp. 375–385), not an empirical study report; the argument rests on a single composite ethnographic vignette and reinterpretation of public cases rather than systematic data.
- Seaver concedes the core claim "is, admittedly, not new"—anthropologists have long redescribed technical systems as sociotechnical (citing Pfaffenberger 1988)—so the contribution is reframing and warning against a disciplinary trap rather than a novel finding.
- Note 4 acknowledges that "methodological challenges face the would-be ethnographer of algorithmic systems," while noting prior tactics for studying elite and secretive groups (e.g., Gusterson 1997; Ortner 2010; Jones 2011; Mahmud 2014).
- The paper itself critiques much algorithm scholarship for granting algorithms "a homogeneity and coherence that is elusive in practice" and for treating engineers as the only people anthropologists are "comfortable treating as homo economicus."
- Scope: the human-centered framing is tempered by noting that "being human within the machine is not a pleasurable or powerful experience" for underpaid data workers and content moderators (Irani 2015; Roberts 2016; Ekbia and Nardi 2017; Gray and Suri 2017).
