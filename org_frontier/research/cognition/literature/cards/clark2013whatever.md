## Summary
This Behavioral and Brain Sciences target article critically reviews the "hierarchical prediction machine" view of the brain: the claim that brains are essentially prediction machines that support perception and action by continuously trying to match incoming sensory input against top-down predictions generated by a hierarchical, bidirectional generative model, with only the residual prediction error propagated forward. Clark synthesizes the lineage from Helmholtz's "perception as inference" through analysis-by-synthesis, the Helmholtz Machine, and Rao and Ballard's predictive-coding model of visual cortex, then traces Friston's extension of the framework to action ("action-oriented predictive processing," active inference) and to the free-energy minimization principle. Sections 1-2 lay out the elements (explaining away, the duplex error/representation architecture, precision-weighting as attention, the Bayesian brain); section 3 raises pitfalls (neural evidence, the "darkened room" scope worry, neats-vs-scruffies); sections 4-5 ask how the framework bears on experience (e.g., delusions in schizophrenia as misweighted prediction error) and on a unified science of mind. Clark concludes the approach offers "the best clue yet" to a unified science of mind and action and a partial answer to Marr's dream of spanning computation, algorithm, and implementation, but argues that explaining distinctively human cognition still requires a hybrid account that adds embodiment, neuroscientific detail, and socio-cultural scaffolding.

## Key facts it relies on
- The framework's core claim: the brain minimizes prediction error within a bidirectional cascade of cortical processing using a hierarchical generative model, where higher-level systems predict the inputs to lower-level ones and only the residual error (a proxy for sensory information, per Feldman and Friston 2010) flows forward (Abstract; sect. 1.1).
- Predictive coding originated as a data-compression strategy in signal processing, driven by James Flanagan and others at Bell Labs in the 1950s; descendants are used in JPEGs, lossless audio, and motion-compensated video coding; prediction error formally corresponds to "surprisal" (Tribus 1961), distinct from experiential surprise (sect. 1.1).
- Rao and Ballard (1999) implemented a bidirectional hierarchical network of "predictive estimators" trained on image patches from five natural scenes; after exposure to thousands of patches it learned first-level features (oriented edges and bars) and second-level combinations, and reproduced "extra-classical receptive field" effects; forward connections carry "residual errors" (p. 79) while backward connections do the "heavy lifting" carrying predictions (sects. 1.1-1.2).
- Hosoya et al. (2005) showed dynamic predictive coding in the retina: putting salamanders and rabbits in varying environments, within several seconds about 50% of retinal ganglion cells altered their behavior to track changing image statistics, modeled via anti-Hebbian feedforward "novelty filters" using amacrine-cell synapses (sect. 1.3).
- Hohwy et al.'s (2008) hierarchical predictive-coding model explains binocular rivalry as a bi-stable "energy landscape containing a double well" in which no single hypothesis combines high prior and high likelihood; rivalry persists because "the prior probability of both a house and face being co-localized in time and space is extremely small" and the system has learned that "only one object can exist in the same place at the same time" (p. 691) (sect. 1.4).
- Action-oriented predictive processing (Friston 2003; 2009; 2010; Brown et al. 2011) treats perception and action as computational siblings following the same logic; in "active inference" / "desert landscape" versions proprioceptive prediction errors act directly as motor commands ("action is both perceived and caused by its perception," Friston 2003, p. 1349) (sect. 1.5).
- The free-energy principle holds that "all the quantities that can change... will change to minimize free-energy" (Friston and Stephan 2007, p. 427); information-theoretic free energy upper-bounds surprisal, and entropy is the long-term average of surprisal, linking prediction-error minimization to resisting entropy and the second law of thermodynamics (sect. 1.6).
- The models posit a "duplex architecture" combining distinct "error units" (forward flow = error) and "representation units" (backward flow = predictions); high-level predictions "explain away" error and tell error units to "shut up" (Friston 2005, p. 829), reconciling the account with biased-competition / top-down enhancement views (sect. 2.1).
- Attention is recast as precision-weighting: altering the gain on error units to "control the relative influence of prior expectations at different levels" (Friston 2009, p. 299), with the neurotransmitter dopamine and the mesolimbic dopaminergic system proposed as a precision-encoding mechanism (Fletcher and Frith 2009, pp. 53-54) (sect. 2.3).
- Egner et al. (2010) found fusiform face area (FFA) activity reflected an interaction of stimulus feature and expectation, with face/house responses becoming indistinguishable under high face expectation, suggesting FFA responses are "determined by feature expectation and surprise rather than by stimulus features per se" (p. 16601) (sect. 3.1).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The "darkened room" / "Nirvana" scope worry (Mumford 1992, p. 247): if cortex aims to minimize prediction error, why don't animals just seek a dark room and stay still? Clark's response (via Friston 2010; Friston et al. 2009) is that organisms have prior expectations for exploration/play and are "embodied models" of their niche, but he concedes this is "good news and bad news" — the framework can accommodate any behavior precisely because it does not itself specify the species-specific priors that do the explanatory work (sect. 3.2).
- Clark grants the gap between very general prediction-error-minimization principles and the specific functional architecture of the human mind "remains daunting"; the account leaves open questions about neural representational formats (e.g., dorsal vs. ventral stream differences) and does not by itself specify a full cognitive architecture (sects. 3.2-3.3).
- Clark argues a full account of human cognition cannot "jump" directly from action-oriented predictive processing to distinctively human thought; it demands a "multiply hybrid" approach combining probabilistic-generative computation, neuroscientific conjecture, and socio-cultural / external-symbolic scaffolding, leaving the "distribution of explanatory weight" as the pressing open question (sect. 5.2).
- Whether the brain genuinely approximates Bayesian inference versus merely behaving "as if" it did is flagged as unresolved; Clark notes the duplex error/representation separation is "a demanding and potentially problematic requirement" that may or may not be realized in cortex (sects. 2.1, 5.2). The article appeared with a large set of peer commentaries and a separate Frontiers research topic (25 commentaries plus Clark's rejoinder), reflecting active debate.
