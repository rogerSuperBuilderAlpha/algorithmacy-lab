## Summary
This is a conceptual philosophy-of-cognition paper that asks what kind of cognition we have given how we interact with increasingly intelligent (AI-powered) tools. It introduces "extracted cognition" as a counter-hypothesis to Clark and Chalmers' "extended mind" (1998): whereas extended cognition holds that we are cognitively augmented by tools we causally control and epistemically exploit, extracted cognition holds that we tend to use tools that first capture, then mimic, and eventually replace ("displace") cognitive skills we previously executed internally, so the agent's cognitive involvement becomes redundant. The method is comparative argument analysis built around two paradigmatic cases — Otto with his notebook (the extended archetype) and his daughter Anna, a digital marketing director at MoMA using an AI-powered laptop (the extracted archetype). The paper first unpacks three externalist assumptions (cognitive worker, cognitive artifact, cognitive profits), then shows how a pocket calculator partially "deviates" from them and an AI assistant fully "subverts" them. It then reconstructs theoretical (constitutive), practical (teleological), and ethical (axiological) arguments for each hypothesis, adding a "stage four" with three novel criteria (decisional power, causal control, epistemic access) that distinguish personal integration (extended) from personal disintegration (extracted). The author frames extracted cognition as a purely descriptive theoretical framework — not a techno-pessimistic, empirical, or normative thesis — that names a cognitive phenomenon (collective "cognitive autocratization") earlier tools could not provoke.

## Key facts it relies on
- The core dichotomy: extended cognizers "seek external tools that still require the co-activation of relevant internal processes to solve a task," whereas extracted cognizers "are surrounded by tools that can, should, and do eventually solve the task without any of our internal contribution."
- Builds on Clark & Chalmers' "The Extended Mind" (Analysis, 58(1), 7–19, 1998); the Otto-notebook example is from pp. 12–18, and the parity principle and "active causal role" of all components from p. 8.
- Three externalist assumptions are named and dissected: (1) cognitive worker, (2) cognitive artifact (notion of "cognitive artifact" introduced by Norman, 1991), and (3) cognitive profits (personal vs. systemic view, cf. Norman 1991).
- The "cognitive economy" principle traces to Clark's "007-principle" (1989, p. 64), later the "hypothesis of cognitive impartiality" (Clark, 2007, p. 174); Rowlands calls it the "barking dog principle" (2010, pp. 15–16).
- The calculator is analyzed as a "substitutive cognitive artifact" (Fasoli, 2018) involving a "distribution of computation" (Heersmink, 2012, p. 53); it partially deviates from the three assumptions while AI fully subverts them.
- The four theoretical stages: (1) coupled system, (2) cognitive system (including portability/"Naked Mind" and parity-principle criteria from Clark & Chalmers 1998, pp. 10–12), (3) centralized system / "core of cognition," and (4) the decisive extended-vs-extracted stage with three criteria: decisional power, causal control, and epistemic access (opacity/transparency, cf. Andrada et al., 2023).
- Practical arguments introduce "cognitive outsourcing" and "cognitive insourcing"; tool practice "turbo-charges" (Clark, 2010b, p. 59) cognition for Otto but "turbo-discharges" it for Anna.
- The ethical argument uses "cognitive blackmailing" (cf. Cassinadri, 2022, p. 7): Otto's stolen notebook as a "severe cognitive infringement" (cf. Carter & Palermos, 2016, pp. 551–552), reversed for Anna whose excessive AI use leads her to the wrong location.
- Mühlhoff (2020, p. 1874) is quoted on machine learning as a "media-cultural constellation"; Clark (1997, p. 180) is quoted: "Our brains make the world smart so that we can be dumb in peace!"
- Funded by the German Research Foundation (DFG), Research Training Group "Situated Cognition," Projektnummer GRK 274877981; received 12 July 2024, accepted 11 February 2025, published online 13 March 2025.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper is explicitly descriptive and disclaims commitment: the author states extracted cognition is "not committed to" a techno-pessimistic, socio-psychological, or medico-pathological criticism (cf. Paglieri, 2024, for a techno-pessimistic stance), and that "any normative evaluation is not implied by this descriptive point."
- Anna's case is acknowledged to be partly speculative/extrapolated ("not science fiction, but consumer reality of tomorrow"); the long-run cognitive decline of individual capacities is conceded to be "an empirical possibility to be tested" (cf. Heinrichs, 2024), not an established result.
- The author concedes the cases do not cleanly separate the hypotheses: the first three theoretical stages "do not yet induce any pertinent divide between the Otto and the Anna case," and Anna "can, but need not" satisfy those criteria — only the added stage-four criteria adjudicate; intuitions can flip with variations such as a medical condition (e.g., an Alzheimer's Otto vs. healthy Otto, or a neurodegenerative Anna).
- The paper anticipates that externalists may argue AI tools "make no difference" to their thesis since tools merely simplify thinking while still becoming constitutive parts of extended systems; the author treats this as the strongest counter-critique to close the paper.
- Scope limit acknowledged: the discussion "exclusively relied on advanced technologies," and whether all skills can be extracted to "cognitive oblivion" is left open, positing a possible "un-extractable core of cognition."
