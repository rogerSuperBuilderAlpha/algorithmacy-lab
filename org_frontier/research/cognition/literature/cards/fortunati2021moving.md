## Summary
This is the editorial introduction to Volume 2 of *Human-Machine Communication*, in which Fortunati and Edwards frame 10 selected articles and advance their own theoretical commentary on the field of human-machine communication (HMC). They organize the volume into four "layers of discourse": (1) updates to theoretical frameworks and paradigms (the Media Are Social Actors / MASA paradigm by Lombard and Kun), (2) ontologizing and prototyping processes (Etzrodt & Engesser; Banks et al.), (3) a critical turn regarding gender (Liu) and ability/disability (Davis & Stanovsek; Dehnert & Leach), and (4) HMC in organizational contexts (Gibbs et al.; Johanssen & Wang; Piercy & Gist-Mackey; Prahl & Van Swol). Their central theoretical argument reframes the dominant CASA ("Computers Are Social Actors") explanation: rather than people treating media as human merely because their "old brains" cannot distinguish mediated from real (Reeves & Nass, 1996), the authors argue this should be reframed through historical, sociological, and semiotic/hermeneutic lenses, proposing instead that media may be read as narrative texts and interpreted as symbolic humans. They contend the term "natural" in CASA should be replaced with "cultural," and offer a list of distinctions that still separate present-day robots from humans. The piece is a programmatic agenda-setting essay rather than an empirical study, calling for more interdisciplinary, critical, and qualitative/mixed-method work and for research on situated practices of use.

## Key facts it relies on
- The volume comprises 10 articles the editors found "groundbreaking," organized into four layers of discourse: theoretical frameworks/paradigms (MASA), ontologizing/prototyping, a critical turn on gender and ability/disability, and HMC in organizational contexts.
- CASA (Computers Are Social Actors) is the influential paradigm proposed by Nass and colleagues in the 1990s (Nass et al., 1994, 1996, 1997); per CASA, people treat computers in some ways they treat humans by mindlessly applying human-human social rules.
- Lombard and Kun's article launches the Media Are Social Actors (MASA) paradigm, offering nine formal propositions and distinguishing mindless and mindful anthropomorphism as two complementary mechanisms, grounded in evolutionary psychology.
- The authors cite Reeves and Nass (1996) that "old brains" did not evolve to distinguish mediated representations from real, noting Homo Sapiens existed for nearly 200,000 years where anything acting socially "really was a person" (p. 12), and that "media are real first, and false only after we think about it" (p. 13).
- The authors invoke Jaquet-Droz's three automata-androids (the Writer, the Musician, the Draftsman) built in the second half of the 18th century, and note reading is a recent cognitive function invented only ~5,500 years ago (Dehaene, 2007; Wolf, 2007).
- The word "robot" derives from the Czech *robotnik* (slave) and was first used in 1921 by Karel Čapek in the play *R.U.R.*; the authors note human beings were the first computers and, symbolically, the first robots (Grier, *When Computers Were Human*, 2005; Hicks, 2018).
- The authors offer Eco's reading typology — "naive reading," "available reading," and "critical reading" — arguing users often apply a naive reading to media because the technologies are relatively new.
- They list distinctions between present-day robots and humans (e.g., a robot is not born from a mother, has no infancy/socialization, lacks self-consciousness (Faggin, 2021), feels no emotions, is a cultural object not a cultural subject, and per Bhattacharya (2020) has no goals "beyond carrying out a pre-assigned program").
- The ABOT (ABOVE) anthropomorphic robot database, cited as open-source, includes 251 different types of real robots and is described as "far from being exhaustive."
- Empirical findings summarized from volume articles: Banks et al. found mind ascription and moral evaluations did not differ by interaction form (social/task/play), but participants expressed more trust following task interactions ("schema-aligned task interactions and perceived playfulness are gateways for building trust in robots," p. 97); Prahl & Van Swol found human advisors rated more favorable (expert, useful, similar), an effect magnified when a human was replaced by a machine; Piercy & Gist-Mackey's results were largely supportive of the skills-biased technical change (SBTC) hypothesis.

## Critical notes from the literature
- This is an editorial/framing essay, not an empirical study; its theoretical claims (CASA reframed via semiotics, the robot/human distinction list) are argumentative and programmatic rather than tested, and the authors describe the reframing discourse as "only sketched" and needing further development and integration.
- The authors explicitly acknowledge "serious limitations" in exploring daily practices of use, especially of social robots, because such robots are still at the prototyping level and users have very limited direct experience of them.
- The authors flag two open empirical questions left unresolved: how long the shift from perceiving media as real to recognizing their true nature takes, and what happens after people realize machines are not humans.
- They acknowledge they cannot make a "precise and exhaustive analysis" of human-robot distinctions, noting that both the nature of robots and concepts of humanity shift over time.
- The editors call for greater interdisciplinarity (more philosophy and politics), more critical approaches challenging structures of privilege/oppression, and research extending HMC to transforming technologies like the automobile (e.g., self-driving and current cars).
