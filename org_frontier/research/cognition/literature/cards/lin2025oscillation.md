## Summary
This qualitative study asks how Douyin users employ folk theories to resist algorithmic curation, and why their resistance shifts ("oscillates") across different sociocultural contexts. Using a walk-through method plus a diary-interview technique with 31 young, urban Chinese participants (aged 21-30, mean age 26; 17 women, 14 men) who recorded their Douyin screens for a week followed by in-depth interviews (over 52 hours of screen recordings), the author tracks resistance to three types of algorithmic irritation: simplified categorization, commercial exploitation, and political agenda-setting. The central finding is a paradox: although respondents said they were algorithmically aware and annoyed by these irritations, they often behaved in ways that contradicted those claims, showing limited or no resistance. The paper theorizes resistance into three categories—dominant, negotiated, and oppositional—graded along three indicators: use of technological affordances (after Shaw's encoding/decoding view), investment (cognitive/time/money), and folk-theoretic understanding of algorithms. It argues this paradox reflects both users' efforts to reconcile sociocultural needs with digital irritations and a sense of "digital resignation" produced by the platform's strict regulation and censorship—notably reinforced by official educational videos from Douyin/ByteDance accounts and government news. The author concludes that folk theories function as resources for negotiation rather than a subversive force, leaving resistance largely confined to the dominant use of technological affordances and unable to disrupt the ideological power relations embedded in algorithm-driven platforms.

## Key facts it relies on
- Method: mixed-method design combining the walk-through method (Light et al., 2018) to analyze Douyin's affordances with a diary-interview technique using video diaries; participants recorded Douyin screens for a week, then did in-depth individual online interviews.
- Sample: 31 participants recruited in 2023 across China's five most popular social platforms (WeChat, Douyin, Weibo, QQ, Xiaohongshu); aged 21-30 (mean age = 26); 17 identifying as women and 14 as men; from urban areas including Beijing, Shanghai, Harbin, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Changsha, Wuhan, and Hohhot; pseudonyms assigned.
- Inclusion criteria: at least 18 years old with Douyin access; regular Douyin use; engagement with algorithm-recommended videos (watching, commenting, sharing, etc.). Data corpus exceeded 52 hours of screen recordings, analyzed via thematic analysis (Attride-Stirling, 2001).
- Theoretical framework (Table 1): three categories of resistance—dominant (compliant use of perceptible affordances, minimal cognitive/time/money investment), negotiated (adaptive/repurposed use of affordances, ambivalent emotions oscillating between irritation, annoyance, and resignation), and oppositional (consciously unexpected uses of hidden affordances, advanced understanding, relatively high investment). Grounded in Shaw's (2017) affordance reading of Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding.
- Three indicators differentiate the categories: (1) use of technological affordances, (2) investment (cognition, time, money—after Picone et al., 2019; Bonini & Treré, 2024), (3) users' folk-theoretic understanding/perceptions of algorithms.
- Resistance behaviors observed (Table 2) span: no resistance (unconsciously swiping away/ignoring disliked content), dominant resistance (deliberately swiping away, turning off privacy buttons, pressing "not interested"/"report"/"thumbed down," unfollowing, revising profiles), negotiated/oscillating resistance, oppositional resistance (multiple accounts, @-sharing in comments, collaborative group chats, sending expensive virtual gifts), and extreme/media resistance (deleting the platform, canceling accounts).
- Three domains of oscillating resistance with sub-counts: simplified categorization (n = 18), commercial exploitation (n = 16; an additional n = 14 showed wavering resistance tied to entertainment needs), and political agenda-setting / surveillance fears (n = 12 feared punishment for resistant behaviors).
- Folk theories were learned not only from lived experience but importantly from educational videos by Douyin's official accounts (e.g., "Douyin Assistant"/"Douyin Security Assistant") and from government/public news; participants frequently used "algorithm" as an everyday term learned from such sources.
- Douyin is positioned as distinct from TikTok because it operates under more intricate power structures (Chinese government, third-party commercial entities, end users), and Douyin's 2023 demographics skew young/urban (cited: ~60% in the 18-35 age range, 53% female / 47% male, ~70% urban/metropolitan).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The author explicitly notes the sample focuses on young, urban, algorithmically aware individuals and "does not capture resistance behaviors from less digitally engaged or algorithmically aware demographics"; future work should sample more diverse age groups, geographic locations, and engagement levels.
- The paper frames its core finding as a "paradox of resistance" resembling the privacy paradox (Barth & de Jong, 2017): stated algorithmic awareness and annoyance do not translate into resistant action, so self-reports of awareness should not be taken as predictive of behavior.
- It argues most resistance remains "predominantly confined to the dominant use of technological affordances," lacking transformative capacity to subvert ideological power—i.e., folk theories function as negotiation, not subversion (an interpretive claim, not a measured effect).
- The author complicates "digital resignation" (Draper & Turow, 2019; Hargittai & Marwick, 2016): here resignation is attributed substantially to platform/state-produced educational videos and censorship, not only to a generic feeling of powerlessness; lack of resistance is reinterpreted as "active passivity" (Siles, 2023) rather than pure apathy.
- As a small qualitative diary study (n = 31) using self-recorded screens and retrospective interviews, findings are context-specific to Douyin in China and not statistically generalizable; the categorization of resistance is presented as fluid and non-mutually-exclusive rather than a fixed typology.
