## Summary
This theoretical review challenges the dominant "Theory of Mind" (ToM) framework for explaining human and animal social cognition, arguing that research on "implicit" ToM (which claims preverbal infants and great apes track others' false beliefs) suffers from explanatory circularity, over-intellectualisation, and inconsistent empirical replication. The authors propose adapting "script theory" — a framework originally developed in artificial intelligence (Schank & Abelson, 1977; Abelson, 1981) to explain how linguistic agents comprehend event sequences — for application to non-linguistic agents (great apes and preverbal infants). Script theory holds that individuals build event-schemata bottom-up through their own experience as agents and observers, organised around agency-detection, non-mentalistic goal understanding, and the perception of basic event structure (agents, objects, actions), then deploy these scripts top-down to recognise and predict others' behaviour. The authors re-interpret the classic Onishi & Baillargeon (2005) change-of-location false-belief paradigm in script-theoretic terms via "causal chaining," "script suspension," and "script de-activation," and argue this explains patterns of inconsistent replication (especially in anticipatory-looking studies) better than ToM. They claim script theory avoids imputing higher-order metarepresentations while still allowing genuinely mentalistic cognition to emerge later in ontogeny, and they generate novel predictions for gaze patterns, "second try" trials, and memory for goal-relevant versus goal-irrelevant scene aspects.

## Key facts it relies on
- The term "theory of mind" was originally coined by Premack & Woodruff (1978) in work with captive chimpanzees (*Pan troglodytes*); the authors use ToM to denote the ability to hold and draw inferences from metarepresentations (representations of others' mental states), treating "mentalizing" and "mind-reading" as synonymous.
- The landmark implicit-ToM study (Onishi & Baillargeon, 2005) used a Violation-of-Expectation (VoE) Sally-Anne paradigm: prelinguistic infants showed greater surprise (longer looking time) when an owner searched in the object's current location rather than where she last saw it, interpreted as implicit false-belief understanding; Southgate, Senju & Csibra (2007) obtained similar results using anticipatory-looking (AL) measures.
- A core critique is an "explanatory circle": metarepresentational ToM is plausibly an outcome of linguistic competence (in part, per Lohmann & Tomasello, 2003), yet 4th-order intentionality (needed to represent communicative intentions, e.g. Moore, 2017; Sperber, 2000) appears much later in ontogeny than language — around 11-12 years (Liddle & Nettle, 2007; Moore, 2017) — so ToM cannot easily be acquired in advance of the capacities it is meant to explain.
- Key results showing implicit false-belief understanding in young infants have not been consistently replicated (Baillargeon et al., 2018; Kampis et al., 2020); replication attempts using VoE paradigms generally show better replication than anticipatory-looking studies (Barone, Corradi & Gomila, 2019), and AL measures do not correlate with VoE measures (Dörrenberg, Rakoczy & Liszkowski, 2018).
- Script theory derives from schema theory (Mandler, 2014) and AI work on scripts (Schank & Abelson, 1977; Abelson, 1981); scripts have two structural features — ontology (roles, props, goals) and scenes (sequences of acts, each serving a sub-goal) — illustrated by the paradigmatic restaurant script (Fig. 1: entry condition customer hungry and has money; roles customer/server/chef; scenes entering, sitting, ordering, eating, paying, leaving).
- The authors instantiate a non-linguistic script with a hypothesised chimpanzee (*Pan troglodytes schweinfurthii*) honey-collecting script (Fig. 2) with three scenes — hive inspection, optional tool acquisition, and honey extraction — contrasting the sponge-dipping strategy of the Sonso (Budongo Forest) community with the stick-dipping strategy of the Kanyawara (Kibale National Park) community (Gruber et al., 2009, 2011, 2012).
- The script re-interpretation of the change-of-location task (Fig. 3) uses "causal chaining" (actions/scenes linked by causal conditions), "script suspension" (causal interrelations upset but agent still pursues the path, entry conditions for the next scene unmet), and "script de-activation" (agent ceases pursuing the goal); the memories formed in familiarisation trials are first-order representations of objective states of the world, not the second- or higher-order representations ToM posits.
- The authors survey and contrast many extant de-intellectualising alternatives — behavioural rules (Perner & Ruffman, 2005), statistical learning (Ruffman, 2014), submentalising (Heyes, 2014), interaction/direct-perception theory (Gallagher & Hutto, 2008), teleology and teleology-in-perspective (Gergely & Csibra, 2003; Perner et al., 2018), the awareness relations account (Martin & Santos, 2016), mental files (Nanay, 2020; Recanati, 2012), the two-systems account (Apperly & Butterfill, 2009), knowledge-first approaches (Phillips et al., 2021), and naïve sociology (Fiske, 1992; Kaufmann & Fabrice, 2003).

## Critical notes from the literature
- This is a theoretical/review proposal, not an empirical study: script theory is offered as a reinterpretation and prediction-generator; the authors explicitly state they "do not propose that scripts can account for every aspect of the failed replications" and that several of their reinterpretations remain "open empirical question[s]."
- The authors acknowledge competing de-intellectualised accounts overlap substantially with scripts (e.g. interaction theory "ha[s] much in common" with their account; behavioural-reading accounts share the central role of goal-understanding), so script theory's distinctiveness rests largely on its hierarchical goal structure, flexibility, and emphasis on salient features of social environments rather than on a fully novel mechanism.
- The novel predictions (script-theoretic gaze biases / "attentional black hole," "second try" trials, differential memory for goal-relevant vs goal-irrelevant scene aspects, cross-species and cross-age cue-triggering studies) are proposed but untested within the paper; the authors note replication attempts are "still on-going" (Schuwerk et al., 2021) and frame their interpretation as a conjecture about what such attempts will show.
- The paper itself notes the empirical instability it builds on: false-belief findings in great apes have "largely failed to find evidence" historically (Call & Tomasello, 1999; Kaminski et al., 2008; Krachun et al., 2009), and even recent positive eye-tracking results (Krupenye et al., 2016; Kano et al., 2019) admit alternative non-mentalistic explanations such as submentalising and behavioural rules.
