## Summary
The paper asks how disclosing AI involvement in customer-service email communication (AI-mediated communication, AI-MC) affects customer trust in an online retailer, and whether the criticality of the service situation moderates this effect. The authors ran a pre-registered-style online between-subjects experiment with a 3 (degree of AI-MC: email written by a service employee vs. service employee assisted by AI vs. AI on behalf of the service employee) x 2 (service criticality: high vs. low) design, presenting N = 294 German-speaking participants with a screenshot of a product-return email scenario in the consumer-electronics sector. Trust was measured on the components of benevolence, integrity, and competence (McKnight et al. 2002) plus behavioral-intention items. They found customer trust in the retailer was significantly higher when the email was labeled as written by a service employee than when it was labeled as written by a service employee assisted by AI (a moderate effect), but trust did not differ significantly between the employee and the "AI on behalf of the employee" conditions. Decomposing trust, the assisted-by-AI condition lowered both benevolence and integrity, while AI-on-behalf-of lowered only integrity; competence showed a significant omnibus effect but no significant pairwise differences. Contrary to the hypothesis (and to Mozafari et al. 2021), service criticality did not moderate the AI-MC–trust relationship, and AI-MC had no effect on behavioral-intention trust measures. The authors discuss implications for the human-in-the-loop CRM approach and the EU AI Act's AI-labeling requirement (in force 1 August 2024).

## Key facts it relies on
- Design: 3 (degree of AI-MC: service employee vs. service employee assisted by AI vs. AI on behalf of the service employee) x 2 (service criticality: high vs. low) between-subjects online experiment; stimulus was a Gmail-style screenshot of a product-return email, employee named "Alex Schneider."
- Sample: N = 494 completed; N = 200 excluded for failing the end-of-survey attention check (could not identify their condition); final N = 294 German-speaking participants (60.20% female, 37.76% male, 2.04% diverse; M age = 27.14, SD = 12.08; 61.22% currently enrolled students).
- H1 (partially supported): one-way ANCOVA main effect of AI-MC condition on mean trust, F(2, 286) = 4.65, p = 0.010, ηp² = 0.03. Trust was higher in the complete-human-agency condition (EMM = 5.16, SE = 0.14) than the shared human/AI-agency condition (EMM = 4.66, SE = 0.09), t(291) = 3.03, p = 0.007, d = 0.54 (moderate). Human vs. complete-AI-agency (EMM = 4.84, SE = 0.08) and shared vs. complete-AI differences were non-significant (ps ≥ 0.118).
- H2 (largely supported): significant AI-MC main effects on benevolence F(2, 286) = 4.59, p = 0.011, ηp² = 0.03; integrity F(2, 286) = 4.02, p = 0.019, ηp² = 0.03; competence F(2, 286) = 3.16, p = 0.044, ηp² = 0.02. Human agency was rated higher than shared human/AI on benevolence (EMM 5.15 vs. 4.49, t(291) = 3.03, p = 0.007, d = 0.54) and integrity (EMM 5.50 vs. 5.01, t(291) = 2.78, p = 0.015, d = 0.50); human vs. complete-AI differed on integrity (EMM 5.50 vs. 5.09, t(291) = 2.42, p = 0.041, d = 0.42, small) but not benevolence (p = 0.088). Competence had no significant pairwise differences (ps ≥ 0.084).
- H3 (not supported / rejected): ANCOVAs showed no significant AI-MC × service-criticality interaction for any trust measure (ps ≥ 0.559) and no main effect of service criticality (ps ≥ 0.090).
- Behavioral-intention trust (willingness to click the link, share name/address, share IBAN) showed no significant AI-MC main effect (ps ≥ 0.167); age and disposition to trust were significant covariates for these items.
- Manipulation check worked: perceived criticality higher in the high (M = 6.45, SD = 0.96) than low (M = 6.16, SD = 1.03) condition, t(284.82) = 2.50, p = 0.014, d = 0.29. Scenarios rated highly realistic (M = 5.49, SD = 1.30, vs. midpoint 4, t(293) = 19.64, p < 0.001, d = 1.15).
- Measures: trust via McKnight et al. (2002) e-commerce scale; benevolence α = 0.80, integrity α = 0.83, competence α = 0.81, aggregate trust α = 0.90; disposition to trust α = 0.89; attitude toward AI via German ATAI scale (Sindermann et al. 2021), 5 items, α = 0.81; all 7-point Likert. Covariates in ANCOVAs: age, gender, disposition to trust, attitude toward AI. ANOVA/ANCOVA used Type III SS due to unbalanced cell sizes; Tukey post hoc on EMMs.
- Trust defined per Mayer et al. (1995) as willingness to be vulnerable; three-component structure (benevolence, integrity, competence) per McKnight and Chervany (2001). Key prior anchors: Liu et al. (2022) found lowest trust when AI completely mediated email; Jakesch et al. (2019) "Replicant Effect"; Mozafari et al. (2021) service-criticality moderation of chatbot-disclosure trust.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors acknowledge the service-criticality manipulation may have been too weak to produce moderation; they label the null H3 finding as preliminary and suggest stronger manipulations (e.g., car vs. car accessory, or uncertain/unsuccessful return outcomes).
- Sample is a convenience sample recruited via a university distribution list and social media in the context of an undergraduate course: highly educated, majority students, German-speaking only; the authors caution results may not generalize across cultures/countries or to other product segments (fashion, food) or business models (B2B, D2C).
- A large exclusion (N = 200 of 494, ~40%) for failing the attention check produced uneven, small cell sizes (e.g., high-criticality complete-human-agency had n = 25); the authors note this imbalance is a limitation, handled via Type III SS.
- The dissociation between explicit trust ratings (affected by AI-MC) and behavioral-intention measures (unaffected) is flagged by the authors as surprising and "at odds" with McKnight et al. (2002); they recommend more established trusting-intention measures in future work.
- The finding that complete AI agency was not less trusted than human agency contradicts Liu et al. (2022); the authors interpret it via algorithm appreciation (Logg et al. 2019) and the Replicant Effect, but present it as an open discrepancy needing replication.
