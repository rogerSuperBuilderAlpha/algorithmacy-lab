## Summary
This conceptual philosophy paper asks what the rapid adoption of commercial Generative AI does to epistemic trust in online communication, focusing on social media. The authors analyze "AI-mediated communication" (AI-MC), defined (following Hancock et al. 2020) as mediated communication in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages. They argue, on descriptive grounds (citing empirical attitude research) and normative grounds, that AI-MC tends to lower epistemic trust, and that this is at least sometimes justified. The central thesis is the "AI-MC dilemma": there are cases where reduced trust in AI-MC is warranted (avoiding epistemic gullibility), and cases where it is unjustified (producing discrimination and epistemic injustice), but because we often cannot tell which case any given instance belongs to, we must choose between maintaining normal trust levels (risking gullibility) or adopting generally reduced trust (risking injustice). They apply this dilemma to social media as an epistemic environment, argue both horns are problematic and that a middle path is hard to achieve at scale, and discuss three proposed remedies (mandatory disclosure, AI literacy, expertise markers), finding each insufficient. They conclude that responsibility may need to shift toward disseminators being epistemically responsible by competently verifying and sincerely endorsing AI-MC as their own.

## Key facts it relies on
- AI-MC is defined (Hancock et al. 2020, p. 90) as "mediated communication between people in which a computational agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication or interpersonal goals"; the authors extend it to originate from the sender, receiver, or both.
- Epistemic trust is framed (Fricker 2021) as trusting that others' communications impart knowledge; the paper argues epistemic trust is directed primarily at the human communicator deploying the AI tool, not the technology, because the human is the bearer of epistemic responsibility.
- The paper draws on Origgi's (2012) seven mechanisms for epistemic trust, McCraw's (2015) four components (belief, communication, reliance, confidence), Grasswick's (2018) two conditions (competence and sincerity), and Daukas's (2006) truth-connected and character-based account.
- Empirical descriptive support cited: AI-MC reduces perceptions of authenticity (Glikson & Asscher 2023), trust (Jakesch et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2022), responsibility (Formosa et al. 2024a, 2024b), and competence (Weiss et al. 2022); greater AI assistance yields larger reductions (Formosa et al. 2024a, 2024b).
- The dilemma is stated as a three-premise argument: (1) AI-MC's epistemic risks justify a general stance of reduced trust toward possibly-AI-mediated social media content; (2) for many cases we cannot reliably tell whether reduced trust is justified; (3) we must therefore choose between (3.1) maintaining normal trust (risking epistemic gullibility) or (3.2) reduced trust (risking epistemic injustice when unjustified).
- Four factors are identified as justifying reduced trust in AI-MC: hallucinations/biases/inaccuracies (undermining the truth-connected element); illusions of understanding and explanatory depth (Messeri & Crockett 2024; Wilkenfeld et al. 2016); AI influencing what people communicate and which viewpoints they express (Jakesch et al. 2023a, 2023b; Williams-Ceci et al. 2024); and opacity of AI systems clouding judgments about content reliability (Origgi 2012).
- Real-world examples cited include the Green Cicada Network (at least 5,000 X/Twitter accounts powered by generative AI engaging in divisive political issues, CyberCX 2024), Vanderbilt University apologizing for using ChatGPT in a console email after a mass shooting (Levine 2023), a lawyer's ChatGPT-produced legal filing citing fake/hallucinated cases (Bohannon 2023), and Apple suspending an AI news-summary feature after BBC/New York Times complaints of inaccuracy (Milmo 2025).
- Evidence cited that people cannot reliably distinguish AI- from human-generated content: Köbis & Mossink (2021) on AI poetry with human selection; Clark et al. (2021) on non-experts being unable to beat random chance for stories, news, and recipes (using GPT-2/GPT-3); Kreps et al. (2022) on AI news content given equal/more credibility; and Yoo et al. (2024) finding false ChatGPT statements with rhetorical nuance perceived as more truthful than accurate non-rhetorical statements.
- Three proposed remedies are evaluated and found insufficient on their own: mandatory AI disclosure/transparency (El Ali et al. 2024), AI literacy (Huang & Ball 2024, who found intermediate-literacy users show more skepticism in high-stakes contexts), and expertise markers (Metzger & Flanagin 2013).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly scope the dilemma to social media, noting it "may manifest differently (or not at all) in other contexts," such as direct interpersonal communication with individuals whose expertise and character are already known or verifiable.
- They acknowledge an objection that AI use can be an epistemic "net positive": domain experts can catch AI biases/hallucinations, and non-experts relying on AI may make claims more likely true than going unaided; but they argue that for non-experts even true AI-MC is a matter of epistemic luck (Pritchard 2005, 2007), which is incompatible with knowledge, so reduced trust remains warranted.
- The paper concedes that some challenges (propaganda, mis/disinformation, gullibility, faked credibility) predated AI-MC, but argues AI heightens these and creates new, more subtle pathways for forging credibility (Glickman & Sharot 2024; Danry et al. 2024).
- The authors note that AI text-detection tools are neither widely accessible nor highly accurate and suffer high false-positive rates (Desaire et al. 2023; Elkhatat et al. 2023; Kumarage et al. 2023), and that blanket disclosure requirements risk epistemic injustice toward non-native speakers and marginalized groups using AI legitimately (Altay & Gilardi 2024).
- This is a normative/conceptual analysis, not an empirical study; its descriptive premises rest on cited prior empirical work rather than new data collected by the authors.
