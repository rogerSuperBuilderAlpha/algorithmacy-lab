## Summary
Although evidence-based algorithms consistently outperform human forecasters, people often abandon them once they learn the algorithms are imperfect, a phenomenon the authors (Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey 2015) call algorithm aversion. Across three incentivized forecasting experiments (predicting high-school students' standardized math-test percentiles from nine variables), participants chose between using their own forecasts or those of an expert-built statistical model that was off by 17.5 percentiles on average. The central finding is that participants were considerably more likely to choose the imperfect algorithm when they could modify its forecasts, and they performed better and earned more money as a result. Notably, this preference held even when modifications were severely restricted, and was relatively insensitive to the magnitude of the allowed adjustment: willingness to use the model was statistically unchanged across conditions that permitted adjustments of 10, 5, or 2 percentiles. The authors interpret this as a desire for *some* control rather than *greater* control. Study 3 further shows that letting people modify the algorithm increased their satisfaction with the process, raised their confidence in and regard for the model relative to themselves, and increased their use of the model on a subsequent round; constraining adjustments (adjust-by-10) kept forecasts closer to the model and yielded better long-run performance than unconstrained use.

## Key facts it relies on
- The forecasting task asked participants to estimate the percentiles of real high school seniors on a standardized math test using nine variables; participants were truthfully told the model was off by 17.5 percentiles on average (i.e., imperfect). Study 1 N = 288 (lab, avg 22 years, 66% female).
- Study 1: only 32% of participants in the can't-change condition chose to use the model, versus 73% in the change-10 condition (chi2(1, N=145)=24.19, p<.001) and 76% in the adjust-by-10 condition (chi2(1, N=146)=28.40, p<.001).
- Study 1 conditions: can't-change (choose own vs. model exclusively), adjust-by-10 (adjust all forecasts by up to 10 percentiles), change-10 (overrule 10 of 20 forecasts by any amount), and use-freely (receive model's forecasts and use as desired).
- Study 1 performance: can't-change participants made larger average errors than adjust-by-10 (t(144)=3.40, p<.001), change-10 (t(143)=3.09, p=.002), and use-freely (t(143)=4.01, p<.001), and earned smaller bonuses (e.g., vs. adjust-by-10 t(144)=-2.90, p=.004).
- Study 2 (N = 816 MTurk; 800 pre-registered target, 200/condition; avg 34 years, 48% female): 47% in can't-change chose the model vs. 70% across adjust-by-X conditions (chi2(1, N=834)=36.46, p<.001). Choice rates were 71%, 71%, and 68% for adjust-by-10, adjust-by-5, and adjust-by-2, not significantly different (chi2(2, N=623)=0.42, p=.809) despite an 80% reduction in allowed adjustment.
- Study 3 (N = 818 MTurk; avg 33 years, 49% female) used a two-round design with model-only (half the sample), use-freely (quarter), and adjust-by-10 (quarter) conditions; participants made 10 forecasts per round.
- Study 3: participants assigned to model-only in Round 1 were less satisfied than those in adjust-by-10 (t(614)=-6.59, p<.001) and use-freely (t(620)=-6.17, p<.001); adjust-by-10 and use-freely participants did not differ in satisfaction (t(410)=0.56, p=.578).
- Study 3 Round 2 choice: over 80% of participants in each condition chose to use the model in some manner; combining own judgment with the algorithm was most common (51%-74%), versus own forecasts only (11%-19%) or algorithm only (10%-32%).
- Study 3 performance: those who chose adjust-by-10 had lower average absolute error (M=17.90) than those who chose use-freely (M=20.25; t(510)=-6.28, p<.001) and own forecasts (M=24.50); a mediation analysis showed deviation from the model mediated this effect (95% CI -2.19 to -0.85).
- Supplementary Study S5 (2x2, N=2,014): allowing modification raised choice of a human advisor's forecasts (21% to 41%) and an algorithm's (46% to 69%) to a similar degree, suggesting the effect generalizes beyond algorithms to advice taking.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors acknowledge that people's attempts to adjust algorithmic forecasts often make them worse (citing Carbone et al. 1983; Goodwin & Fildes 1999; Hogarth & Makridakis 1981; Lim & O'Connor 1995; Willemain 1991); the proposed benefit is that increased algorithm use outweighs the cost of degraded forecasts, especially when adjustments are constrained.
- The Study 1 cross-condition deviation comparisons are described by the authors as "imperfect due to potential selection concerns" (participants self-selected into using the model); the clean causal test of constraint vs. free adjustment is Study 3.
- Scope conditions noted by the authors: results may differ for tasks where forecasters hold valuable private information the algorithm lacks (large adjustments could then help, citing Fildes et al. 2009; Lawrence et al. 2006); effects may diminish for far-more-imperfect algorithms; insensitivity to adjustment magnitude was only tested over a 2-to-10 percentile range, so wider ranges (e.g., 1 or 20 percentiles) are untested.
- All studies used student-population samples (lab and MTurk) and a single forecasting domain; the authors note research with professional forecasters would be especially informative, and that effects could depend on task or population characteristics.
