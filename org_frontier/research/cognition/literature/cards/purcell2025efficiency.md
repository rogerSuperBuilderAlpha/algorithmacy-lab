## Summary
This paper asks whether using AI assistance to compose messages changes how much trust those messages earn, and whether any time saved makes trust-building more efficient. Across two trust-game studies, senders (Player B) wrote messages either alone ("write-alone") or with AI assistance ("AI-assisted"), with AI use either disclosed or not, while receivers (Player A) decided how much to trust them (points sent, plus subjective trust ratings and competence/benevolence/integrity sub-ratings). In Study 1 the manipulation succeeded (treatment strongly shifted beliefs that AI had been used), but treatment had little or no effect on objective or subjective trust, with Bayes factors favoring the null (e.g., subjective trust 36.8x more likely under the no-treatment model). In Study 2, with more receivers and games, AI use produced only a small reduction in objective trust (write-alone eM=5.78 vs. AI-assisted eM=5.51) and no robust subjective-trust penalty, while AI use had a large positive effect on "Return on Investment Indices" (ROIIs) — trust earned per unit of composition effort — for both objective (eM=3.52 vs. 2.27) and subjective trust (eM=35.51 vs. 21.52). The headline conclusion is that AI assistance barely dents trust but substantially boosts trust-building efficiency. An exploratory, non-preregistered finding is an AI-Use x Gender interaction: male (but not female) participants extended less trust to AI-assisted messages.

## Key facts it relies on
- Design: a trust-game paradigm where Player B composes messages either write-alone or AI-assisted, crossed with disclosure (and in Study 2 also expected disclosure); Player A's points sent = objective trust, plus subjective trust and competence/benevolence/integrity ratings (this PDF is the supplementary material; main-text Methods narrative is not included here).
- Study 1 manipulation check was successful: treatment strongly affected beliefs that AI was used (Deviance=121.34, num df=2, den df=599, p≈0), with all pairwise contrasts significant (e.g., AI-Assisted+Disclosed vs. Non-Disclosed estimate=-2.36, Z=-9.67).
- Study 1 found no significant treatment effect on trust: objective trust F=2.72, p=0.068; subjective trust F=0.44, p=0.647; expected return F=0.39, p=0.675 (all num df=2).
- Bayesian ANOVAs in Study 1 favored the null: Bayes factors of 0.26 (objective trust; data 3.9x more likely without treatment), 0.03 (subjective trust; 36.8x), and 0.03 (expected return; 35.8x).
- Study 1 found no treatment effect on the three trust components: competence F=2.50 (p=0.084), benevolence F=0.91 (p=0.403), integrity F=0.98 (p=0.376).
- Study 2 (larger: e.g., manipulation-check null residual df=3,934) showed a small but significant AI-use effect on objective trust (F=5.21, p=0.023): write-alone eM=5.78 (SE=0.14) earned more trust than AI-assisted eM=5.51 (SE=0.14).
- Study 2 efficiency results: AI use had a large effect on ROII:objective (F=41.66, eta sq.=0.09, p<0.001; AI-assisted eM=3.52 vs. write-alone eM=2.27) and on ROII:subjective (F=52.95, eta sq.=0.11, p<0.001; AI-assisted eM=35.51 vs. write-alone eM=21.52).
- Study 2 subjective trust showed a main effect of expected disclosure (F=4.31, p=0.039; non-disclosed-expected eM=55.7 > disclosure-expected eM=53.93) and an AI-Use x Actual-Disclosure interaction (F=7.02, p=0.008), but no write-alone vs. AI-assisted difference within either disclosed or non-disclosed conditions.
- Exploratory, non-preregistered AI-Use x Gender interaction (Study 2): male participants sent significantly fewer points to AI-assisted messages, t(687)=-3.07, p=.002, estimate=-0.42; female participants showed no significant difference, t(729)=-1.13, p=.26, estimate=-0.16 (Kenward-Roger df).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly caution that the gender-difference findings are small and not preregistered and "should be considered tentative until apriori confirmation is achieved."
- The trust penalty for AI use is small (Study 1 showed essentially none; Study 2 objective-trust eta sq.=0.01), so the central claim rests on the efficiency (ROII) gain rather than on AI improving trust per se; the supplement notes Study 1's effects were "little or no effect of treatment on trust" and that the nuanced AI/disclosure effects were exploratory.
- Several Study 2 interactions involving disclosure (e.g., AI-Use x Actual-Disclosure on benevolence/integrity) were significant in the omnibus test but had no significant pairwise comparisons after multiple-comparison adjustment, limiting their interpretability.
- This acquired PDF is the supplementary information only; the main-article abstract, full Methods (sample sizes, AI tool used, exact ROII formula and timing measurement), and primary-analysis prose are not contained in this file, so several design specifics are inferred from supplement table notes rather than read from the main text.
