## Summary
This is a commentary by two anthropologists of data and AI responding to growing calls for ethnography as a method to study Artificial Intelligence. The authors agree that ethnography can reveal AI as a sociotechnical phenomenon and expose underlying causes and power asymmetries that quantitative methods miss, but they argue that ethnography must be properly grounded in specific ways of engaging with the field site or research outcomes risk becoming distorted. They identify three key methodological requirements for robust ethnographies of AI: (1) committed fieldwork with adequate time investment (or, where time is limited, close attention plus appropriate local support), (2) trusting relationships between researchers and participants, and (3) attentiveness to subtle, ambiguous, or absent-present data. They illustrate each requirement with examples from their own and other anthropologists' fieldwork, emphasizing that subtle, hidden, or silent data is often overlooked yet crucial. They close by advocating research that studies the interactions, collaborations, frictions, and failures in human-machine relations rather than human or technological elements in isolation.

## Key facts it relies on
- The paper is a COMMENT/commentary (not an empirical study); it states data sharing is not applicable as no individual data were generated or analyzed, and oral informed consent was obtained from all participants.
- It names three key methodological focus points for ethnography of AI: committed fieldwork, establishing trustful relationships in the field, and attentiveness to subtle/nuanced/absent-present data.
- It frames the problem via critiques of quantitative methods: uncritical positivist quantitative use fails to consider contextual factors (Marda and Narayan, 2021) and underlying causes and power asymmetries, contributing to the "blackboxing" of AI; organizations can exploit this blackboxing to evade legal responsibility (Sartori and Theodorou, 2022).
- Long-term fieldwork example: Nick Seaver's (2021) fieldwork on recommendation algorithms in the music industry spanned several years, yielding the insight that engineers balance "care and scale" rather than treating them as contradictory values.
- Jakarta slum case (first author's PhD research, conducted 2008-2014): only after living in the community for over a year did she learn residents refused free, praised flood-protection technology because they feared being identified/traced via collected personal data (many were undocumented or in criminalized work such as sex work or gang membership); it took 6 months to realize she was living with an undocumented sex worker.
- Dementia case: Featherstone and Northcott (2021) conducted a 5-year ethnographic study in acute wards in the UK, observing that people with dementia could not verbally rate pain on a standardized "pain ladder from 1 to 10" but ethnographers were attentive to their cries of agony.
- "Gaps and silences" / "absent present" (Law, 2002): example of a Netherlands nursing-home resident whose plush cat decreased her anxiety so efficiently she needed no medication for restlessness, yet the cat did not appear in her healthcare records ("on paper, the cat did not exist") and was invisible in policy and funding schemes.
- Human-nonhuman complexity case (first author, UK health app): a medium-sized organization gave employees "fit points" and gift vouchers for health behaviors; the ethnographer learned that although users believed an algorithm awarded vouchers, decisions were actually made manually by the programmers because the algorithm made numerous mistakes in testing, and management's plan to scale it up caused anxiety until programmers built a functioning algorithm within a few weeks.
- It draws on Seaver (2018) to warn against binary narratives (humans vs. algorithms, culture vs. technology) and against merely filling the "analog slot" with social insight while centering the digital.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper explicitly identifies itself as a commentary entailing the authors' ideas/opinions based on their careers, not new shared participant data; the three cases come from research across their careers, and only the Jakarta case received formal ethical approval (AISSR Ethics Advisory Board) while the second and third were preliminary and did not collect direct quotes or personal data.
- It is a short opinion/agenda-setting piece with no systematic methodology, no comparative evaluation, and no quantitative claims; its "evidence" is a small set of illustrative anecdotes selected to support the three focus points.
- The authors acknowledge a tension they do not fully resolve: long-term fieldwork (months to years) is presented as central to uncovering crucial data, yet they also claim short-term fieldwork "can be characterized by intense moments" of deep understanding (citing Pink and Morgan, 2013; Marcus and Okely, 2007) provided local support and attention are present.
- The paper itself warns that without proper grounding, ethnographic research outcomes on AI "may become distorted" and that failing to observe the three key points risks distorting outcomes; this is a normative caution rather than a demonstrated finding.
