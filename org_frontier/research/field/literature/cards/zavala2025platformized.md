## Summary
The article asks how app stores construct children as a software audience through platform governance and industry practices, and whether a form of "audience commodity" specific to children exists in app stores operating under revenue-sharing models. Drawing on platform studies, critical childhood studies, and Smythe's audience-commodity concept, the authors apply an adapted "critical walkthrough" method plus textual analysis to compare Google Play and Apple's App Store. They examine four interfaces across two material layers: the developer submission platforms (App Store Connect, Play Console) and the consumer-facing "Kids" sections, with walkthroughs conducted on the English/Netherlands stores between April and July 2023 using three devices. They find app stores govern children's apps commercially: target-audience selection by age forces children into a distinct segment, while data-protection regulation (COPPA, GDPR) blocks the usual data-driven commodification, so platforms fall back on classic market segmentation by age. The authors argue this combines with "industry lore" of a universal childhood (Havens) to produce what they term "platformized childhood" — a regime where platform governance inscribes children as a global audience commodity for developers, advertisers, and the kidtech ad sector, while recasting "Kids" sections as educational and safe to address parental concerns.

## Key facts it relies on
- Globally, an estimated one-third of internet users are children (UNICEF, 2017); the authors repeatedly frame this demographic as one-third of the online/global population.
- In Western markets Google Play and Apple's App Store hold a duopoly (Nieborg et al., 2020); platform owners typically retain 30% of every transaction as fees (Cuadrado and Dueñas, 2012).
- Timeline of children's sections: Apple introduced the "Kids" category in 2013 (linked to COPPA compliance); Google launched "Design for Families" in 2015; in 2020 Google replaced it with the "Kids" tab and the "Teacher Approved" badge.
- Method: adapted walkthrough method (Light et al., 2018) focused on abstract-level affordances using goal-oriented research personas, combined with textual analysis of secondary sources; walkthroughs ran April–July 2023 on three devices, analyzing the English "Kids" sections in the Netherlands.
- Two regulatory regimes govern Western markets: US COPPA and EU GDPR, both requiring verifiable parental/caregiver consent before collecting Personal Identifiable Information (PII) from children; these disrupt freemium monetization of children's apps.
- The freemium model is the most prevalent on app stores; compliance with children's regulations spurred "kidtech" child-safe ad networks (e.g., SuperAwesome, Kidoz) offering third-party SDKs with "contextual targeting technology."
- Content-rating forms differ: Apple's App Store Connect "Age Rating" form uses 12 categories and a three-scale system with four age groups (4+, 9+, 12+, 17+); Google's Play Console "Content Ratings" form is granular, with up to 75 nested questions, and converts to regional authorities (e.g., ESRB, PEGI, coordinated via IARC).
- Both platforms categorize child audiences into three groups, with two shared groups ("ages 5 and under" and "6–8"); placement in the "Kids" section is driven by selecting children as target audience, not by picking a category — "Kids" is curiously absent from the app-category dropdown.
- "Kids" is not a standalone category: on the App Store users scroll through approximately 57 lists to find "Kids" under "Top Categories"; each platform offers 17 game genres, but only the App Store lists "Kids" as one (redirecting to the Kids apps section).
- Ad rules differ: Apple prohibits external analytics and behavioral-advertising data collection for children, allowing only third-party contextual, "human-reviewed" ads; Google requires children's-app ads to come exclusively from services in the "Families Self-Certified Ads SDK Program." Both require "age gating" (Google's "neutral age screen"; Apple's "parental gates").

## Critical notes from the literature
- Scope/positionality: the authors analyzed only the English-language "Kids" sections in the Netherlands, reflecting their location and devices; they argue generalizability rests on the standardized global design of app stores and consistent developer interfaces, but acknowledge regional regulatory variations may affect "Kids" section composition.
- The study analyzes interfaces and documents only; the authors note future research could add interviews with developers and advertisers to test the prevalence, circulation, and "translation" of the industry lore they infer, and could examine kidtech SDK certification and advertising models directly.
- The "platformized childhood" / "audience commodity" claims are interpretive, built atop borrowed concepts (Smythe's audience commodity, Havens's universal childhood, Nieborg's player commodity, Ørmen and Gregersen's all-participant commodification) rather than measured outcomes; the paper is a critical conceptual contribution, not an empirical measurement of monetization.
- The authors explicitly distinguish their proposed "market segmentation as governance strategy" from the three instruments of Poell et al. (2022) (regulation, curation, moderation), positioning it as a novel addition — a framing internal to the paper rather than independently validated.
