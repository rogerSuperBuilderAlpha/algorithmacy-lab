The actual PDF (Corporaal & Lehdonvirta 2017) studies the demand side of online freelancing platforms: how and why large firms adopt "platform sourcing" to hire freelancers on demand. Using nine qualitative case studies (seven Fortune 500 firms and two MNCs) with 24 manager interviews plus platform-company interviews and documentary sources, the authors identify three motivations for adoption: easy access to a scalable source of labour/skills/expertise; reduced start-up and transaction costs; and the elimination of conventional hiring barriers. Comparing platform sourcing to conventional staffing agencies, they identify six dimensions of value (cost, speed, flexibility, organization, expertise, quality). They distinguish online freelancing from crowdsourcing, microwork, and outsourcing platforms, and argue that adoption (the decision to use) is distinct from implementation (skilled use in practice), which requires organizing work. They present seven practices of successful adoption and nine "do's and don'ts" for hiring/managing freelancers, and conclude with recommendations for building sustainable platform ecosystems. The work is positioned within management/innovation literature on numerical, functional, and financial flexibility and on innovation implementation.

## Key facts it relies on

- Method: nine case studies — seven Fortune 500 firms and two MNCs — sourcing work through one of the largest online freelancing platforms; 24 interviews with managers (Table 1), plus interviews with platform-company staff and documentary sources.
- Three motivations for adopting platform sourcing: (1) easy access to a scalable source of manpower/skills/expertise; (2) reducing start-up and transaction costs; (3) eliminating conventional hiring barriers (geographical, informational, administrative).
- Six dimensions of value of platform sourcing versus agency-based sourcing: cost, speed, flexibility, organization, expertise, quality.
- Cost evidence: House's manager Jason reported 60–70% aggregate savings versus conventional channels; an Echo manager could source the same work for about a third of the price; traditional-agency overhead was typically 25–30% (per Foxtrot's Rajeev).
- Speed evidence: hiring via an agency could take 6–8 weeks versus 2–4 days on average via a platform; ~60% of the time platform work was completed/delivered faster; a Samsung manager hired a freelancer and completed a project within a two-day timeframe.
- Quality evidence: at House, finance manager Jason found that 60% of the time platform freelancers delivered similar or higher-quality work than conventional channels.
- Top categories of work sourced on a leading platform overall: writing and translation (28% of projects), administrative support and customer service (25%), design and creative (14%), software development (14%); category mixes varied widely by firm (Table 3).
- Three flexibility types from management/IR literature (Atkinson 1984): numerical, functional, and financial flexibility; a typical workforce has a skilled "core" plus a flexible "periphery."
- Adoption vs. implementation distinction (Klein & Knight 2005): adoption is the decision to use an innovation; implementation is when members become skilled, consistent, and committed in practice.
- Concrete examples: Alpha's Bruce scaled content marketing from 50 to 1,500 articles published annually over two years by pairing in-house staff with platform freelancers; Golf's Gina built 10 global customer-support teams totaling 150 freelancers; House allocated $100,000 to project managers for a 100-day experiment with 15 project managers (10 projects each); Samsung ran a three-month pilot with 13 teams using engagements of two days to two weeks.

## Critical notes from the literature

- The authors explicitly note their participants were all early adopters experimenting with a new sourcing solution, so findings reflect pioneer firms rather than typical practice; for many managers the dominant pathway remained hiring temporary workers through conventional staffing agencies.
- Scope conditions acknowledged: participants still preferred agencies for work that must be performed onsite, requires someone full-time over a sustained period, or is hard to specify and evaluate; platform sourcing also requires a different communication/expectation-management style not every manager is comfortable with.
- Sample is small (nine cases) and demand-side only; the platform company participated in case selection and made initial introductions (though the authors state the rest of the research was independent), a potential selection/access bias.
- The report is largely qualitative and prescriptive (do's/don'ts, recommendations) rather than offering quantitative generalizable estimates; cost/speed/quality figures are drawn from specific manager accounts, not a representative survey.
