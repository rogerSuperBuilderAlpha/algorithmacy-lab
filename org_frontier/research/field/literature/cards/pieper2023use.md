## Summary
This is a systematic literature review of how scholars have empirically applied the Institutional Grammar (IG) 1.0 — Crawford and Ostrom's conceptual tool for breaking institutional statements into ABDICO components (Attribute, Deontic, aIm, oBject, Condition, Or Else) — for institutional analysis, especially commons governance. Using a forward-citation search in Web of Science (citing Crawford and Ostrom 1995, Basurto et al. 2010, and Siddiki et al. 2011) plus the IGRI website, the authors assembled a corpus of 51 peer-reviewed empirical articles published between 2010 and 2021 and coded them on geography, jurisdictional scale, publication outlet, research purpose, analytic approach, and empirical contribution. They find IG 1.0 is a flexible, adaptable tool concentrated in the Global North (49% of articles focus on U.S. institutional systems), dominated by descriptive and single-case/small-N studies, and increasingly used to operationalize hard-to-measure concepts such as polycentricity, discretion, and compliance. They argue that despite IG's utility for measuring individual concepts, it has not yet supported strong theoretical generalization about institutional design, and that frequency-counting and difficulty aggregating micro-level data remain limits. They conclude IG 1.0 (and the newer IG 2.0) could anchor an emergent "Computational Institutional Science" as text-analysis, NLP, and machine-learning advances enable automated extraction of rule configurations into structured databases.

## Key facts it relies on
- Corpus: 51 peer-reviewed empirical articles applying IG 1.0, spanning 2010 to 2021 (the abstract describes a "corpus of 48 empirical articles"; the body and conclusion repeatedly use 51). First actual implementation of the IG was Basurto et al. 2010, which is why the dataset begins in 2010 despite Crawford and Ostrom's 1995 origin paper.
- Method: forward search in Web of Science citing three pioneer articles (Crawford and Ostrom 1995; Basurto et al. 2010; Siddiki et al. 2011). In September 2020 there were 475 citations after removing duplicates, 403 of them citing only Crawford and Ostrom (1995). Filtering yielded 42 articles; a July 2021 IGRI-website review added 9 more, for 51.
- IG 1.0 components (ABDICO): A-Attribute (the actor), D-Deontic (may, must, must not, should, should not), I-aIm (the action), B-oBject (receiver of the action, added by Siddiki et al. 2011), C-Condition (where/when/how), O-Or Else (consequence for noncompliance). Statement types: strategy (AIC), norm (ADIC), rule (ADICO).
- Geography: 49% of reviewed articles focus on U.S. institutional systems. Figure 1 country-of-study counts: United States 25, Europe 8, Central America and Caribbean 5, Australia 4, Middle East 2, South America 1; 6 of 51 not mapped (3 multi-country, 3 modeling simulations).
- Jurisdictional scale (Table 3, total 51): Organizational 3, Local 15, Sub-national 14, National 12, Regional 2, International 2, No level 3 (more than half sub-national/local; 23% national/federal).
- Publication outlets: 36 journals; International Journal of the Commons is the top outlet with 6 articles, followed by Policy Studies Journal (5) and Public Administration (3).
- Purpose of analysis (Figure 2): (1) descriptive analysis 21 (41%), (2) policy analysis/program evaluation 17 (33%), (3) methodological approach 13 (26%).
- Analytic approach (Figure 3): Single Case Study 17, Single Case Study Longitudinal 9, Comparative Case Study (Small-N) 16, Comparative Case Study (Large-N) 5, Modeling 4. Over half (27) of papers use frequencies in some form.
- Empirical contribution categories: comparative institutional analysis (19 of 51 articles), interactions among actors (10 articles, 8 on individual/org interactions), actors and prescribed actions (9 articles), compliance/non-compliance (6 articles), grammar development (7 articles).
- Concepts operationalized (Table 4): policy coerciveness (Siddiki 2014), rule performance (Abebe et al. 2019), Networks of Prescribed Interactions/NPIs (Olivier 2019), polycentricity (Heikkila and Weible 2018), information sharing (Weible et al. 2017), discretion (Turner and Stiller 2020), legitimacy (Siddiki et al. 2012), self-reported compliance (Tschopp et al. 2018), machine coding via NLP R package (Rice et al. 2021).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors acknowledge IG 1.0 has "yet to support significant theoretical explanations"; most studies remain exploratory/descriptive and have not advanced to developing and testing theoretically grounded hypotheses about institutional design.
- Aggregation problem: coding generates large amounts of micro-level data, but the unit of analysis is often higher than the institutional statement; no single strategy has emerged for aggregating IG-coded data, a limitation the authors also attribute to Siddiki et al. (2022).
- IG 1.0 coding does not capture links or relationships between individual statements, causing loss of context and richness from the original document.
- Prior reviews differ in scope: the authors note Dunlop et al. (2019) criticized early empirical applications for merely counting IG components, and this review analyzes nearly twice as many articles (51) as Dunlop et al. (26) and Siddiki et al. (20).
- On IG 2.0 (Siddiki and Frantz 2022 / Frantz and Siddiki 2021): it adds constitutive-statement coding and nesting but the most-used components (Attributes, Deontics, aIms, oBjects) change little; the authors caution IG 2.0 "cannot resolve the challenges of theory development and cumulation of knowledge." No IG 2.0 applications were published at review time, so the review covers only IG 1.0.
