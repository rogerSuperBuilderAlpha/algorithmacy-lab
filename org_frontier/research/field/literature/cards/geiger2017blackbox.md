## Summary
Geiger asks what it means to participate in a highly automated, "anyone can edit" organization like Wikipedia, and argues that algorithmic transparency debates focused on "opening up the black box" of source code miss a crucial dimension: the local, contextual, learned expertise required to work with algorithmic systems. Drawing on a multiyear ethnography of infrastructure in Wikipedia (participant-observation, interviews, archival analysis, bot development), he presents two autoethnographic vignettes — a conflict-of-interest "edit request" process clerked by AnomieBot, and a "speedy deletion" (CSD) process driven by templates and the Twinkle browser extension. He shows that Wikipedia's bot-supported bureaucracies are experienced very differently by newcomers versus veterans, even though all the relevant code is open source. The core argument extends Burrell's three forms of machine-learning opacity (intentional secrecy, technical literacy, inherent ML opacity) by adding a fourth: the opacity of learning a particular institutional/organizational culture supported by algorithmic systems. He concludes that technical decisions are sociocultural decisions and that we should ask not whether algorithmic systems are negotiable in general, but for whom they are formal/rigid versus in-flux/negotiable, treating "algorithms as culture" (following Seaver) rather than merely "algorithms in culture."

## Key facts it relies on
- In the English-language Wikipedia, 22 of the 25 most active editors (by number of edits) are bot accounts, and in July 2017 bots made about 20% of all edits to encyclopedia articles.
- The author has participated in Wikipedia as a volunteer for 12 years starting in 2004, and began formally studying it as an ethnographer in 2008 (participant-observation, interviews, archival analysis, computational social science), including developing and operating his own bot and interning at the Wikimedia Foundation for three months.
- The argument extends Burrell's (2016) three forms of opacity in machine learning — intentional secrecy (proprietary source code), technical literacy (e.g., reading code), and opacities inherent in machine learning (e.g., interpretability) — by adding a fourth: opacities in learning an institutional/organizational culture supported by algorithmic systems.
- The CSD (criteria for speedy deletion) process follows a "four eyes" principle: an administrator may only delete an article after another Wikipedian has independently tagged it (e.g., with {{db-web}} or A7); anyone except the original creator may remove the tag without justification, and a majority of speedy-deleted articles are tagged with A7 rationales (Geiger and Ford, 2011).
- In a quantitative analysis cited, in 2011, 70% of articles nominated for speedy deletion were tagged within 10 minutes of being created, and 25% were deleted within 10 minutes of being created (Geiger, 2011b).
- Halfaker et al. (2013): of newcomers making high-quality contributions, 25–35% continued editing two months after their first contributions in 2003–2006, dropping to ~10% in 2007–2009 and 5% in 2010 after new tools/processes were introduced around 2007; by 2011 ~40% of such newcomers had at least some edits reverted by bots or semiautomated tools; the highly active editor community fell from a peak over 55,000/month in 2007 to a trough under 35,000/month in 2012.
- The saveMLAK vignette is timestamped minute-by-minute: article created at 9:43 am (9 August 2013, Wikimania Hong Kong), tagged for speedy deletion via Twinkle by 9:47 am, deleted by 9:48 am, and undeleted by an administrator at 9:51 am — three minutes after deletion — after the author posted to Twitter with the #Wikimania hashtag.
- Following Seaver (2013/2017), Geiger studies "algorithmic systems" (encoded procedures deployed in social-technical context) rather than "algorithms" in the abstract, and defines "algorithmic" as involving encoded procedures that are typically but not exclusively computationally implemented.
- Bots have existed almost from the start of Wikipedia's 15-year history, beginning with Ram-Bot in 2002, which nearly doubled Wikipedia's size by creating an article for every U.S. city and town from public-domain census data (Livingstone, 2016); the deletion process alone has been supported by 39 different bots over its 10 years.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper is explicitly autoethnographic and methodologically situated: it relies on two vignettes (one a "composite vignette," Note 1) from the author's own veteran experience, and Geiger frames generalizability cautiously — the vignettes are chosen because they "take place hundreds of times a month" and are "not abnormal outliers," but he extends Tkacz's (2015) claim that there is "no generalizable Wikiocracy," only the singularity of different organizational forms.
- Scope is the English-language Wikipedia; Geiger notes his argument about algorithmic systems in organizations has broader implications, but the empirical material is from one language version with a strong open-source ethos, which he treats as a "glimpse into a potential future world" rather than a typical platform.
- The author declares a conflict of interest: he was employed by the Wikimedia Foundation as a 2011 summer research intern and as a 2011–2012 research consultant, though he states he received no WMF funding or compensation for this specific project.
- The paper's central critique is reflexive about transparency discourse itself: Geiger argues that "opening up the black box" (open-sourcing code) is "not a cure-all," citing the paradox of openness (Tkacz, 2015) that fully exploiting released materials can require substantial time, expertise, and resources — code access "made little difference" in his vignettes versus organizational-cultural expertise.
