## Summary
This conceptual/review article asks how algorithmic management—the delegation of managerial functions to machine-learning algorithms—reshapes work when it spreads beyond the platform-mediated gig economy into "standard" work settings (stable, continuous, full-time employment with a unitary employer). The authors argue algorithmic management should be understood not as autonomous technology replacing humans but as a sociotechnical phenomenon emerging from the continuous interaction of managers, workers, and algorithms, and shaped by organizational choices about where and how to deploy it. They organize the analysis around three issues: (1) how algorithmic management reshapes power dynamics, simultaneously increasing managerial control over the workforce while decreasing the agency of individual (especially middle) managers; (2) how it demands new roles and "algorithmic competencies" while fostering oppositional attitudes such as algorithm aversion and cognitive complacency; and (3) how it affects knowledge and information exchange through opacity, which they split into technical opacity and organizational opacity. The paper concludes by situating algorithmic management in debates on the future of work and accountability, noting that gig-work findings do not transfer cleanly to standard settings, and proposes three future research directions (contextual/industry variation, the shift from rule-based to deep-learning AI, and worker-centered ethnographic/participatory research).

## Key facts it relies on
- The paper defines algorithmic management (a.k.a. "management-by-algorithm") as the delegation of managerial functions to algorithms (citing Lee, 2018; Lee et al., 2015; Noponen, 2019), and frames it as a sociotechnical concept reflecting both technological infrastructures and organizational choices.
- It distinguishes prior gig-economy research—where algorithms track performance, do job matching, generate rankings, and resolve disputes—from "standard work settings," and stresses gig findings are "not perfectly translatable" (e.g., Uber and Deliveroo were founded ex nihilo, without pre-existing management–workforce relationships).
- Power can increase for management as a whole: examples include automated scheduling in retail/service, Amazon warehouse algorithms that rate workers' speed and terminate those who fall behind (Dzieza, 2020), voice-analysis algorithms in call centers gauging empathy, GPS trucker monitoring (Levy, 2015), and Covid-19-era remote-monitoring tools like InterGuard capturing screenshots, login times, and keystrokes.
- Power can simultaneously decrease for individual managers: drawing on Shrestha et al. (2019), it names three manager–algorithm decision modes—full delegation, sequential decision-making, and aggregated decision-making—and warns that delegation can deprive managers of tacit/experiential knowledge (Lin et al., 2021 recruiter study).
- New roles are required: Wilson et al. (2017) identify "trainers, explainers, and sustainers," and Gal et al. (2020) propose "algorithmists" as human translators/mediators of algorithmic logic; the authors warn of a mix of upskilling and deskilling, leaving deskilled managers as an "appendage to the system" (Zuboff, 1988).
- Two oppositional attitudes are defined: algorithm aversion (reluctance to use algorithmic output after observing imperfect performance, even when algorithms may outperform humans; Dietvorst et al., 2016; Prahl and Van Swol, 2017) and cognitive complacency (uncritically deferring to algorithmic outputs, especially under high workload/time pressure; Logg et al., 2019; Newell and Marabelli, 2015).
- Opacity is split into technical opacity (the "black box" character of ML such as neural networks; the XAI subfield, fueled by the U.S. DARPA program, producing "local" vs "global" explanations, per Adadi and Berrada, 2018) and organizational opacity (strategic/IP concealment; standard firms often using third-party "AI-as-a-service," e.g., Microsoft 365 "productivity score," limiting their ability to "tweak" or understand algorithms). It cites Burrell's (2016) three forms of opacity: intentional concealment, lack of technical literacy, and algorithmic complexity/scale.
- It documents inherited bias: algorithms learn historical organizational biases (Keding, 2021); Lambrecht and Tucker (2019) found fewer women than men shown gender-neutral STEM-career ads, and Amazon scrapped an AI recruiting engine that built on 10 years of male-dominated resume data (The Guardian, 2018).
- Remedies for opacity discussed include algorithmic audits, the EU GDPR "Right to Explanation," "transparency by design" (Felzmann et al., 2020), and participatory AI design (Lee et al., 2019); the paper notes "agency laundering" (Rubel et al., 2019), where decision-makers deflect blame to automated systems, undermining algorithmic accountability.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors are explicit that this is a conceptual/theoretical synthesis, not new empirical work; they call for empirical, worker-centered ethnography and participatory action research to study how algorithmic management is experienced "on the front lines" in standard organizations.
- They flag a key scope limitation: most existing evidence comes from the platform gig economy, whose lack of pre-existing management systems and non-standard contracts limits transferability to standard settings—so much of the standard-setting discussion is extrapolated rather than directly evidenced.
- They note most prior algorithmic-management research concerns previous-generation (rule-based) AI and "may not fully reflect" the characteristics of emerging deep-learning systems, so current findings may understate or misrepresent newer technology's effects.
- They acknowledge their own normative tension: they argue both that workers can exercise agency/work-arounds and that algorithmic management is "usually top-down and imposed," with workers having "limited power to resist"; support correlates with managerial rank (Kolbjørnsrud et al., 2017: top managers relish AI, mid/front-line managers less so).
- The paper cautions against technological determinism (quoting Fleming, 2019, that socio-organizational forces and power, not technology, determine organizational design) and warns that isomorphic "keep up with rivals" pressures can drive adoption that is "neither welcomed nor necessary."
