## Summary
This is a systematic methodological review of how cognitive task analysis (CTA) methods --- an umbrella term for tools used to elicit, document and transfer the tacit knowledge experts use to make decisions --- have been applied to understand expert clinical decision-making in real-world health settings. The authors searched MEDLINE, EMBASE and PsycINFO from database inception to March 2019 (using only free-text terms, because the relevant MeSH heading was too non-specific), restricting to primary studies in which qualified clinicians made clinical decisions in naturalistic (non-simulated) settings. They included 81 articles representing 80 unique studies from 13 countries, published 1993--2019, most commonly from surgical and critical care settings and predominantly from the USA. The most common methods were CTA interviews and the critical decision method (CDM), with hierarchical task analysis, task knowledge structures, think-aloud and others used less often; most studies investigated routine/typical events. The review is purely descriptive (no risk-of-bias or outcome assessment) and concludes that CTA is increasingly used in specialties characterised by time pressure, complexity and uncertainty, and has under-recognised potential for developing, refining and adapting complex interventions, clinical protocols and practice guidelines.

## Key facts it relies on
- 81 articles representing 80 unique studies were included, from 13 countries, published from 1993 to 2019; searches covered database inception (MEDLINE 1966, EMBASE 1947, PsycINFO 1967) to 12 March 2019, run through Ovid.
- Screening flow: 1060 records from database searches plus 4 from other sources; after duplicate removal 1053 remained, 904 excluded at title/abstract, 149 assessed at full text, 68 excluded with reasons, leaving 81. The 68 exclusions included "not about decision-making" subtypes: procedure/task step description (n=22), non-clinical decisions (n=13), simulation setting (n=11), not clinicians making decisions (n=9), systematic/literature reviews (n=5), did not use CTA methods (n=4); plus 4 unretrievable copies.
- Country distribution: USA (n=48), UK (n=9), Canada (n=5), Australia (n=5), Slovakia (n=3), Ireland (n=2), Taiwan (n=2), and one each from Germany, Iran, France, Norway, Sweden, the Netherlands.
- Clinical settings: hospitals (n=60), university/medical schools (n=11), community (n=5), pre-hospital (n=3), military training command (n=1). Top specialties: surgery (n=30), critical/intensive care including neonatal/paediatric (n=11), anaesthesia/trauma (n=10), general medicine (n=7).
- Knowledge-elicitation methods: critical decision method (CDM) (n=36) or CTA interviews (n=30) were most common (both, n=1); less common were hierarchical task analysis (n=11), task knowledge structures (n=8), think-aloud (n=4), team knowledge audit (n=2), timeline analysis (n=2), SHERPA (n=2), concepts-processes-principles (n=2).
- Data-collection sources (most studies, n=42, used a combination): interviews (n=73), observation (n=27), think-aloud (n=10), surveys/questionnaires (n=6), focus groups (n=5), review of interview-generated protocols (n=4), SHERPA (n=3).
- Events studied were largely routine/typical (n=64), with fewer challenging (n=13) or uncommon/rare/anomalous (n=3); data targets were mostly past (n=41) or present (n=36), rarely future (n=2). The most common aim was understanding expert decision-making for a particular clinical scenario (n=35), followed by training/educational framework development (n=14).
- The review reports that, in verbal accounts, experts may leave out up to 70% of the components and decision steps needed to perform a task successfully (citing Sullivan et al.), which can yield incomplete or ineffective intervention protocols.
- CTA derives from the naturalistic decision-making paradigm; meta-analyses comparing CTA-based training with other methods are cited as showing large effects on procedural knowledge and technical performance. Registered on PROSPERO (CRD42019128418); reported per PRISMA.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors state the review was purely descriptive: it did not assess outcomes of included studies and did not assess risk of bias for individual studies or across studies, consistent with guidance on methodological reviews but acknowledged as a limitation some readers will object to.
- Scope was deliberately narrow --- only CTA used for expert clinical decision-making by qualified clinicians in naturalistic settings; studies focused on simulation, education/training evaluation, or novice decision-making were excluded, so the review undercounts CTA's broader use.
- The authors caution that the number of eligible studies is too small to rule out chance variation in the apparent rise-then-small-decline of CTA use over time (Fig. 2), and note that promising methods often fail to become embedded in routine practice.
- Four apparently eligible articles could not be retrieved; the authors judged from titles/abstracts that they replicated settings/applications already represented. They also took a "liberal view of expertise" (qualified vs. unqualified clinicians), noting people can over/under-estimate their own expertise (Dunning-Kruger, earned dogmatism).
- A practical critique raised: many hierarchical task analysis studies focused narrowly on task breakdown without extending to decision-making, and introducing CTA into practice may require cost-utility analysis because it is more intensive.
