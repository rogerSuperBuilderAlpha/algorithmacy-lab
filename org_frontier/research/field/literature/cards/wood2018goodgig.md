## Summary
NOTE: The acquired PDF at this path is not the Wood et al. (2018) journal article named in the citekey/title; it is the CIPD survey report "To gig or not to gig? Stories from the modern economy" (CIPD, March 2017). The body below is authored strictly from that PDF. The report asks whether gig-economy work is a viable, secure and meaningful alternative to traditional employment in the UK, and how much autonomy versus control gig workers experience. Method is a mixed design: a nationally representative online survey of 5,019 UK adults aged 18-70 (with the gig-worker subgroup boosted, n=417 gig workers), fielded 2-15 December 2016, plus follow-up qualitative telephone interviews with a proportion of gig workers in January-February 2017. Key findings: about 4% of working UK adults had done gig-economy work in the last 12 months; gigging is rarely the main income source (only ~25% call it their main job) and usually supplements other employment (58% of gig workers are permanent employees); hourly pay is low (median £6 for transport/delivery, £7-£7.70 for online tasks); yet self-reported satisfaction with income (51%) and with independence/autonomy (55% satisfied vs 10% dissatisfied) is relatively high. The report concludes gig work offers flexibility and freedom but also drawbacks (insufficient work, income insecurity), and that these problems are not confined to gig workers but also affect many traditional employees.

## Key facts it relies on
- Survey base: 5,019 UK adults aged 18-70, online, fieldwork 2-15 December 2016; weighted to be representative by social grade, region, gender, age and ethnicity; gig-worker subgroup boosted (n=417 gig workers; comparison "other workers" n=2,292). Gig workers defined as people who used an online platform at least once in the last 12 months for transport, vehicle/accommodation rental, food/goods delivery, or short-term online jobs.
- About 4% of working UK adults had done gig-economy work in the last 12 months; part-time workers under 8 hours/week were more likely (8%) than full-time workers (4%); 7% of full-time students and 6% of the unemployed had participated.
- Only ~25% of gig participants say gig work is their main job; ~58% of gig workers are permanent employees; gig workers are twice as likely to be self-employed (20% vs 10%) and more likely temporary employees (8% vs 3%) than other workers.
- Median hourly pay is low: £6 for those providing transport or delivering food/goods, £7 for short-term online jobs, £7.70 for "other" online platform work; 60% of gig participants say they don't get enough work on a regular basis (vs 26% who do).
- Despite low pay, 51% are satisfied/very satisfied with their gig income (highest among transport providers at 60%, lowest among food/goods deliverers at 39%); satisfaction is attributed to gig income usually being supplementary.
- Autonomy/control: 38% of gig workers say gig work makes them feel like their own boss (49% for transport/Uber-type drivers, only 31% for food/goods deliverers); 55% are satisfied with the independence, autonomy and control they experience at work vs 10% dissatisfied (deliverers least satisfied at 50%).
- Financial resilience is lower for gig workers: 47% could last only up to two months without falling behind on bills if they lost income (vs 42% of other workers); just 12% could last more than six months (vs 23%); yet 46% expect their economic situation to improve over the next 12 months (vs 26% of other workers).
- Awareness is low: only 20% of all UK adults (and 35% of gig workers) had heard of the term "gig economy"; 12% of non-participants are considering trying gig work in the next year.
- Box 2 cites the October 2016 UK employment tribunal ruling that Uber drivers are "workers" (not self-employed), based on Uber's level of control (recruiting drivers, requiring acceptance of trips, fixing fares, handling complaints, performance reviews via customer ratings); the reality of the relationship matters more than the written contract.

## Critical notes from the literature
- Citekey/PDF mismatch: the file is the CIPD (2017) practitioner survey report, not the peer-reviewed Wood, Graham, Lehdonvirta & Hjorth (2018) WES article. The CIPD report focuses on UK platform work informing the Taylor Review; the actual WES paper studies remote gig workers across Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa. Any citation to "Good Gig, Bad Gig" should not be supported by figures from this PDF.
- The report flags that earnings data "should perhaps be treated with some caution," because respondents may calculate hourly pay differently (including/excluding administration time or expenses).
- Self-selection / definitional issues: the report opens by noting the gig economy is "hard to define, hard to measure and hard to interpret," with surveys notable for "their lack of comparability"; the gig-worker incidence is low so the subgroup is boosted, and highly marginal occasional gig activity may be under-reported.
- The high self-reported autonomy/income satisfaction is largely an artifact of supplementary use: most gig workers have another job, so the report cautions findings would differ for those dependent on gig work as their main income (echoed in case studies, e.g. Deliveroo riders monitored and auto-logged-out for declining jobs).
- Subjective vs objective security diverge: gig workers rate themselves as objectively less well-off (lower resilience, less employer-pension coverage at 30% vs 57%) yet report more confidence/optimism about the future, so a single satisfaction metric can mislead.
