## Summary
This foundational methodological monograph argues that sociology has overemphasized the verification of theory at the expense of the prior, equally important task of generating theory from data — what the authors term "grounded theory." Their central question is how the discovery of theory from data systematically obtained and analyzed in social research can be furthered, in order to close the long-standing "gap between theory and empirical research." The proposed method is a general method of comparative analysis: the systematic choice and study of several comparison groups to generate theory inductively, in contrast to theory generated by logical deduction from a priori assumptions. The authors claim grounded theory is superior because it "fits" the situation researched (categories are readily, not forcibly, applicable to the data) and "works" (it explains the behavior under study, providing prediction, explanation, interpretation, and application). They contrast grounded theory with "grand"/logico-deductive theorizing and criticize opportunistic uses of ungrounded theory such as "tacked-on" explanations and "exampling." They also reject the prevailing view that qualitative data is merely exploratory/preliminary to quantitative verification, arguing the qualitative-versus-quantitative clash is really about the primacy of generation versus verification, and that both data types can serve both purposes. ## Key facts it relies on
- "Grounded theory" is defined as the discovery of theory from data systematically obtained and analyzed in social research; the authors call its discovery "a major task confronting sociology today."
- The two criteria for a good theory are "fit" and "work": by "fit" they mean categories "must be readily (not forcibly) applicable to and indicated by the data under study," and by "work" they mean categories "must be meaningfully relevant to and be able to explain the behavior under study."
- Five interrelated jobs of theory in sociology are enumerated: (1) enable prediction and explanation of behavior; (2) be useful in theoretical advance in sociology; (3) be usable in practical applications; (4) provide a perspective on behavior; (5) guide and provide a style for research on particular areas of behavior.
- The proposed strategy is "a general method of comparative analysis" involving "the systematic choice and study of several comparison groups" to generate theory that is both "substantive" and "formal."
- The book is structured in three parts: Part I (Generating Theory by Comparative Analysis, Chapters II-VI, covering generating theory, theoretical sampling, substantive-to-formal theory, the constant comparative method, and assessing comparative studies); Part II (The Flexible Use of Data, Chapters VII-VIII, qualitative/documentary and quantitative data); Part III (Implications of Grounded Theory, Chapters IX-XI, credibility, application, and insight/theory development).
- The authors were trained respectively at Columbia (associated with Merton's middle-range theory and Lazarsfeld's quantitative methodology) and Chicago (the "Chicago tradition" of qualitative research); they argue neither tradition closed the theory-research gap, which was "as wide today as it was in 1941, when Blumer commented on it, and in 1949, when Merton optimistically suggested a solution."
- The work was funded by Public Health Service Research Grant NU-00047 from the Division of Nursing, and grew out of related studies including Glaser and Strauss's "Awareness of Dying" (1965) and Quint's "The Nurse and the Dying Patient" (1967).
- They critique two opportunistic uses of ungrounded theory: the "tacked-on" explanation (e.g., deviance papers concluding with an interpretation based on Merton's anomie theory) and "exampling" (finding confirming examples for a dreamed-up or logically deduced idea after the fact, yielding "the image of a proof when there is none").
- On qualitative versus quantitative data, they argue "there is no fundamental clash between the purposes and capacities of qualitative and quantitative methods or data"; the real clash "concerns the primacy of emphasis on verification or generation of theory," and "each form of data is useful for both verification and generation of theory."

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly frame the book as "a beginning venture": they state they "shall often state positions, counter-positions and examples, rather than offering clear-cut procedures and definitions, because at many points we believe our slight knowledge makes any formulation premature."
- They acknowledge their position "is not logical; it is phenomenological," and that for many colleagues it "will be at best a hypothesis, to be tested in the years to come" — i.e., the superiority claim for grounded over logico-deductively-derived theory is asserted as belief, not demonstrated.
- They concede the researcher "does not approach reality as a tabula rasa" and "must have a perspective that will help him see relevant data and abstract significant categories," qualifying the inductive purity of the method (a tension later central to debates over the approach).
- The book is openly polemical: the authors note their suggestions are "deliberately interspersed with occasional frank polemic" against the "verification rhetoric" and "grand theory," and that colleagues felt "in some controversy" on several issues the authors expect to remain "controversial in sociology for years to come."
