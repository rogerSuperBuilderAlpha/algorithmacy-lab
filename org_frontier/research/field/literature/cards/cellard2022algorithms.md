## Summary
This conceptual/methodological article in critical algorithm studies asks what the notion "algorithm" actually means and how it travels across contexts. Cellard argues that rather than fixing a normative definition, the algorithm is better understood as a "figure": a discursive short-hand pointing to diverse modes of procedural governance that are not always digital. Drawing on STS (especially Ziewitz's question of treating algorithms as figures rather than techno-scientific artifacts) and a "post-digital" sensibility from media theory (Cramer, Berry), he uses an ethnography of algorithmic regulation in French public-sector organisations to show how regulators and judges "rebranded" administrative procedures as "algorithms." Fieldwork was conducted between March and October 2018 at Etalab, the French open-data task force attached to the Prime Minister, plus meetings with regulatory bodies and impacted citizens, tracing effects of the Lemaire Bill of 2016 and its new right to explanation of algorithmic decision-making. The paper's main contribution is a program of four ethnographic strategies for describing the production and circulation of algorithmic figures: observing the observers of algorithms; mapping the creative provocation of (figuring) algorithms; drawing relations across algorithmic contexts; and analysing the effects of algorithmic figurations. The core finding is that the legal/regulatory device (the right to explanation, FOI requests) performatively brings "algorithms" into being and shows that computer science no longer holds a monopoly on the ontology and epistemology of algorithms.

## Key facts it relies on
- The empirical study was conducted between March and October 2018 at Etalab, the French open-data task force attached to the French Prime Minister, plus meetings with regulatory bodies and citizens impacted by algorithmic decision-making.
- Etalab initiated the Lemaire Bill of 2016, which led to a new right to explanation of algorithmic decision-making; this device "rebranded many bureaucratic procedures as procedural 'algorithms'."
- The French regulator CADA (Commission of Access to Administrative Documents), responding to FOI requests, qualified as "algorithms": a decision tree coordinating firefighters/ambulances, the calculus of pensions for independent workers, and a paper-based grid of criteria used to score secondary-school students seeking entry to a special high school — i.e., not all digital.
- The paper proposes four ethnographic strategies: (1) observing the observers of algorithms; (2) mapping the creative provocation of algorithmic figures; (3) drawing relations across algorithmic contexts; (4) analysing the effects of algorithmic figurations.
- It cites the standard CS definition (Cormen et al., 2009: 5): "An algorithm is any well-defined computational procedure that takes some value, or set of values, as input and produces some value, or set of values, as output," and traces the modern definition to the 1970s structured-programming movement (Burke, 2019).
- It draws on Dourish (2004) to contrast four CS assumptions about algorithms (code/symbols; delineable; stable/separable; entity acting "in" a context) against four STS/social-science assumptions (relational; borders unknown in advance; situated/contingent; generated by a specific context of production).
- It uses Selbst et al. (2019)'s five "traps" of fair-ML contextualisation: framing, portability, formalism, ripple effect, and solutionism.
- The running empirical example is the French housing tax figured as an "algorithm" — a complex figure composed of tenants, tax letters, landlords' declarations, regional fiscal legislation, parameters set by Parliament, and technologies of calculation.
- It builds on Ziewitz (2017): "What would it take to understand algorithms not as techno-scientific artifacts, but as a figure...?" and Seaver (2017)'s tactics for an ethnography of algorithmic systems.
- "Post-digital" is framed (after Cramer) as an analytical attention, not a period — the distinction between digital and non-digital becomes less clear and less useful than it seems.

## Critical notes from the literature
- This is a single-site, single-country (France), single-period (2018) ethnographic/conceptual intervention; the author explicitly notes that providing a general picture of the broader semantic shift "will require more research and not necessarily ethnographic ones," and points to Yu (2021) for a historical account.
- The paper acknowledges a core tension (via Muniesa, 2011): whether a "trial in explicitness"/figuring merely clarifies an already-existing stable algorithm or performatively generates the coming-into-being of the algorithm — the two readings are not resolved.
- Drawing on Strathern (2002) and Amoore (2020), the author concedes that mapping the "decontextualization contexts" of algorithms is possibly endless and could be seen as a limit of every ethnographic inquiry (the "nonclosure" and "excess" of algorithmic contexts).
- The author anticipates the objection that treating algorithms as "figures" rather than digital objects may seem counter-intuitive or contradictory to actors who see algorithms only as digital, and locates resistance to it in critics' "loyalty" to computer science's claimed monopoly on the ontology of digital objects.
- The position is offered as complementary to, not a replacement for, ethnographies focused on engineers/data scientists (Seaver), language translation limits (Sack), or cross-sector comparison (Christin) — it does not claim algorithms are "just texts" or lack material reality.
