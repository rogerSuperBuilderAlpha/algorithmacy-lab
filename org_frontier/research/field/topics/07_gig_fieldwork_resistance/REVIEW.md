# Gig fieldwork: control and resistance

Empirical fieldwork on ride-hail, delivery, and remote gig labor converges on a paradox: platforms displace the human supervisor with algorithmically mediated, customer-sourced control, yet this very arrangement opens seams that workers learn to exploit. Foundational interview-and-survey work across Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa established that algorithmic management couples flexibility with low pay, isolation, overwork, and exhaustion (Wood et al., 2018). A comparative ethnography of two large platforms reframed control and resistance as co-constituted across the labor process, showing that worker latitude is widest before a task and narrows at each subsequent stage, while everyday worker-customer interactions can quietly manipulate the ratings algorithms depend on (Cameron and Rahman, 2022).

Much of the field reads gig resistance through James Scott's "hidden transcripts" and "weapons of the weak." Studies of remote African platform workers recast resistance, resilience, and reworking as everyday agency under algorithmic surveillance (Anwar and Graham, 2020). Ride-hail ethnography in Lagos documents sabotage, false compliance, and gaming of reward zones spread through driver social-media networks (Arubayi, 2021); Uber drivers in Dhaka deploy off-platform khep rides to subvert allocation and supplement income (Lata, 2025). Migrant food-delivery riders in China enact a "dodge–leverage–subvert" repertoire locked in a tug-of-war with iteratively retuned algorithms (Huang, 2025), while Indonesian couriers contest platform gamification through both infrapolitics and collective "gamification from below" (Novianto, 2024).

Delivery fieldwork foregrounds embodiment and mobility. Deliveroo couriers in London and Manchester negotiate platform "smartness" through entrepreneurialism and solidarity (Popan, 2021); Parisian riders recode degrading work as labor respectability, with some routing grievances into Yellow Vests protest (Rubert, 2023). German riders turn app-based tracking itself into an arena for counter-surveillance and supply-chain disruption (Randerath et al., 2024). At Uber, accounting fieldwork frames algorithmic control of demand as disciplinary self-formation that workers endure, subvert, or exit (McDaid et al., 2023).

A complementary strand reads control as metric anxiety and surveillance. Cross-platform interviews show metrics function as affective measures saturating workers' anticipatory practices (Chan, 2022); app-based workers move along a "sousveillance spectrum," watching the watchers (Mitson et al., 2024), motivating co-designed worker sousveillance tools (Do et al., 2024). Interviews across ride-hail, grocery, and delivery further show platforms expose workers to customer malfeasance while inviting differentiated normative orientations toward risk (Schor and Vallas, 2023). Across settings, resistance is real, adaptive, and routinely outpaced by algorithmic countermoves.
