# Inductive coding of coordination rules

Abstracting determination rules from interviews, observation, and documents sits at the intersection of grounded-theory coding pipelines and institutional analysis. The grounded-theory tradition supplies the core machinery: open, axial, and selective coding that moves data fragments toward categories and a core construct, with theoretical sampling and constant comparison governing when the analyst stops (Cascio et al., 2019). The Gioia template formalized this for organizational settings, tracing first-order informant concepts into second-order themes and aggregate dimensions through a transparent data structure that makes the abstraction auditable (Gioia et al., 2013). Hybrid designs blend such inductive engagement with deductive templates derived from prior theory, supporting analysis of interview transcripts alongside organizational documents (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006).

A persistent tension concerns whether rules can be said to "emerge." Abductive accounts argue that induction alone does not yield novel determinations and that surprising evidence drives theory construction through defamiliarization and alternative casing (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012); operationalized abductive coding adds codebooks and code equations that combine codes to capture phenomena spanning individual labels (Vila-Henninger et al., 2024). For large, team-coded interview corpora, flexible coding leverages qualitative-data-analysis software to index and retrieve segments, departing from artisanal grounded theory while preserving inductive intent (Deterding & Waters, 2021).

When the object of coding is explicitly a rule, norm, or strategy, the Institutional Grammar provides a syntactic scaffold. The ADICO/ABDICO components (Attribute, Deontic, aIm, Conditions, Or-else) decompose institutional statements drawn from legislation, regulations, and meeting records into coordination-relevant primitives (Basurto et al., 2010). Institutional Grammar 2.0 extends this specification with tiered expressiveness for encoding institutional design (Frantz & Siddiki, 2021), and a review of 51 empirical applications documents how coders operationalize discretion, compliance, and polycentric structure from text (Pieper et al., 2023). This line offers a rule-typed alternative to open coding when the analytic goal is the determination logic of who must do what under which conditions.

Computational and generative methods now augment these pipelines. Computational grounded theory interleaves unsupervised pattern detection, interpretive refinement, and confirmation to surface candidate rules at scale (Nelson, 2020). Generative models can replicate hand-coding output and approximate inductive workflows through natural-language prompting (Than et al., 2025), though inductive thematic analysis with LLMs recovers main themes only partially and demands human verification (De Paoli, 2024). The convergence suggests rule abstraction as a human-led, machine-assisted pipeline where provenance and inter-coder consensus remain load-bearing.
