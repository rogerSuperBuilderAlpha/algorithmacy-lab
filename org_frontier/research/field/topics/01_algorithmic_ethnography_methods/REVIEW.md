# Ethnographic methods for opaque algorithms

Fieldwork on algorithmic systems confronts a recurring obstacle: the object of study is proprietary, dynamic, and inaccessible at the level of code. A foundational response reframes the problem so that opacity becomes data rather than a barrier. Seaver (2017) argues that algorithmic systems should be enacted ethnographically as heterogeneous sociotechnical assemblages, treating "algorithms as culture" and approaching access as a texture of resistance the "scavenging" ethnographer documents. This as-culture stance refuses the demand to open a black box and instead studies the practices, discourses, and material arrangements through which systems are made and lived (Seaver, 2019). Kitchin (2017) catalogues the practical difficulties—opacity, heterogeneity, ontogenetic instability—and surveys complementary tactics including reverse engineering, code inspection, and interviewing developers.

The most cited methodological vocabulary for the topic comes from Christin (2020), who argues that ethnographers can enroll opaque algorithms as research instruments through three meso-level strategies. Algorithmic refraction examines the reconfigurations produced when software, people, and institutions interact; algorithmic comparison uses similarity-and-difference across sites to isolate a system's distinctive features; algorithmic triangulation deploys the algorithm itself to elicit qualitative data. Christin (2020b) extends these tactics to remote and pandemic-constrained fieldwork.

A parallel tradition studies how non-experts theorize systems they cannot inspect. Bucher (2017) develops the "algorithmic imaginary"—the affectively charged ways users picture algorithms—as both an analytic object and a generative force shaping the systems themselves. Ytre-Arne and Moe (2021) map five folk theories that organize everyday algorithmic irritation, and DeVito (2022) shows transfeminine TikTok creators using folk theorization to navigate visibility under opacity, a method-as-practice that ethnographers can observe and elicit.

Structured techniques supply complementary purchase. The walkthrough method (Light, Burgess and Duguay, 2018) steps systematically through an app's interface and "environment of expected use," turning a closed system into observable, recordable encounters. Cellard (2022) reframes the algorithm as a discursive "figure" and proposes observing the observers, mapping figures, and tracing their governance effects. Recent work consolidates principles for ethnography of AI more broadly, emphasizing committed fieldwork, trust, and attentiveness to absent-present data (van Voorst and Ahlin, 2024), while Savolainen and Ruckenstein (2024) demonstrate refraction and triangulation in practice by analyzing dimensions of autonomy across human-algorithm relations.

Across these strands a shared commitment emerges: opacity is approached as a relational and cultural condition to be characterized through comparison, refraction, triangulation, and attention to lived theorization, situating the algorithm within the practices that enact it.
