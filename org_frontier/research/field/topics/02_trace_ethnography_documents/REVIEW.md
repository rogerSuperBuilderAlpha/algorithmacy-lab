# Trace ethnography and documentary coordination

Trace ethnography names a methodology for reconstructing distributed coordination from the documentary residue of sociotechnical systems: transaction logs, version histories, institutional records, conversation transcripts, and source code (Geiger & Ribes, 2011). Its premise is that participants in networked settings do not merely leave traces but actively use them to coordinate and to render each other's actions accountable, so that a thin log entry can be decoded back into a "thick" account of who did what under which mediating rule. The foundational demonstration follows a single Wikipedia vandal's removal across edit timestamps, bot reverts, warning templates, and noticeboard filings, showing that an apparently spontaneous ban is in fact an assemblage of human and algorithmic actors executing prescribed sequences (Geiger & Ribes, 2010).

The approach draws on older accounts of how inscriptions hold coordination together. Ethnomethodology's documentary method treats each appearance as standing for a presupposed underlying pattern that members continually work to sustain (Schüttpelz, 2019), while infrastructure studies frame traces as relational artifacts legible only within installed bases and classification regimes (Star & Ruhleder, 1996). These lineages position the log as a determination rule made durable: a record that both encodes and enforces what counts as a legitimate next move.

A methodological strand treats traces as the analytic object itself. Ribes (2014) reads "scalar devices" off institutional records to show how actors fit a national infrastructure into a single room, and Ekström (2021) couples trace data with visualization to study information practices around systems. Applied to opaque algorithmic governance, the method warns against reading quantitative trace counts at face value: bot-versus-bot reverts that look like conflict resolve, on ethnographic inspection, into routine maintenance (Geiger & Halfaker, 2017; Geiger, 2017).

Recent work extends documentary reconstruction to forensic and audit registers. Hodges (2021) reconstructs invisible biomedical repair labor from PDF metadata in pandemic-era manual archives, and Marsh (2016) follows a museum object through distributed records to surface affect and institutional ecology. Platform-labor studies pair logs with interviews and "go-alongs" to show how documentary infrastructures intensify teachers' work (Moraiti, Bergviken Rensfeldt, & Lundin, 2024). Most recently, an audit-plus-trace-ethnography of the LAION-Aesthetics predictor reconstructs how an opaque scoring model encodes contestable aesthetic determinations into training-data curation (Taylor, Agnew, Sap, Fox, & Zhu, 2026). Across these cases the through-line holds: institutional records function as evidence of mediated determination rules, and reconstructing coordination means recovering the rule each trace enacted.
