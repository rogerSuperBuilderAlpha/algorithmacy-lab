# Algorithmic control mechanisms on platforms

Research on algorithmic control treats the platform as an opaque, interested third party that coordinates and disciplines workers it does not formally employ. The organizing framework comes from Kellogg, Valentine & Christin (2020), who recast algorithms as a "new contested terrain of control" and identify six mechanisms (the 6 Rs): restricting and recommending to direct work, recording and rating to evaluate it, and replacing and rewarding to discipline it. This typology consolidated a prior wave of platform studies. Rosenblat & Stark (2016) documented how Uber's information asymmetries and soft "nudges" structure indirect control while preserving a rhetoric of driver autonomy, and Wood et al. (2018) showed across six countries that the same control techniques yield both flexibility and overwork, low pay, and exhaustion. Griesbach et al. (2019) added cross-platform variation, naming Instacart's stringent regime "algorithmic despotism."

Two analytic distinctions recur. Möhlmann et al. (2021) separate matching from control, arguing that platforms deploy algorithms along both dimensions and that the tension between them generates worker grievances over compensation and belonging. Wiener et al. (2021) and the broader information-systems literature distinguish gatekeeping (restricting access) from guiding control, finding that workers judge guiding control as comparatively legitimate while gatekeeping erodes perceived autonomy and fairness. Parent-Rocheleau & Parent-Cabana (2022) translate the mechanisms into six management functions — monitoring, goal setting, performance management, scheduling, compensation, and termination — and link each to job resources and demands.

Opacity is a defining property of the recording and rating mechanisms. Rahman (2021) shows that when evaluation criteria are unknown and shifting, freelancers face an "invisible cage": they cannot align behaviour with the rating system, so some over-experiment and others withdraw. Control does not depend on coercion alone. Cameron (2024) finds that constant, confined choices manufacture consent, producing a "good bad" job in which workers experience the algorithm as a source of agency. Vallas et al. (2022) document a parallel mix of techno-economic despotism and consent-generating mechanisms in Amazon warehouses, extending the framework beyond gig platforms into standard employment.

Recent work consolidates these strands. Duggan et al. (2023) theorize "algorithmic HRM control," stressing the influence of non-organizational parties such as customers whose ratings feed disciplinary systems. Zhang et al. (2025) synthesize five framings of algorithmic management — surveillance, neutral tool, agentic boss, sociotechnical process, and contradictory unity — and foreground tensions between control and autonomy, transparency and opacity, efficiency and fairness. Across this literature, the platform's interestedness and informational opacity remain the conditions that make algorithmic direction, evaluation, and discipline effective.
