# Qualitative research — literature review

Search method: structured queries across WebSearch, WebFetch, the Consensus academic tool, and the Scholar Gateway semantic search corpus, with DOIs and titles confirmed against publisher and indexing records. Run date 2026-06-23.

The qualitative program of algorithmacy-lab draws on two literatures that have matured independently and now intersect: methodological standards for interpretive organizational research, and empirical studies of how workers experience opaque algorithmic systems. The methodological foundation rests on Lincoln and Guba's (1985) naturalistic paradigm, which replaced positivist validity and reliability with credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability, supported by thick description, member checking, audit trails, and reflexivity. Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton (2013) operationalized comparable standards for inductive theory building, separating informant-grounded first-order concepts from researcher-derived second-order themes and aggregate dimensions in a transparent data structure. These two frameworks supply the warrant for treating interview and ethnographic material as evidence about coordination rather than anecdote.

Sensemaking theory supplies the interpretive engine. Weick (1995) framed sensemaking as the retrospective, social, enactive process through which people convert equivocal cues into plausible accounts that guide action. Weick's (1993) Mann Gulch analysis showed how role structures and shared meaning can collapse together under ambiguity, and Weick, Sutcliffe, and Obstfeld (2005) consolidated the perspective around the movement from action to meaning. Sandberg and Tsoukas (2020) reviewed a half-century of this work, and Brown (2025) and Holt and Cornelissen synthesized its constituents and limits, giving the program a current map of where sensemaking theory stands and where it remains contested.

The applied core concerns workers facing algorithmic third parties they cannot inspect. Rosenblat and Stark (2016) documented the information and power asymmetries Uber's app imposes on drivers, and Möhlmann and Zalmanson (2017) detailed how drivers guess, resist, switch, and game an opaque system to recover autonomy. Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin (2020) organized employer mechanisms into the "6 Rs" of algorithmic control and framed the workplace as contested terrain. Christin (2020) addressed the methodological problem directly, proposing refraction, comparison, and triangulation as strategies for studying systems whose internals are hidden, a problem that overlaps with the lab's interest in opaque, interested intermediaries.

Recent peer-reviewed work extends these themes with fine-grained accounts of worker experience. Bucher and colleagues describe algorithmic paranoia as gig workers' affective response to abusive, illegible management (Bucher et al., 2024). Studies of folk theories and the social construction of algorithms show workers building actionable theories of systems they cannot see (Vassilakopoulou et al., 2024; Anwar et al., 2025). Resistance is documented as gaming, tactical workarounds, out-of-app activity, and a typology of labour agency (Cameron, 2024; Sun et al., 2023; Wood et al., 2025). The duality of algorithmic management, its simultaneous control and empowerment effects, now anchors several research agendas (Meijerink and Bondarouk, 2025; Cai et al., 2025), and attribution-based accounts examine when workers read the same system as fair or unfair (Zhang et al., 2025). Perceived algorithmic fairness has been tied empirically to transparency and anthropomorphism (Shulner-Tal et al., 2024), and contestation rights under data protection law connect worker sensemaking to governance (Lyons et al., 2021).

Disagreement-as-data sits at the methodological frontier. The reflexive thematic analysis tradition treats coder divergence as interpretive signal and challenges intercoder reliability as an inappropriate criterion for interpretivist work, while codebook and coding-reliability variants retain agreement metrics for team consistency. This debate matters for a program that intends to use disagreement between informants, and between coders, as evidence about how opaque systems are read differently by differently positioned actors.

Three through-lines define the program. Opacity is treated as a property actors cope with through theory-building and sensemaking, not merely a technical defect. Worker accounts are treated as rigorous evidence when handled under Gioia and Lincoln-Guba standards. Divergence in those accounts is treated as data about the coordination an interested intermediary produces.
