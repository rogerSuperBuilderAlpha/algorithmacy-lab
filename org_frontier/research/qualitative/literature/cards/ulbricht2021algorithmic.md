## Summary
This is the editors' introductory essay to a Special Issue of Regulation & Governance, offering a critical synthesis of its contributions in order to assess the concept of "algorithmic regulation" as articulated by Yeung in 2017 (sustained, intentional attempts to employ algorithmic decisionmaking, "ADM," to influence behavior or manage risk). The method is conceptual/synthetic: the authors review changes in public and scholarly debate about algorithms over the five years since Yeung's 2016 manuscript submission, then group the Special Issue contributions into three sets and read across them. The three sets (i) extend and refine Yeung's concept and taxonomy, (ii) interrogate complementary concepts such as "data jurisdictions" and "modes of governance," and (iii) address the regulation of algorithms and the quest for "algorithmic accountability." The authors argue the core concept is valuable and maturing: it has evolved into an "analytical bridge" that fosters cross-disciplinary, context-sensitive analysis, enriching its early "skeletal"/"thin" form with disciplinary "flesh." They also surface limitations: the concept's purposive definition struggles with cases lacking clear pre-specified aims or decisions (e.g., data-visualization platforms that are better called "algorithmic surveillance"), and its decisionmaking orientation downplays the data supply chain / "one half" of the computer's ontology. The essay deliberately exploits the interpretive ambiguity of "algorithmic regulation" to cover both regulation by/through algorithms and regulation of algorithms.

## Key facts it relies on
- The anchoring definition: algorithmic regulation entails "intentional attempts to manage risk or alter behavior in order to achieve some pre-specified goal" (Yeung 2017, p. 508), based on Julia Black's work (Black 2014) treating regulation as a subset of governance characterized by a triumvirate of cybernetic functions: (i) standard-setting, (ii) information-gathering/monitoring, and (iii) intervention/sanctioning.
- The Special Issue originated from a four-day Volkswagen Foundation "hot-housing" retreat in October 2017, followed by panels at the seventh Biennial Conference of the ECPR Standing Group on Regulatory Governance in Lausanne, July 2018.
- The authors highlight three contextual changes since Yeung's August 2016 submission: (i) the explosion/diversification of scholarship on algorithms and data; (ii) increasing public-sector take-up of algorithmic regulation, accelerated by COVID-19 (e.g., contact-tracing/self-diagnosis apps); and (iii) growing anxiety about hazards and calls for meaningful limits.
- The choice of "regulation" over "governance" is justified two ways: fidelity to Yeung (2017), and the observation that computer-and-data-driven ordering tends to do "rowing" rather than merely "steering" (invoking Osborne & Gaebler 1992's government-to-governance distinction).
- Eyert, Irgmaier & Ulbricht (2020) relabel Yeung's three components as representation ("information-gathering"), direction ("standard-setting"), and intervention ("behavior-modification"), and apply the framework to Uber's algorithmic governance of drivers.
- Bellanova & de Goede (2020) take an "infrastructural perspective" on the PNR system and the TFTP (fed by SWIFT data), identifying three infrastructure dimensions (data structuring, architectural constraints, interfaces) and positioning "risk flagging" between Yeung's two enforcement categories (recommendations vs. fully automated sanctions).
- Johns & Compton (2019) propose "data jurisdictions" (market; public science; humanitarianism) via the UN's Haze Gazer and CycloMon visualization tools, which the editors note fall outside Yeung's conception because they make no decisions; Gritsenko & Wood (2020) place algorithmic governance within the "modes of governance" typology (hierarchy/bureaucracy, markets, networks) using cases of speeding, disinformation, and social sharing.
- The accountability-focused papers draw on: principal-agent theory for risk-based ADM regulation (Krafft et al. 2020); private certification systems (Matus & Veale 2020); the GDPR (in force 2016) including a regulatory-governance "demystification" (Yeung & Bygrave 2021), the Article 22 "right to contest" (Bayamlioglu 2020), information theory distinguishing data protection as a "knowledge-communication"/information technology from machine learning as a "knowledge-production"/knowledge technology (Gellert 2020), ECtHR/ECHR human-rights agency and "algorithmic groups" (Kosta 2020), and "individualized justice" (Binns 2020).
- The EU's proposed AI Regulation, released as the Special Issue went to press, is described as adopting a "fairly blunt form of risk stratification," more proportionate than the preceding White Paper but less nuanced than the approach of Krafft, Koenig & Zweig (this volume).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors themselves flag that Yeung's purposive definition is strained by socio-technical systems with "uncertain and malleable goals" (Bellanova & de Goede) and by platforms (Haze Gazer, CycloMon) that make no "decision" and offer no recommendation, which they argue are better described as "algorithmic surveillance" than algorithmic regulation.
- They acknowledge a structural limitation: the concept's orientation toward decisionmaking downplays the larger socio-technical ecosystem and data supply chains, opening up "only one half of the computer's ontological world" (data structures + algorithms) and taking data inputs largely as given; data ownership/governance thus lies largely outside its reach.
- The concept is repeatedly characterized as a "skeleton" / "thin" framework whose generality is both a strength (flexibility across domains) and a shortcoming, since "thicker" conceptual flesh may be a necessary precondition for deep contextual analysis.
- The essay is an editors' synthesis of a curated, non-representative set of contributions ("not representative of all research fields and topics"), so its claims are interpretive rather than the product of systematic empirical study.
- The authors note (via Yeung & Bygrave 2021) a paucity of literature critically investigating the relationship between rights and regulation, and that the highly individualized orientation of rights-based claims may fail to protect collective goods threatened by aggregate algorithmic use.
