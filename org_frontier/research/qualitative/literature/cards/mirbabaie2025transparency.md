## Summary
The paper asks how algorithmic transparency affects the perceived fairness of algorithmic management (AM) on digital labor platforms (DLPs). Grounded in organizational justice theory (OJT), which distinguishes distributive, procedural, and informational justice, the authors ran a 4×1 between-subjects online experiment with 234 DLP workers recruited via Prolific on a self-developed, no-code (Bubble.io) study-recruitment platform where a simulated algorithm (Wizard-of-Oz) allocated tasks. Participants were split into four groups: no transparency (control), distributive transparency, informational transparency, and both combined. The main result is a "transparency fallacy": providing transparency generally did not raise perceived fairness — only distributive transparency produced a small-to-moderate, significant increase in perceived distributive fairness (H1a supported), while informational transparency (H1b) and overall/combined transparency (H1c) showed no significant effect. Affinity for technology and trust in AM did not moderate the transparency–fairness relationship (all H2 and H3 rejected), but both were significant correlated predictors of perceived fairness independent of transparency level. Qualitative responses indicated workers largely accepted the algorithm as "unbiased and objective" and trusted it, and even the no-transparency group perceived allocation as fair because they had no basis to question it.

## Key facts it relies on
- Design: 4×1 between-subjects online experiment; four conditions (no transparency / distributive / informational / both); data collected in one day (September 9, 2024) via Prolific, which the authors argue itself qualifies as a DLP (Dunn et al. 2023).
- Sample: 236 took part, 2 excluded for failing attention checks → 234 valid cases (Group 1 n=60, Group 2 n=56, Group 3 n=58, Group 4 n=60); ages 18–72 (M=30.8, SD=9.70); 46.58% women, 51.71% men, 1.71% diverse; 75.65% held a university degree; 82.91% employed; 52.99% worked daily as digital workers. G*Power indicated a required total of 280; mean completion time 21:44 min.
- A separate manipulation-check prestudy (120 acquired, 1 excluded → N=119) confirmed the four conditions differed in perceived transparency: control M=4.2, distributive M=6.0, informational M=5.0, both M=5.9; Kruskal–Wallis F=106.84, p=0.000; all three treatment vs control Mann–Whitney comparisons significant (p<0.000).
- H1a supported: distributive fairness differed across groups (Kruskal–Wallis F=13.708, p=0.003). Pairwise: Group 1 (M=3.48) vs Group 2 (M=3.85, p=0.027), Group 2 vs Group 3 (M=3.44, p=0.007), Group 1 vs Group 4 (M=3.90, p=0.014). Effect sizes Cohen's d=−0.39 (G1–G2) and d=−0.45 (G1–G4); G2 vs G4 not significant (p=0.872).
- H1b not supported: informational fairness did not differ across groups (Kruskal–Wallis F=6.880, p=0.076); G1 (M=3.17) vs G3 (M=3.19) not significant (p=0.996). However G1 vs G4 (M=3.61) was significant (p=0.029, d=−0.43).
- H1c not supported: combined/overall perceived fairness did not differ across groups (Kruskal–Wallis F=0.621, p=0.891).
- All moderation hypotheses rejected: affinity for technology did not moderate transparency–fairness (p=0.842; distributive p=0.359; informational p=0.284 — H2a/b/c); trust in AM did not moderate (p=0.520; distributive p=0.190; informational p=0.818 — H3a/b/c); the affinity×trust interaction was also non-significant (perceived p=0.191, distributive p=0.156, informational p=0.578).
- Exploratory Spearman correlations (independent of transparency): trust in AM with perceived fairness ρ=0.566 (p<0.001) and affinity ρ=0.311; trust ρ=0.413 and affinity ρ=0.373 with informational fairness; trust ρ=0.268 and affinity ρ=0.225 with distributive fairness.
- Measures/scales (Table 2): Affinity for Technology Interaction (ATI; Franke et al. 2017; α=0.90, 6-point); Human–Computer Trust Scale (Gulati et al. 2019; α=0.83–0.88, 7-point); Perceived fairness and Perceived transparency (Höddinghaus et al. 2021; α=0.96 and 0.82, 7-point); Distributive and Informational fairness (Colquitt 2001; α=0.93 and 0.90, 5-point). Methods used a Wizard-of-Oz simulated algorithm.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors state their results may not be generalizable beyond study-allocation DLPs (e.g., they note Uber-type ride-hailing platforms could differ) and that the sample was limited to English-speaking Prolific digital workers, restricting cultural generalizability.
- They flag the use of mixed Likert anchors (5-, 6-, and 7-point scales) as a limitation that may have affected comparability across measures and their interpretation as metric scales.
- The retained sample (234) fell below the G*Power-recommended 280, which the authors acknowledge marginally reduced statistical power (they argue it remained adequate for medium effects).
- Their transparency operationalization was non-interactive (monitoring/controlling-focused) and did not let workers question, shape, or feed back into the algorithm; including scientific terms (e.g., "algorithmic matching/controlling") in the design may have affected participant understanding.
- The framing positions the finding against prior work claiming transparency increases fairness (e.g., Parent-Rocheleau and Parker 2022; Zhang et al. 2022; Benlian et al. 2022) and aligns with a stream arguing transparency has limited ability to increase perceived fairness (Ochmann et al. 2021; Wanner 2021); the paper warns practitioners against "fairwashing" working conditions. Note: the manuscript labels the moderation hypotheses H2/H3 in the theory section but refers to them as H4–H5 in the procedure description (an internal numbering inconsistency).
