## Summary
This is a single-author "Dear Editor" letter/commentary aimed at medical and nursing researchers, motivated by the growing adoption of qualitative methods (used either autonomously or within mixed-method designs). It asks how the quality of qualitative research projects can be ensured, and answers by laying out a comprehensive strategy grounded in the four trustworthiness criteria identified by Lincoln and Guba: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. For each criterion the article maps concrete researcher strategies: credibility via prolonged engagement, reflexivity, and triangulation; transferability via thick descriptions and articulated sampling strategies; dependability via methodological documentation and audit trails; and confirmability via peer debriefing, member checking, and reflexive journaling. These strategies and their rationales are consolidated in Table 1. The piece argues that establishing trustworthiness advances cumulative knowledge, informs policy, and improves professional practice, while acknowledging that qualitative research is limited by its subjective/interpretive nature and by the time, cost, and practicality demands of rigorous methods. It is a narrative/conceptual contribution rather than an empirical study; the author states no data were used.

## Key facts it relies on
- The four trustworthiness criteria are attributed to Lincoln and Guba: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (refs [16,17]; Lincoln & Guba, Naturalistic Inquiry, 1985, ref [17]).
- Credibility is "the degree to which the findings accurately reflect the reality that the participants experienced," developed through extended/prolonged engagement, persistent observation, and triangulation.
- Transferability is "the degree to which the research findings can be extrapolated to alternative contexts or situations," enhanced through thick/comprehensive descriptions and clearly articulated sampling strategies.
- Dependability is "the enduring and unwavering nature of the research findings across time," assured through rigorous methodological documentation and the creation/preservation of an audit trail (a comprehensive log of decisions).
- Confirmability is "the impartiality and objectivity of the findings," improved through peer debriefing, member checking, and reflexive journaling.
- The article cites Tong et al.'s Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), described as a 32-item checklist for interviews and focus groups (ref [14], Int. J. Qual. Health Care 19 (2007) 349–357).
- It notes that a checklist alone "cannot be guaranteed" to ensure a qualitative project's quality, even though it makes aspects measurable.
- Table 1 ("Strategies for Ensuring Trustworthiness in Qualitative Research") maps each of the four components to specific researcher strategies with detailed rationales (e.g., triangulation = "interviews, observations, documents" to cross-verify findings).
- The article cites prior work on rigor in qualitative pharmacy/health-services research, including Hadi and Closs (ref [10], Int. J. Clin. Pharm. 38 (2016) 641–646) on areas where demonstration of rigor is lacking.
- The article reports no funding, no competing interests, no data used, and that ethical approval was not required; it is single-authored by Sirwan Khalid Ahmed (Department of Adult Nursing, University of Raparin, Kurdistan Region, Iraq).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The author explicitly states that an essential limitation of qualitative research is its subjective nature; despite reflexivity and triangulation, the researcher's interpretive perspective "might still impact the findings" across data collection, analysis, and interpretation (ref [23], [6]).
- The author acknowledges that demanding trustworthiness methods (prolonged engagement, thorough documentation) impose burdens of time, financial resources, and practicality, and that some projects lack sufficient field time or resources for full documentation and verification.
- The author notes that member checking and peer debriefing can fail to deliver accurate validation due to social desirability bias, power dynamics, or limited participant/peer understanding of the research objectives.
- Scope/format caveat (from the document itself): this is a "Dear Editor" commentary with no empirical data ("No data were used in the research described in this article"); it synthesizes and re-presents the established Lincoln & Guba framework rather than testing or extending it, so its claims are conceptual and reliant on cited secondary literature.
