## Summary
The paper asks how employees perceive distributive justice (the fairness of an outcome) when an algorithmic manager fails to fulfill its promises, and whether anthropomorphism of the manager moderates this. Drawing on psychological-contract, anthropomorphism, and organizational-justice theories, the authors hypothesized that employees feel lower distributive justice when an algorithmic manager fails to fulfill transactional rather than relational promises, especially when the manager is non-anthropomorphized, and that perceived rigidity mediates this. They ran two vignette experiments using a 2 (nonfulfilled promise type: relational vs. transactional) x 2 (anthropomorphism: high vs. low) between-subjects design with UK/US employees recruited via Prolific (Study 1 N=258; preregistered Study 2 N=248). Study 1 supported the predicted main effect (transactional nonfulfillment lowered distributive justice, B=-.51, p<.05) and the anthropomorphism moderation (B=.71, p<.01): low-anthropomorphism transactional nonfulfillment lowered justice, but no effect under high anthropomorphism. Study 2 did not replicate the main effect or direct moderation, but found a moderated-mediation: nonfulfillment x anthropomorphism predicted perceived rigidity (B=-.89, p<.05), and rigidity in turn lowered distributive justice (B=-.20, p<.01), with the conditional indirect effect significant only under low anthropomorphism. The authors conclude anthropomorphic design can buffer negative reactions by reducing perceived rigidity, but flag the divergent results as non-robust and raise ethical concerns that anthropomorphizing algorithmic managers may blur accountability.

## Key facts it relies on
- Design: 2 (nonfulfilled promise type: relational vs. transactional) x 2 (anthropomorphism: high vs. low) between-subjects vignette experiments; participants imagined working at "Beta Management Company" supervised by a "robot manager" (low: "Robo3000," a chat tool; high: "Lily/Noah Adams," human-like name, image generated via Midjourney/DALL-E 3, conversational language).
- Study 1: N=258 UK employees (M age=36.76, SD=11.05; 61% female; all working >=20 hrs/week), recruited via Prolific, data collected 5-12 September 2024; an a priori MANOVA power analysis required 200 participants for a small effect f^2=.06 at 80% power.
- Study 2 (preregistered): N=248 employees (45% UK, 54.8% US; M age=37.12, SD=9.46; 56.9% female), data collected 10-20 October 2024.
- Measures: distributive justice via Colquitt's sub-measure (4 items; Study 1 alpha=.95, Study 2 alpha=.91); anthropomorphism manipulation check 2 items (Study 1 alpha=.72, Study 2 alpha=.77); perceived rigidity single item ("How rigid do you think your Robot Manager would be...," 7-point); controls were tenure position and AI attitude (Grassini 4-item scale, alpha=.91).
- Study 1 results: transactional (vs. relational) nonfulfillment negatively predicted distributive justice (B=-.51, p<.05); anthropomorphism moderated this (B=.71, p<.01). Cell means (Table 2): low-anthropomorphism transactional M=1.88 (SD=1.32) vs. low relational M=2.38 (SD=1.36); high transactional M=2.42 (SD=1.65) vs. high relational M=2.21 (SD=1.19).
- Study 2 results: main effect of nonfulfillment on distributive justice NOT significant (B=-.02, ns); direct moderation NOT significant (B=-.21, ns). Nonfulfillment x anthropomorphism predicted rigidity (B=-.89, p<.05); under low anthropomorphism transactional nonfulfillment raised rigidity (B=.77, p<.01), no difference under high (B=-.12, ns); rigidity negatively predicted distributive justice (B=-.20, p<.01); moderated mediation significant only under low anthropomorphism (Est.=-.15, BootCI [-.33, -.01]).
- Manipulation checks: anthropomorphism manipulation worked in both studies (Study 1 high M=3.57 vs. low M=2.30, F(1,254)=60.25, p<.001, eta^2=.19; Study 2 high M=3.67 vs. low M=2.50, F(1,244)=36.32, p<.001, eta^2=.13); promise-type check was only marginal in Study 1 (transactional M=5.51 vs. relational M=5.11, F(1,254)=3.38, p=.06) but significant in Study 2 (M=6.30 vs. M=4.98, F(1,244)=44.41, p<.001, eta^2=.15).
- Theoretical framing distinguishes form anthropomorphism (human-like names/faces/avatars) from behavioral anthropomorphism (conversational/social cues), manipulated in combination; relational promises = long-term socioemotional exchanges, transactional promises = economic/task-oriented exchanges (Tomprou and Lee-inspired vignettes).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly state the effects "are not fully robust and should be interpreted cautiously": Study 2 failed to replicate Study 1's main effect and direct moderation, surfacing only an indirect (rigidity-mediated) effect. They attribute divergence to differing reference frames (Study 1 self-referential justice vs. Study 2 generalized/system-level) and to different promise framings across studies (salary/career vs. task-performance/well-being).
- Perceived rigidity, the central mediator in Study 2, was measured with a single item, which the authors concede reduces reliability and increases measurement error; they call the mediation a conservative estimate to be interpreted cautiously.
- The promise-type manipulation check in Study 1 was only marginally significant (p=.06) and relied on a single item, which the authors flag as a limitation weakening certainty in Study 1's findings.
- The global anthropomorphism manipulation check could not disentangle form vs. behavioral anthropomorphism, so the paper cannot say which facet drove effects; the vignette-based design also limits ecological/external validity and the cross-sectional design cannot establish causal direction (an exploratory reverse-mediation in Study 2 was non-significant but not definitive).
- The authors raise an ethical critique of their own practical recommendation: anthropomorphizing algorithmic managers to "soften" perceptions of rigid/unfair decisions may blur accountability, create deception, and suppress employees' critical awareness of systemic bias rather than improving substantive fairness. The study examined only distributive justice, not procedural or interactional justice.
