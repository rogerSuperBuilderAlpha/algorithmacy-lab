## Summary
The article asks what distinctively characterises work done through (and for) a digital platform, and argues that labour process theory (LPT) — a Marxist approach to relations of production in industrial capitalism, following Braverman (1974) and the "core theory" of Thompson (1989, 1990) — is an under-utilised but valuable resource for theorising the gig economy. It is a conceptual/theoretical paper that does not present its own fieldwork; instead it supports its argument with empirical illustrations drawn from existing research on platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, Upwork/Freelancer, Uber and Deliveroo. The argument is organised around three LPT concepts: the "point of production", emotional labour, and managerial control. Gandini contends that gig platforms constitute a distinctive digital-based "point of production" that repurposes and "fences off" social relations into relations of production; that feedback, ranking and rating systems embed emotional labour at the heart of the labour process and produce a "qualitative intensification" of it; and that these same metrics enable "techno-normative" forms of control (e.g. "management by customers", gamification). The paper concludes that gig workers should be understood not as mere "users" of a digital medium but as paid labourers in a real capital–labour relation, and calls for LPT to take a more central role in critiquing platform work.

## Key facts it relies on
- The paper adopts Heeks's (2017) framework treating the "digital gig economy" as a continuum of three platform types: (a) "click-work" micro-task platforms (e.g. Amazon Mechanical Turk); (b) freelance-matching platforms for local manual labour (TaskRabbit, Handy, Angie's List) or global knowledge work (Upwork, Freelancer); (c) consumer-led service work such as delivery or cab driving (Uber, Deliveroo); it explicitly excludes platforms not engaging workers in a capital–labour relation (e.g. Airbnb).
- It reports at least 45 million users worldwide registered as workers on digital platforms in 2015 (Codagnone et al., 2016).
- In the US, work arrangements alternative to standard employment grew from 10.7% to 15.8% of the workforce between 2005 and 2015, with "gig work" accounting for 0.5% overall (Katz and Krueger, 2016).
- In the UK, an estimated 1.3 million workers (around 4% of the workforce) regularly engage in gig work (Taylor et al., 2017); Uber and Deliveroo operate in more than 75 UK cities and more than 500 cities worldwide (Balaram et al., 2016).
- On Amazon Mechanical Turk the platform charges a percentage fee of between 20% and 45% of the value of a gig (Silberman and Irani, 2015; Paolacci et al., 2010); its labour process is described as three steps — requesters post tasks, workers choose and do them, requesters approve or reject (Silberman and Irani, 2015: 6).
- Deliveroo couriers are paid on a fixed piece-work basis of £3.75 per gig in the UK (originally an hourly rate; Woodcock, 2016); the Uber algorithm sets a ride's value in real time from time, place, traffic and driver availability, which workers can only accept or decline (Rosenblat and Stark, 2016).
- The paper draws on the "core theory" of LPT (Thompson, 1990), summarised in four points: study of labour's function in capital accumulation at the "point of production"; attention to skills; control of the labour process; and structurally antagonistic capital–labour relations (autonomy, consent, resistance) — and notes the core theory explicitly refuses Marx's labour theory of value.
- Key conceptual borrowings include Hochschild's (1983) "emotional labour", Fuller and Smith's (1991) "management by customers", Vincent's (2011) "economy of feelings"/"conforming" emotional labour, and Friedman's (2014) "shadow employer"; on Uber, drivers must meet an average rating target that "hovers around 4.6 out of 5 stars" and "perform emotional labor in exchange for ratings instead of tips" (Rosenblat, 2016; Rosenblat and Stark, 2016: 3775).
- Deliveroo couriers receive personalised monthly "service level assessments" on metrics such as average "time to accept orders", "travel time to restaurant/customer", "time at customer", "late orders" and "unassigned orders" (O'Connor, 2017), against the platform's own estimate of expected speed.

## Critical notes from the literature
- This is a theoretical/conceptual contribution illustrated with secondary examples, not original empirical research; the author states the analysis "brings more questions than conclusions" and explicitly leaves skills, autonomy, consent and resistance largely outside the inquiry, flagging them for future research.
- The paper acknowledges LPT has itself been criticised as "resistant" (Böhm and Land, 2012: 220) to the transformations of the "new economy", being perceived as focused on the "waged workplace" (Thompson and Smith, 2009: 917), and that the core theory has "attracted remarkable criticism" (e.g. Jaros, 2005), including over its rejection of the labour theory of value.
- Gandini positions LPT against rival Marxist approaches — Autonomist/post-workerist Marxism ("immaterial labour", Lazzarato) and the "digital labour" framework (Fuchs, 2014) — arguing the latter's focus on unpaid/"invisible" user labour is of limited use for gig work where workers are "actual", paid labourers; this is a contestable framing internal to a live disciplinary debate.
- The numbers cited are acknowledged as uncertain: estimates of the gig workforce are "difficult to provide" because workers register on multiple platforms and the population is hard to separate from broader figures on freelancing and contingent work; the picture is described as "small but growing".
- Following Tweedie (2013), the paper warns against overstating the relevance of employment-status disputes and urges attention to workers' lived experiences and the social/cultural implications of transactionalised work relations.
