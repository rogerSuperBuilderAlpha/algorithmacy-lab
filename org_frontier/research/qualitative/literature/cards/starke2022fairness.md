## Summary
This paper presents a systematic review of the empirical literature on how people perceive the fairness of algorithmic decision-making (ADM), motivated by calls for a human-centric approach to designing and implementing such systems. The authors ask "How do individuals perceive the fairness of algorithmic decision-making?" and follow Petticrew and Roberts's (2006) seven-step review protocol, combining a Boolean systematic search across four databases plus gray-literature and citation searches, screening more than 3000 entries with two coders. The final sample comprises 58 empirical studies incorporating over 33,000 unique observations of citizens' fairness perceptions. The authors systemize the literature along four dimensions: algorithmic predictors, human predictors, comparative effects (human vs. algorithmic decision-making), and consequences of ADM. Key findings are that fairness perceptions are highly context-dependent (varying across domains, tasks, and algorithmic designs), that respondents tend to favor simpler fairness definitions, that comparative HDM-vs-ADM results are ambiguous, and that very few studies (eleven) examine the consequences of perceived (un)fairness. The review also notes that nearly all evidence comes from Western-democratic (especially US) contexts. The authors advocate for more theoretical groundwork, harmonized measurement, non-Western research, and an interdisciplinary "society-in-the-loop" framework.

## Key facts it relies on
- The review synthesizes 58 empirical studies incorporating over 33,000 unique observations of citizens' fairness perceptions of ADM.
- The search applied Boolean operators: ("big data" OR "artificial intelligence" OR "machine learning" OR "algorithm*") AND ("fair*" OR "unfair*" OR "just*" OR "discrimina*" OR "bias" OR "disparate"), on titles/subtitles, restricted to English-language work published since January 2010, across Web of Science, PsycINFO, IEEE Xplore, and Scopus.
- The search yielded 5117 contributions (24 February 2022), 3097 after de-duplication, plus 101 gray-literature items; two raters screened 3198 publications, selecting 389, then 46, and added 12 via citation searches of three "pearl" papers (Binns et al. 2018; Grgic-Hlaca et al. 2018a; Lee 2018) for a final 58.
- Inter-rater reliability: Cohen's kappa = .55 ("fair agreement", 72 abstracts) in the first test and .74 ("good agreement", 39 abstracts) in the second.
- National context was highly homogeneous: 32 studies were conducted in the US, three in China, three in Germany, two in the UK, two in the Netherlands, one in South Korea, one in Cyprus, four in multiple countries; ten studies did not indicate the country.
- Methods: 8 studies qualitative, 37 quantitative, 13 mixed-method; domains were dominated by work-related decisions (16, especially hiring) and criminal justice (13, especially pretrial risk assessment).
- Fairness concepts followed organizational-justice dimensions: distributive (23 studies), procedural (22 studies went beyond distributive), interactional (5 studies), and informational fairness (6 studies); the four-dimension framework draws on Greenberg (1990), Deutsch (1975), and Leventhal (1980).
- Measurement varied: 14 studies used single Likert items (5- or 7-point), 17 adapted existing fairness scales (e.g. Colquitt and Rodell 2015), and 5 used stated preferences between ADM options.
- Comparative effects: 24 studies compared HDM vs. ADM with ambiguous results; e.g., Helberger et al. (2020) found 54% of respondents thought AI makes fairer decisions vs. 33% for humans.
- Only 11 studies investigated the consequences of perceived algorithmic (un)fairness.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors state four explicit limitations: the review covers only English-language work (not exhaustive/representative of other languages); it included published research without a public call for unpublished studies (mitigated by including pre-prints); search strings were applied only to titles/subtitles, missing studies that use keywords only in the main text; and the authors are social scientists who acknowledge reading the interdisciplinary literature through a disciplinary lens.
- The evidence base is overwhelmingly WEIRD (White, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) and US-centric, which the authors flag as a generalizability problem (citing Henrich et al. 2010), calling for diversification toward non-Western contexts such as China and South Korea.
- The authors attribute much of the inconsistency in empirical results to the absence of coherent theoretical frameworks and inconsistent use of fairness concepts (e.g., procedural fairness defined narrowly as input features by some, broadly to include consistency/revocability by others); they also note formal fairness definitions are often mutually incompatible (Kleinberg et al. 2017).
- Most reviewed studies rely on fairness concepts developed for human decision-making (Greenberg 1990), yet evidence (Dietvorst et al. 2015) suggests people evaluate ADM by different criteria, so transferring these concepts may be inappropriate.
- The literature emphasizes drivers of fairness perceptions over consequences, and the authors note the binary HDM-vs-ADM framing neglects hybrid/human-oversight forms (human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, human-in-command) that better reflect real deployments.
