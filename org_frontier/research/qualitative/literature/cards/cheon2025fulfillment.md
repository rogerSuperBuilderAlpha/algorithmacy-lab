## Summary
This CSCW paper asks how workers in a traditional (non-platform) work sector experience algorithmic management, using Amazon fulfillment centers (FCs) as a case to extend a literature dominated by platform/gig studies. Drawing on two years of multi-sited ethnography—online participant observation of three FC-worker communities (two subReddits and one Discord) plus 16 interviews with FC workers across 16 FCs in 12 US states—the authors document how the FC "labor-tracking system" assembles productivity metrics, barcodes, scanners, the A-to-Z app, cameras and workstation monitors to track workers, and how discrepancies between tracked data and actual labor require human (manager) intervention to resolve. They show this quantification reifies workers as numbers, and that workers respond with self-vernacular "tricks" (e.g., cherry-picking, virtual picking, pick-and-choose, exploiting the "scan to scan" timer for longer breaks, fake-GPS clock-ins). Theoretically, they read these tricks through Burawoy's sociological concept of "work games"—games workers initiate that grant a sense of agency while aligning with management's productivity goals—and propose three forms: active consenting-resistance, passive consenting-resistance, and non-consenting resistance. They also characterize the FC manager as a "context prober" who corrects/validates algorithmic data, a source of human unpredictability absent in pure-platform settings ("double monitoring"), and note FC workers rely on online communities rather than in-person coworkers for algorithmic sensemaking, with little evidence of collective action.

## Key facts it relies on
- Empirical base: two years of ethnographic research (first author), beginning September 2020, including online observations of three FC-worker online communities (two subReddits, one Discord channel) and 16 interviews; field method framed as multi-sited ethnography to "follow the people."
- Observation effort: ~2-3 hours/week reading postings for a year (to September 2021), then two intensive non-participant observation periods (Sep-Oct 2021; May-Jun 2022) of at least 20 hours/week; the 2500 most-replied postings from each community over Nov 1, 2020-Oct 31, 2021 were collected (1998 from distinct users).
- Interview sample: 16 workers, all starting as level-one associates; roles included Packers (4), Stowers (6), Pickers (6), with 11 of 16 holding more than one role; no managers; tenure 6 months to 5 years; 16 different FCs in 12 US states; ages late teens to 30s (13 in their 20s); gender 4 female, 10 male, 2 non-binary; mean interview 81 min (range 55-107); each paid a $30 Visa gift card.
- The "labor-tracking system" is defined as an assemblage: individual barcodes, handheld scanners (track activity/speed/location), workstation computers and always-on performance monitors, strategically placed cameras, the "A-to-Z" app, and clock-in/out stations; managers track in real time via software such as the Associate Development and Performance Tracker.
- Productivity is measured as "rate" (tasks/hour), with task-specific scan rate, stow rate, pick rate; per worker online discussions, a pick rate over 200 units/hour is standard at hire while 420 is expected during peak seasons; failing to "make rate" can lead to disciplinary action up to termination.
- Specific metric mechanics cited: "scan to scan" (time between scans), UPH (Units Per Hour), TAKT (average time to scan and stow an item), TPH (Throughput per Hour), and TOT (Time off Tasks); the system flags inactivity after ~3-5 minutes (2-3 min threshold elsewhere), and over 2 hours of TOT can mean "instant termination."
- Direct labor (receive, stow, pick, pack) is heavily rated/tracked automatically; indirect labor (e.g., batching, restocking, clearing totes, teaching) is "not computationally intelligible" and must be entered manually, sometimes by managers.
- Documented worker "tricks": cherry-picking (logging out so the system reassigns a better pick path), virtual picking (printing barcodes to scan in place and boost rate via scan-to-scan), pick-and-choose (sequencing item sizes to boost pick rate), exploiting menu screens / leaving an item unscanned for longer breaks, having a friend stow at your station to avoid TOT, fake-GPS clock-ins via the A-to-Z app, and repurposing boxes/water cases as seats.
- Theoretical anchor: Michael Burawoy's "work games" / "making out" (factory ethnography), where "playing the game simultaneously produces consent to its rules" (p.193-194) and "consent rather than fear ruled the shop floor"; the paper also cites Donald Roy's "banana time."

## Critical notes from the literature
- Access limitation acknowledged by the authors: direct access to physical FCs was restricted, so the study centers workers' online narratives and digital-space observation rather than in-situ engagement with the systems; they defend this via Gusterson's "polymorphous engagement" tradition and note that an Amazon partnership would have imposed constraints on outcomes.
- Self-reported recruitment difficulty and positionality: workers frequently declined or distrusted that the first author was not affiliated with Amazon, often citing NDAs; neither author has lived experience as a blue-collar/warehouse worker, and they frame the work through a constructivist-interpretive paradigm with "empathetic understanding."
- Scope/transferability caution stated by the authors: modern FCs differ markedly from the 1950s factory floors where work games originated and from platform-mediated gig work, so the work-games framing is applied as an analytical lens rather than a claim of equivalence; findings are positioned as specific to traditional workplaces with co-present managerial oversight.
- The authors note an ethics tension in reporting workers' "tricks" (the company could patch them) but argue the tricks largely serve management's interest in high productivity, so corrective action is judged unlikely.
- The paper reports little evidence of collective action / "algoactivism" among FC workers, attributing resistance to self-organized, individualized "tricks"; this is partly explained by the company's monitoring and thwarting of unionization, a scope condition that bounds the consent/resistance claims.
