## Summary
This methodological paper illustrates how Guba and Lincoln's "parallel criteria" for establishing trustworthiness, also called the Four-Dimensions Criteria (FDC: credibility, dependability, confirmability, transferability), can be adapted and applied to qualitative research on indigenous social protection systems. The author draws empirical material from a larger ethnographic doctoral study on the dynamics of indigenous versus formal social protection in handling covariate shocks (with emphasis on floods) in Southeast Nigeria, using in-depth interviews, focus group discussions and participant observation in two purposively selected communities (Umueze-Anam and Nzam, Anambra State). Following Tuckett (2005), Morse (2015a) and Forero et al. (2018), the criteria were adapted point by point, selecting per-criterion strategies that fit the study (e.g., prolonged engagement, persistent observation, debriefing, referential adequacy, rich description, member checks, triangulation, reflexivity, sampling techniques, saturation). The paper concludes that the FDC is viable for establishing trustworthiness in this research area and can support credible, policy-relevant outcomes, and notes this is the first application of the FDC to the study of indigenous social protection systems. It argues such qualitative inquiries should complement the quantitative-dominated social protection research space.

## Key facts it relies on
- The FDC's four criteria map onto quantitative criteria as analogs: credibility ~ internal validity, dependability ~ reliability, confirmability ~ objectivity, and transferability ~ external validity/generalizability (Lincoln and Guba, 1986).
- Lincoln and Guba's (1986) idea of scientific rigor comprises two components: parallel criteria of trustworthiness and unique criteria of authenticity; the authenticity criteria are explicitly stated to be beyond the scope of this paper.
- The underlying study used an ethnographic qualitative design (in-depth interviews/IDIs, focus group discussions/FGDs, participant observation) in two communities, Umueze-Anam and Nzam in Anambra State, Southeast Nigeria, selected purposively for rural/agrarian location, minority status, and history of perennial flood vulnerability in the Niger River lowlands.
- Study participants spanned three categories: 7 key informants (relevant staff of state ministries/entities), 38 extremely poor and vulnerable community members (e.g., persons with severe physical disability and poor aged), and 11 indigenous community-based associations.
- Indigenous community-based systems studied included Town Unions (community development associations), social clubs (mutual aid), age grades (community support networks), ROSCAs (rotating savings and credit associations), and indigenous women's organizations.
- Prolonged engagement involved spending two months at each of the two study sites; each observation generally lasted between 2 and 10 hours, with no notes taken on the spot (events recorded later at the researcher's apartment).
- Saturation: 7 IDIs with key informants, 36 IDIs with vulnerable community members across the two sites, and 11 FGDs were conducted; after constant in-field review, 2 additional interviews were carried out to confirm saturation, bringing the total IDIs with the vulnerable to 38; theoretical saturation (not code/meaning saturation) was applied.
- Data were fully transcribed and analyzed using Braun et al.'s (2019, p. 852) six-phase reflexive thematic analysis approach; ethical approval came from the Faculty of Humanities Research Ethics Committee, University of Pretoria.
- Strategies were mapped to criteria: credibility (prolonged engagement, persistent observation, debriefing, referential adequacy); dependability (rich description, member checks - process not terminal); confirmability (triangulation, reflexivity); transferability (sampling techniques - purposive and snowball, saturation), with negative case analysis, stepwise replication, and audit trail marked "Not applicable" in Table 1.
- Triangulation used within-methods triangulation (IDIs, group interviews, observations combined in an ethnographic approach) and data-source triangulation across vulnerable populations, government establishments, and different indigenous groups in two settings.
- Context: Nigeria ratified a National Social Protection Policy (NSPP) in 2017, in which indigenous social protection systems are given a peripheral position; a 2016 International Workshop in Johannesburg deliberated on these indigenous systems.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper acknowledges ongoing debate about qualitative rigor: Morse (2015a) suggested returning to mainstream terminology (rigor, reliability, validity, generalizability) rather than trustworthiness/dependability/credibility/transferability, and the proliferation of criteria can undermine rather than clarify the concept (Johnson and Rasulova, 2016; Morse et al., 2002).
- The author notes criticisms of Guba and Lincoln's (1989) argument abound (cited in Morse et al., 2002), though such criticisms have also been addressed (e.g., Lincoln and Guba, 1994), framing the paradigm shift as work-in-progress.
- Scope limitation stated by the author: how qualitative FDC-based research can be integrated with quantitative social protection research for a holistic system is beyond the scope of the paper and is left for future research; the authenticity criteria are likewise not addressed.
- This is a single-study, single-author demonstration/illustration of applying the criteria (drawn from the author's own doctoral fieldwork) rather than an empirical evaluation comparing methods; the contribution is methodological adaptation, and the author claims success but the assessment is self-reported.
