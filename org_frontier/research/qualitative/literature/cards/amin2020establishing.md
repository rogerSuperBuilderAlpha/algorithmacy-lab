## Summary
This commentary in Research in Social and Administrative Pharmacy provides methodological guidance for establishing quality (rigor) in qualitative social pharmacy research, responding to the steady growth of qualitative and mixed-methods work in the field. It explains each of Lincoln and Guba's four trustworthiness criteria — credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability — and the techniques used to establish them (prolonged engagement and persistent observation, member checking, triangulation, peer debriefing, negative case analysis, thick contextual description, external audit/audit trail, and reflexivity/transparency). It then introduces the less-widely-used authenticity criteria — fairness, ontological, educative, catalytic, and tactical authenticity — which the authors note had not previously been addressed in the context of qualitative social pharmacy research. For each criterion the paper supplies a definition, applicability to social pharmacy, guidance on when and how to use it, pros and cons, and concrete examples drawn from the pharmacy literature. The authors stress that these criteria are not a checklist; strategies must be selected to fit the underlying problem and specific research question. The piece closes by acknowledging competing viewpoints on evaluating qualitative research and inviting reflection rather than prescription.

## Key facts it relies on
- The paper distinguishes four characteristics of science (theory-based, systematic techniques, cumulative, predictive) and four common research paradigms: positivism, post-positivism, interpretivism, and critical theory.
- It organizes quality around Lincoln and Guba's four trustworthiness sub-criteria — credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability — and maps them to positivist criteria (internal validity, external validity, reliability, objectivity) in Table 1.
- Triangulation is presented in four classical types — methodological, data, investigator, theoretical — plus a fifth, environmental triangulation; Bryman's within-method vs between-method distinction is invoked for methodological triangulation.
- The authenticity criteria (fairness, ontological, educative, catalytic, tactical authenticity) come from Guba and Lincoln; Table 1 notes there are no equivalents for the authenticity criterion within the positivist paradigm, and fairness is considered the most important authenticity sub-criterion.
- The audit technique uses the six Halpern audit trail categories (raw data; data reduction/analysis products; data reconstruction/synthesis products; process notes; materials on intentions and dispositions; instrument development information) and a five-step audit process (preentry, determination of auditability, formal agreement, determination of trustworthiness criteria, closure); Lincoln and Guba argue an audit can be done in seven to ten days.
- In Haugbølle et al.'s catalytic-authenticity example, 29 pharmacies (85% of participating pharmacies) made decisions to develop activities for angina pectoris patients; staff-oriented activities were initiated in 23 pharmacies and patient-oriented activities in nine pharmacies.
- In the antibiotic data-triangulation example, 13 pharmacy staff were recruited from community pharmacies that dispensed antibiotics and two from pharmacies that did not, with that study involving three authors (a former practicing pharmacist, a non-practicing physician, and a primary care practitioner) for investigator triangulation.
- Tong et al.'s COREQ is cited as a consolidated 32-item checklist for reporting qualitative research; the authors caution that checklists do not necessarily ensure quality.
- The paper reports it was unable to identify a pharmacy study using external audit and audit trail, drawing instead on health-services examples (e.g., the BETTER Trial and a physiotherapy "conditions of engagement" study).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly state the criteria are not meant to be treated as a checklist; strategies must be chosen in combination with the underlying problem and specific research question, and they invite reflection rather than prescription.
- Member checking is debated: the paper cites Morse, who does not recommend it because participants may not recognize their own story in a synthesized text, and notes concerns about favoring more literate participants, politeness bias, and "reconstructing an average or typical position."
- The paper notes saturation's position as a gold standard has been questioned, citing Saunders et al.'s argument to treat saturation as a process (with four models) rather than an event, and introduces crystallization as an alternative to triangulation under interpretive/critical/postmodern paradigms.
- The authors acknowledge alternative positions: Hammersley argues qualitative research should focus on political action rather than knowledge production (offering "guidelines" rather than fixed criteria), and others propose pluralism via reflexive dialogue; some hold that no set of criteria should be used at all.
- Self-acknowledged scope limits: the commentary is not a comprehensive manual on conducting qualitative methods and does not expound on the importance of sound conceptual/theoretical underpinnings (addressed elsewhere); it states that no study can inspire 100% confidence and reflexivity discussions in existing pharmacy studies were often too brief.
