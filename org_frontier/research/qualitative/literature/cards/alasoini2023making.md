## Summary
The paper asks how and for what purposes platform workers operating in three different types of control contexts have practiced and developed their "digital agency" for "making out" (securing extra earnings, autonomy, or working the system to their benefit). Drawing on sociological labor-process theory (Roy, Burawoy), the educational concept of digital agency (Passey et al., 2018), and Maffie's (2020) classification of platform control, the authors study three Finnish platform types: food-delivery (Alpha and Beta, low-skill standardized work), a global freelance marketplace (Gamma, skilled knowledge work), and an interim-management platform (Delta, high-skill managerial work). The empirical base is an analysis of platform webpages plus 32 semi-structured interviews (10 food couriers, 15 Gamma freelancers, 7 Delta experts). Coding 488 agency "episodes" into questioning, making do/out, and ideating, they find questioning dominates among couriers (Type 1) while making do/out dominates among freelancers and experts (Types 2 and 3). The forms of platform control shape both the room workers have for agency and their motivation to direct it toward making out; less fairness and more standardization tend to constrain making out while raising questioning. The authors conclude that algorithmically controlled platforms are new arenas of work that reproduce, or even amplify, offline labor-market inequalities between low-skilled, high-skilled, and managerial workers.

## Key facts it relies on
- The study uses 32 semi-structured interviews: 10 food couriers (Alpha/Beta), 15 freelancers (Gamma), and 7 interim-management experts (Delta), plus an analysis of platform webpages.
- Platform-type selection followed an analysis of webpages and terms of service of 46 labor platforms operating in Finland, divided into six models by market vs. corporation HRM logics (Immonen, 2023).
- The control analysis applies Maffie's (2020) classification across six dimensions: performance evaluation, centralization of worker-client communication, centralization of payment (exchange centralization), and filtering, price setting, and matching (market control).
- Maffie's layered control has three stages: architecture of identity, centralization of exchange (trust, communication, payment), and market control; the three types differ in matching: Type 1 = forced, Type 2 = open, Type 3 = single-sided.
- Digital agency (Passey et al., 2018) comprises three elements: digital competence, digital confidence, and digital accountability; transformative agency goes beyond accepted routines (Vänninen et al., 2015).
- 488 agency episodes were coded; distribution by type — Type 1: questioning 111 (49%), making do/out 69 (31%), ideating 48 (19%), total 228; Type 2: questioning 85 (42%), making do/out 95 (47%), ideating 22 (11%), total 202; Type 3: questioning 14 (24%), making do/out 31 (53%), ideating 13 (22%), total 58.
- Four illustrated forms of making out: working on multiple platforms, taking on new roles (e.g., a freelancer also registering as a client on Gamma), tricks (e.g., Alpha couriers gaming the forced-break rule triggered after rejecting three invitations in a row), and disintermediation (taking clients off-platform).
- Interview specifics: couriers were interviewed in 2021-2022 (9 of 10 men, 8 with immigrant background); Gamma interviews in 2018-2019 (11 of 15 men, 10 native Finnish, biggest categories translators N=4, graphic designers N=3, consultants N=3); Delta in 2021-2022 (all native Finns, 5 of 7 men, all with academic degrees).
- The authors' assumptions: the more work can be standardized, the less room for worker agency and making out; the less fair workers perceive operations, the greater their motivation to develop digital agency (in line with the stratification hypothesis, Van Deursen et al., 2017).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors note they cannot estimate how statistically representative the interviewees are of all Finnish platform workers, since no such data basis exists in Finland; they argue only that the sample is diverse and not clearly biased on any single background factor.
- The study is in Finland, a highly regulated Nordic labor-market context; the authors believe national context did not greatly affect results (food-delivery and Gamma procedures resemble those elsewhere) but acknowledge institutional factors may differently constrain platforms in other countries.
- The authors concede that drawing a line between "making do" and "making out" is not straightforward (McCabe, 2014), partly because they do not know enough about platform companies' underlying intentions or algorithms, which are beyond their reach and may be intentionally or technologically opaque (Kellogg et al., 2020; Van Doorn and Badger, 2021).
- They found few examples of couriers' collective agency (resembling Veen et al., 2020 from Australia but differing from European studies showing self-organization); they suggest this may relate to the immigrant backgrounds of many Finnish couriers and to Alpha's disbanding of designated courier teams.
- The Delta (interim-management) sample is small (N=7) and represents a group "seldom, if ever, examined" in platform-work studies; the handling editor declared a past collaboration with authors LS and TA.
