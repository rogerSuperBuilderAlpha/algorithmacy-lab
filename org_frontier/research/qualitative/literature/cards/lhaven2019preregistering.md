## Summary
This conceptual/argumentative article asks whether preregistration, a transparency measure developed for quantitative research, can be adapted to boost the credibility of qualitative research. The authors ground their argument in the distinction between prediction (presenting a hypothesis at t1, then observing a confirming/disconfirming event at t2) and postdiction (observing an event at t1, then presenting a hypothesis for it at t2), arguing that qualitative research is "a real-life example of postdiction research" because it uses data to generate rather than test hypotheses. They consider three objections to preregistering qualitative studies — that qualitative research's goal is theory generation not hypothesis testing, that its flexible emergent design makes preregistration infeasible, and that its inherent subjectivity makes a frozen plan useless — and rebut each. Their rebuttals hold that preregistration's value extends beyond fixing hypotheses to placing a study's aims, design, and analysis plan on an open platform; that a qualitative preregistration can be a "living document" frozen more than once to track design changes; and that registering the researcher's theoretical lens and presuppositions actually makes subjectivity transparent and thus enhances credibility. They contextualize preregistration against Lincoln and Guba's confirmability audit (noting preregistration is open to the community, free, and done at study start rather than end), argue it combats "dissemination bias" affecting qualitative evidence synthesis, and conclude by proposing modifications to the Open Science Framework (OSF) preregistration form tailored to qualitative studies (Table 1).

## Key facts it relies on
- The article's central conceptual move is the prediction/postdiction distinction, drawn from Nosek et al. (2018): "Mistaking generation of postdictions with testing of predictions reduces the credibility of research findings," and preregistration is the solution that distinguishes the two.
- Prediction is formalized as: hypothesis A presented at t1, then observation of event B at t2 confirming/disconfirming A; postdiction as: observation of event B at t1, then presentation of hypothesis A for B at t2 (Fetzer 2017); preregistration "freezes" hypothesis A at t1.
- The authors argue qualitative research "is a real-life example of postdiction research" because data are collected to generate, not test, hypotheses; thematic analysis is given as an example (Ritchie et al. 2013).
- Three objections are raised and rebutted: (1) the goal of qualitative research is to generate rather than test theory; (2) the flexibility of emergent design makes preregistration infeasible; (3) the high subjectivity of the qualitative researcher (who functions "as part of the measurement instrument itself") challenges preregistration.
- The rebuttal proposes a qualitative preregistration be a "living document" that can be "frozen" more than once, allowing reviewers to track the study's development rather than scoring design changes as a mistake.
- The credibility framing follows Eisner (1991) — "a confluence of evidence that breeds credibility" — and the confirmability audit from Lincoln and Guba (1985), which assesses confirmability (analyses coherent, interpretations fair) and dependability (data collection sound).
- Preregistration is distinguished from an audit trail on four points: it is open to the whole scientific community rather than one trusted auditor; involves no formal agreement; is ideally done at the study's start rather than near its end; and is free, whereas an audit can be time-consuming and costly (Creswell 2007).
- Preregistration is argued to combat "dissemination bias" in qualitative research (Toews et al. 2017; Booth 2017), which threatens qualitative evidence synthesis, the qualitative counterpart to systematic reviews.
- Table 1 presents the OSF preregistration form with proposed qualitative-specific modifications: new subheadings (bold), sections deemed irrelevant for qualitative work (strikethrough, e.g., randomization, statistical models), and tradition options (grounded theory, phenomenology, narrative, ethnography, text-based, generic, other), sampling types (purposive, theoretical, convenience, snowball, random), and stopping rules (saturation, planning, resources, other).
- The article references five main qualitative approaches following Creswell (2007): narrative research, phenomenology, ethnography, case studies, and grounded theory; and notes "constant comparison" (Glaser and Strauss 1967) in grounded theory as a prediction–postdiction interplay.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper is explicitly conceptual and argumentative, not empirical: it proposes and rebuts objections and offers a template, but presents no data, pilot, or evaluation of whether the modified OSF form works in practice.
- The authors note the idea of preregistering qualitative research is "relatively novel" and that the three objections "do not originate in the published literature directly" — they are constructed by the authors (given "a charitable review") rather than cited from critics, so they are the authors' framing of potential resistance.
- The authors acknowledge there is "no parallel ready-at-hand for p-hacking in qualitative research" and concede that preregistering is "given more weight" in quantitative research, granting that the case for qualitative preregistration is weaker/different in kind.
- The authors stake out a specific philosophical position (Note 2): they do not side with postmodernist conceptualizations and place their defense "somewhere between positivism and postpositivism" (Denzin and Lincoln 1998), so the argument may not generalize to all qualitative paradigms.
- They acknowledge a "living document" preregistration makes demands "tougher for the qualitative researcher," and Note 3 flags ongoing debate among preregistration proponents about whether preregistering at study start is best (the authors take the start-of-study view).
