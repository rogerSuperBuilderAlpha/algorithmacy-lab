## Summary
This is a methods commentary in the Canadian Medical Education Journal addressing the long-running, "acrimonious" debate over whether quantitative intercoder reliability (ICR) measures (e.g., kappa, weighted kappa, binomial ICC) belong in qualitative research, given critics' view that such measures import positivist standards into an interpretivist paradigm. The authors argue it is possible to develop a qualitative-based ICR measure compatible with the interpretivist epistemological paradigm, one that need not be statistical/quantitative but can be descriptive and must characterize the extent to which independent coders agree or disagree on codes, emphasizing consistency over mere quantification. Drawing on prior literature and their own mixed-methods coding experience, they present eight process-based guidelines (summarized in Table 1 as a yes/no checklist with a justification column) for evaluating and reporting ICR. The guidelines cover number of coders, coder independence and expertise, shared analytic framework, consensus through dialogue, conflict resolution, and codebook development. The piece is aimed across the research continuum but is positioned as particularly valuable for beginning researchers who lack clear ICR guidance.

## Key facts it relies on
- ICR is defined (citing O'Conner and Joffe) as "a numerical measure of the agreement between different coders regarding how the same data should be coded."
- The paper offers exactly eight process-based guidelines, summarized in Table 1 as a checklist with Present (Yes/No) columns and a "Justification (If 'no' selected)" column.
- Guideline 1: a minimum of two coders must code the data, except when assessing intracoder (within-coder) reliability across multiple time points.
- Guideline 2: at least one coder must be external to / removed from the data collection process to minimize bias and allow a fresh perspective.
- Guideline 3: at least one coder must have expertise and previous experience coding qualitative data; steps must ensure novice coders do not produce unreasonable discrepancies.
- Guidelines 4-6: if there are multiple participant groups, a minimum of two researchers should code each group's transcripts; coders should use the same analytic framework (inductive, deductive, abductive); and coders should focus on shared meaning of codes through dialogue and consensus.
- Guideline 7: another coder with expertise in qualitative methods should be consulted to resolve outstanding conflicts/discrepancies.
- Guideline 8: consensus should produce a codebook applied to the remaining transcripts; in inductive and abductive analyses, new codes may be added iteratively until code saturation is reached.
- Table footnote clarifies code names need not be identical but the meaning of codes must be the same; researchers should use as many criteria as possible and justify any criterion not applied.
- Named quantitative ICR examples cited as the contested measures: the kappa statistic, weighted kappa statistic, and binomial intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors acknowledge the debate is real and unresolved: critics (citing Guba and Lincoln; Madill et al.) hold that quantitative ICR imports standards derived for positivist research, and Braun and Clarke assert reliability is not an appropriate criterion for judging qualitative work and is epistemologically problematic.
- The paper itself notes a "major pitfall": using quantitative ICR can create the incorrect assumption that such measures do not contradict the interpretative agenda of qualitative research, and could mask that a rigorous, in-depth qualitative analysis was not undertaken.
- This is a commentary/guideline piece, not an empirical validation; the guidelines are grounded in extant literature and the authors' own research experience rather than tested against data, and they are explicitly meant to be used in conjunction with other existing guidelines.
- The proposed "qualitative-based" alternative emphasizes achieving consistency and reflexivity rather than quantifying agreement, so it deliberately does not yield a single reliability coefficient; it relies on the relativist ontology / subjectivist epistemology framing of the interpretivist paradigm.
