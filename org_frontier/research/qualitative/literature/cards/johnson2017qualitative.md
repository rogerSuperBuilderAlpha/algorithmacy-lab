## Summary
This conceptual paper asks how rigour in qualitative impact evaluation of development interventions can be better addressed in the wake of intense debate over randomised control trials (RCTs) and the "politics of evidence." The authors argue, through a literature review rather than empirical study, that both the demand for higher quality and concerns over whose voices matter can be met through Guba and Lincoln's "trustworthiness" criteria, and especially the fifth principle of authenticity, which was developed to make research transformative and emancipatory. They review the paradigmatic disagreements over whether qualitative rigour can even be specified (foundationalist, quasi-foundationalist, and non-foundationalist positions), present the four well-used trustworthiness criteria (credibility, confirmability, dependability, transferability), and then examine the authenticity principle and its five dimensions (fairness, ontological, educative, catalytic, and tactical authenticity) in depth. Recognising that "bureaucratic," commissioned evaluations leave little room for participatory or empowering practice, the authors nonetheless argue that authenticity can be adopted into checklists of rigour as an evaluation "artefact" — a way of "playing the game to change the rules" (Guijt 2015) by raising ethical standards with commissioners. They conclude that checklists alone guarantee little and must be underpinned by an ethical orientation and constant reflexivity toward power relations, operating at both micro- and macro-ethical levels.

## Key facts it relies on
- The paper is conceptual/theoretical (a literature review and argument), not an empirical study; it was an output of a two-year collaborative Knowledge Transfer Partnership (KTP009285) between the University of Bath and Oxford Policy Management, funded by Innovate UK and the ESRC, with the main research undertaken 2014–2016.
- It builds on Guba and Lincoln's framework of "trustworthiness" (1981; 1985), proposed as a naturalist-paradigm parallel to positivist criteria for rigour, and their later "fourth generation" constructivist evaluation (1989) underpinned by the authenticity principle.
- Lincoln and Guba's five trustworthiness criteria are credibility (~internal validity), confirmability (~objectivity), dependability (~reliability), transferability (~generalisation), and authenticity (Table 1); authenticity is the criterion that distinguishes Lincoln and Guba (1985) from Guba's (1981) earlier four-aspect model.
- The authenticity principle (Guba and Lincoln, 1989) has five elements (Table 2): fairness, ontological authenticity, educative authenticity, catalytic authenticity, and tactical authenticity; its main drive is negotiation of constructs and values, and its product is "an agenda for further negotiation" rather than conclusions and recommendations (Guba 1987, p. 39).
- The debate over criteria is framed via Denzin (2011) as three positions: foundationalists (all research conforms to shared criteria), quasi-foundationalists (qualitative-specific criteria, e.g. Sandelowski 1993; Lincoln and Guba 1985), and non-foundationalists (criteria inappropriate; inquiry is "always political and moral").
- Commissioned evaluations are characterised as "bureaucratic" evaluation (Macdonald, in Norris 2015), where evaluators are hired for independence and often operate with virtually no involvement of commissioners, severely limiting the space for authenticity and participation.
- The paper proposes a "starting checklist" (Table 3) mapping authenticity strategies across four stages — Design, Data collection and analysis, Dissemination and follow up, and Review — including purposeful sampling, enabling space for voice and negotiation, and re-visiting stakeholders to assess what changed.
- It invokes the distinction between "micro-ethical issues" (consent, anonymity) and "macro-ethical issues" (whose interests evaluation serves, its role in society and public debate), drawing on Barnett and Munslow (2014).
- The critique that RCTs are "off the gold standard" and calls for "relevant rigour" requiring negotiation draw on Camfield and Duvendack (2014) and Guijt and Roche (2014); the "experimentalist surge" framing is from Picciotto (2014); the article is 7,922 words and appeared in Journal of Development Effectiveness vol. 9, no. 2, pp. 263–276.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors are explicitly self-critical ("somewhat pessimistic assessment"): they note that in commissioned evaluations the space to affect commissioners' constructions is "often extremely limited or indeed non-existent," that researchers (not participants) are more likely to change, and that it is hard to foresee change sustainable enough to catalyse action.
- They acknowledge non-foundationalist critiques (Barbour 2001; Morse et al. 2002) that reducing qualitative research to a checklist or "list of methodological procedures" does not ensure rigour and risks becoming a "box-ticking exercise"; they concede checklists "guarantee very little" and must be underpinned by mind-set, ethics, and organisational culture.
- The paper flags that opening up evaluation as a space for debate is "potentially dangerous terrain" — it can waste participants' time or, given power relations, lead to withdrawal of resources or victimisation if aggrieved actors react to revealed information.
- It records ongoing methodological disagreement: some hold rigour is essential to qualitative research as systematic science (Morse et al. 2002), others that it need not be discussed at all (Sparkes 2001); and that member/dependability checks may conflict with the multiple-realities premise of qualitative research (Smith 1993, in Tobin and Begley 2004).
- Scope condition: the framework is positioned for impact-assessment research operating within a "post-positivist paradigm of realist ontology and modified objectivist epistemology" (development consultancy), and the authenticity ideal sits largely outside the participatory paradigm that would best realise it.
