## Summary
This is a conceptual/methodology article (not an empirical study) that proposes a tri-dimensional reconceptualisation of transferability as a quality criterion for qualitative research in Health Professions Education (HPE). The authors argue that transferability is usually flattened to a single dimension — whether educational innovations or outcomes can be applied in different contexts — and that this oversimplifies a "slippery, multidimensional concept" with a tangled history rooted in generalisability. Tracing the origins of transferability from Lincoln and Guba's (1985) naturalistic inquiry through Firestone's (1993) case-to-case translation, they argue transferability should be conceptualised "on its own terms as a distinct, multidimensional construct" rather than as a type of generalisability. They propose three dimensions — applicability (relating findings practically), theoretical engagement (relating findings conceptually through theory), and resonance (relating findings affectively/emotionally) — each understood as a different way of moving qualitative findings beyond their immediate context. For each dimension they provide a definition, ontological scope conditions, guidance for use across a project's lifespan, reflective-prompt tables, and a worked example from the HPE literature. They present this "3D" model as a starting point and invitation for discussion rather than a definitive or exhaustive taxonomy.

## Key facts it relies on
- The paper proposes three dimensions of transferability: applicability (practical), theoretical engagement (conceptual), and resonance (affective); high-quality research will "likely consider at least two" of the dimensions, and projects need not consider all three.
- Transferability is "commonly mentioned as one of four or five quality criteria for qualitative research, often under the umbrella of trustworthiness" (Lincoln 1995; Shenton 2004; Stenfors 2020); the other criteria named are credibility, dependability, confirmability, and (more recently) reflexivity.
- The term transferability is attributed to Lincoln (Coghlan & Brydon-Miller 2014), who with Guba proposed it as a substitute for generalisability; Lincoln and Guba (1985) describe enhancing transferability through "thick description," a concept taken from Geertz (1973).
- The authors note Lincoln and Guba's later work (Lincoln et al. 2011) replaced transferability with the concept of "cumulation" — "ways in which similar studies...might be cumulated into meta-analyses" (p117) — which they flag as potentially problematic for interpretivist research because it draws on quantitative, hierarchical evidence-synthesis assumptions.
- Applicability connects to Lincoln and Guba's idea that findings should be relevant/useful in other contexts; the authors prefer the phrase "thoughtful description" over "thick description" and over the risk-laden "any and all information" reading, supported by a Table 1 of reflective prompts across five considerations: Context, Interpretation, Meaning, Reflexivity/Positionality, and Richness.
- Theoretical engagement is framed using Sandelowski's (1993) account of how theory varies by source (existing vs. generated), centrality (peripheral vs. informing all aspects), and temporal placement (theory-informed vs. theory-informing inductive); Table 2 summarises Varpio et al.'s (2020) three approaches: fully inductive theory development, fully theory-informed inductive, and theory-informing inductive.
- Resonance is adopted from its use in phenomenological research (Aagaard 2018) and Ellingson's (2009) arts-based quality criteria; it draws on rhetoric (Ettema 2005), Van Manen's (1997) "phenomenological nod," and assumes an interpretivist ontology; Table 3 gives reflexive prompts across five considerations (Individual experience, Understanding others' experiences, Social and cultural influences, Research context, Patterns of resonance), modelled on Absolon (2022).
- The paper builds directly on the authors' own prior short overview article on discussing transferability (Stalmeijer, Brown & O'Brien 2024, The Clinical Teacher).
- Worked examples cited: Cleland et al. (2016) on surgical training boot camps (applicability); Sawatsky et al. (2020) applying Goffman's impression-management theory to professional identity formation (theoretical engagement); and Brown et al. (2021) poetic inquiry with new doctors (resonance).
- Data availability statement: "No datasets were generated or analysed during the current study"; the article is explicitly "not an empirical article."

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly state they "do not believe there are only three dimensions of transferability"; the 3D model is positioned as "a starting point" and "an invitation to reflect on, adapt, and extend these ideas," not a definitive taxonomy.
- They acknowledge resonance carries risks as a quality criterion: findings that feel "obvious" or deeply familiar may be dismissed as lacking novelty, and "dismissing research on the basis of a lack of resonance could be rooted in bias, and discrimination"; they caution against treating novelty as the sole marker of value.
- The authors flag a power/equity scope condition: researchers in the Global West often treat their contexts as self-evident, while Global South researchers are "taxed with providing long explanations," and tight medical-education journal word counts make full contextual description difficult — they urge reviewers/editors to be generous and wary of stripping contextual detail.
- They note terminology in this area is "increasingly tangled," with transferability, applicability, and translation used "interchangeably or inconsistently" (Yilmaz 2013; Varpio et al. 2021), and that transferability is often used as a "catch-all" or relegated to the limitations section without conceptualisation (Varpio et al. 2020; Ross & Bibler Zaidi 2019).
- The dimensions are described as "not innate to any one project design or methodological choice" but a "collaborative choice between the research team, and the readers of a paper," underscoring that transferability judgements are interpretive and reader-dependent rather than fixed properties of a study.
