# Disagreement-as-Data and Intercoder Reliability Debates

The methodological fault line organizing this topic separates approaches that treat coding convergence as a quality guarantee from those that treat divergence as interpretive signal. Braun and Clarke formalized the split through a typology of three "schools" of thematic analysis (TA): coding reliability, codebook, and reflexive (Braun & Clarke, 2021). In reflexive TA, themes are generated through situated interpretation, so consensus procedures and reliability coefficients are framed as paradigmatically incoherent imports from neo-positivist work (Braun & Clarke, 2019; Braun & Clarke, 2020). The empirical anchor for treating divergence as substantive predates this typology: when six analysts coded one focus-group transcript, they agreed on basic themes but "packaged" them differently, demonstrating that disagreement indexes analytic perspective and is not solely error (Armstrong et al., 1997).

A countercurrent defends intercoder reliability (ICR) without collapsing into positivism. O'Connor and Joffe (2020) review the controversy and argue ICR can improve systematicity, communicability, and reflexive team dialogue. Cofie et al. (2022) propose process-based, interpretivist-compatible reliability guidelines, and a 2023 study formalizes inter-rater methods for qualitative case study research (Cole, 2023). This strand reframes the coefficient as a prompt for deliberation while contesting its use as a sufficiency claim.

The most direct articulation of disagreement-as-evidence comes from work insisting that coding outputs are tabulated claims about data, so disagreement rates and their substance warrant reporting and analysis (Hammer & Berland, 2014). Computational and HCI research operationalizes this. Zade et al. (2018) define collective disagreement through diversity and divergence in coding distributions, and Reyes et al. (2021) propose a "living codebook" with a difference list documenting unmatched codes. In machine learning, the learning-from-disagreement program abandons single gold labels and trains on annotator divergence as information (Uma et al., 2021).

Recent work (2023-2026) instruments divergence at finer grain. Clarke et al. (2023) document the perils of forcing high agreement and methods for building shared seeing. Two 2026 systems make disagreement an analytic object: Reflexis scaffolds positionality-aware deliberation so coder differences become productive dialogue (Ye et al., 2026), while reasoning-trace analytics quantify LLM-agent disagreement as a signal correlated with human coding reliability and codebook ambiguity (Tajik et al., 2026). For a coordination program studying opaque third parties, this convergence is suggestive: divergence among interested coders, human or algorithmic, can be measured as structured information about the object rather than discarded as noise.
