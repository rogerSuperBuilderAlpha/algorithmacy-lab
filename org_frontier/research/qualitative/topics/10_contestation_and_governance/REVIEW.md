# Contestation Rights and Algorithmic Governance

The legal anchor for contestation is Article 22 of the GDPR, which grants data subjects subject to solely automated decisions the right to obtain human intervention, express a view, and contest the outcome. Early scholarship narrowed expectations: the much-cited claim of a binding "right to explanation" was shown to rest on a recital rather than enforceable text, leaving only a limited "right to be informed" under Articles 13-15 (Wachter et al., 2017). A counter-reading reframed the same provisions as a "right to legibility" requiring intelligible accounts of an algorithm's architecture and implementation (Malgieri & Comandé, 2017). Within this debate, the right to contest emerged as the operative remedy: a due-process provision whose transparency demands derive from the need to challenge, with intervention and the right to a view subordinate to it (Bayamlıoğlu, 2021).

Contestation links individual sensemaking to systemic governance. The GDPR pairs individual rights with collaborative oversight, a duality framed as "binary governance," where data protection impact assessments connect the two and supply "multi-layered explanations" that serve both as a systemic safeguard and as scaffolding for individual recourse (Kaminski, 2019; Kaminski & Malgieri, 2020). Human intervention, treated as an isolated individual entitlement, has been judged an "unenforceable second-class right"; its value depends on embedding it within accountability culture and impact-assessment processes rather than ad hoc review (Lazcoz & de Hert, 2023). Recent comparative analysis confirms that Article 22 remains functionally weak through vague language and broad exceptions, with non-EU regimes offering stronger opt-out, audit, and impact-assessment mechanisms (Shaikh, 2025). A health-conformant interpretation, informed by the CJEU's SCHUFA ruling, shows how doctrinal reading can extend protection into new domains such as automated medical decisions (van Kolfschooten, 2024).

A parallel design and HCI literature operationalises these rights as "contestability by design," making systems responsive to dispute across their lifecycle instead of relying on after-the-fact appeals (Almada, 2019; Alfrink et al., 2022). Contestability is positioned as a governance objective distinct from transparency, sustaining ongoing critical engagement by professionals and affected publics (Mulligan et al., 2019). Empirical study of content-moderation appeals finds that users contest the decision and also the legitimacy of automation itself, yet common appeal designs fail to improve perceived fairness (Vaccaro et al., 2021). Beyond individual recourse, scholars argue for public contestability, supported by capacities to organise, separation of powers, and independent information (Cohen et al., 2024). These threads connect to broader accountability theory holding algorithms to administrative-law standards (Busuioc, 2020) and to the maturing concept of algorithmic regulation that governs both of and through algorithms (Ulbricht & Yeung, 2021).
