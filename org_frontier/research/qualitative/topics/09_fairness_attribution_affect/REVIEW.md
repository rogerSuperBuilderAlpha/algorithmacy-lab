# Perceived Fairness, Attribution, and Affect

When workers read an opaque system as fair or abusive, organizational justice theory supplies the dominant vocabulary, partitioning the reading into distributive, procedural, interpersonal, and informational dimensions (Starke et al., 2022; Ochmann et al., 2024). A foundational experimental finding is that identical decisions are evaluated differently by source: algorithmic and human managers fare equally on mechanical tasks, but algorithms are judged less fair and provoke more negative affect on tasks seen to require human judgment, with that negativity attributed to a dehumanizing experience of being assessed by a machine (Lee, 2018). The same asymmetry appears in moral framing, where algorithmic work is read as less authentic because it is presumed to lack moral sincerity (Jago, 2019).

Transparency, the obvious remedy, behaves unreliably. Disclosing functionalities and procedures does not lift informational fairness and shifts only distributive perceptions, a "transparency fallacy" (Mirbabaie et al., 2025). Beyond a threshold, added disclosure reverses, degrading fairness perceptions and amplifying resistance (Hu et al., 2024). Under opacity, riders abandon procedural reasoning and form fairness heuristically from salient distributive cues (Xiang et al., 2025).

Attribution turns on perceived agency. Workers and consumers assign less responsibility to systems credited with little mind, so blame for failures routes to the firm behind the algorithm (Srinivasan & Sarial-Abi, 2021). Anthropomorphism reweights this calculus, and its valence is conditional: human-like cues can raise interpersonal and informational justice (Ochmann et al., 2024) and buffer reactions to broken transactional promises by reducing perceived rigidity (Fousiani et al., 2026), yet the same cues backfire when an already-angry worker meets a humanized agent, sharpening expectancy violation and anger (Crolic et al., 2022). Attribution of human-like intentions also seeds psychological contracts with the platform, generating obligations the system was never built to honor (Sherman et al., 2025).

Affect, treated as an outcome of fairness reading, also constitutes it. The strongest recent contribution names "algorithmic paranoia": when management is experienced as non-transparent, arbitrary, and retaliatory, freelancers develop persecutory suspicion of human and non-human actors alike, coping through hypervigilance and pre-emptive self-protection (Alacovska et al., 2025). Paranoia reframes the abuse reading as an affective stance toward an opaque interested third party, the construct this program studies through integrated information. Open questions concern whether anthropomorphism is governable without eroding accountability (Fousiani et al., 2026), and how heuristic distributive inference (Xiang et al., 2025) interacts with persecutory affect when the system stays dark.
