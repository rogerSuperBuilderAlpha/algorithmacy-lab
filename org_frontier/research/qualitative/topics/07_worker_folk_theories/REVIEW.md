# Worker Folk Theories and Algorithmic Imaginaries

Workers managed by opaque algorithmic systems develop interpretive frameworks to act on machinery they cannot inspect. Two complementary concepts anchor this literature. Bucher's "algorithmic imaginary" describes how people imagine, perceive, and experience algorithms, and argues that these imaginations play a generative role in molding the systems themselves (Bucher, 2017). The "folk theory" tradition treats such understandings as intuitive, experience-based causal models that let users predict and influence opaque outputs even when technically inaccurate (Eslami et al., 2016; DeVito, 2021). Both ideas grew from social-media users and were extended to labor as algorithmic management spread (Lee et al., 2015).

A defining feature of worker theorization is its collective production. Bishop's study of YouTube beauty vloggers introduced "algorithmic gossip," the communal exchange of strategies for managing visibility under recommender systems (Bishop, 2019), and Cotter frames such understanding as practical knowledge situated between discourse and practice within a community (Cotter, 2024). DeVito shows that on changing platforms workers continually revise theories, treating adaptive folk theorization as a route to algorithmic literacy (DeVito, 2021). This communal sensemaking recurs across ridehail, delivery, freelancing, and crowdwork forums, where workers pool observations to reconstruct hidden incentive and matching logics (Sannon et al., 2022).

The generative claim, that imaginaries shape system behavior, is documented empirically in worker settings. Bucher et al. find that digital freelancers practice "anticipatory compliance," undervaluing work and staying under the radar to placate an assumed algorithm, thereby co-constructing the algorithm's power (Bucher et al., 2020). Cameron and Rahman show control and resistance are co-constituted: drivers' theories of the system drive both accommodation and evasion (Cameron and Rahman, 2022). Jhaver et al. document Airbnb hosts performing a "double negotiation" toward guests and toward partially transparent evaluative algorithms, with perceived opacity breeding anxiety and coping routines (Jhaver et al., 2018).

The affective texture of these theories has become a distinct focus. Alacovska et al. introduce "algorithmic paranoia," showing how adverse, arbitrary, and seemingly retaliatory management cultivates mistrust toward human and nonhuman platform actors and anticipations of harm (Alacovska et al., 2025). Participatory work responds by treating workers' imaginaries as design resources, surfacing information asymmetries and manipulative incentives that workers would redesign (Zhang et al., 2022).

Two gaps remain salient for the lab's coordination-through-opaque-intermediaries program. First, accuracy is rarely measured: most studies document that theories guide action without testing how well they track system mechanics, leaving the imaginary-to-behavior loop underspecified. Second, aggregation is undertheorized: how individually noisy folk theories combine through gossip into collectively load-bearing, sometimes self-fulfilling, models of an interested third party invites formal treatment.
