# Methods for Studying Opaque Algorithmic Systems

The methodological problem at the center of this literature is that algorithmic intermediaries are opaque: their code, training data, and ranking logic are hidden by trade secrecy, technical complexity, and continual change (Kitchin, 2017). Two broad responses have developed. The interpretive tradition treats opacity as an object to be studied ethnographically, while the audit tradition treats it as a parameter to be inferred through systematic querying.

Within the interpretive strand, Christin (2020) supplies the most cited toolkit, delineating three meso-level strategies for enrolling algorithms in fieldwork: refraction, which traces how software, people, and institutions reconfigure one another; comparison, a similarity-and-difference approach that surfaces an instrument's distinctive features; and triangulation, which uses algorithmic outputs to elicit richer qualitative data. The comparison strategy is demonstrated in Christin's (2017) multi-sited study of web journalism and criminal justice, where similar buffering practices (foot-dragging, gaming, open critique) reveal a "decoupling" between intended and actual algorithmic effects. Seaver (2017) frames algorithms "as culture," arguing that researchers should enact them as heterogeneous sociotechnical systems instead of pinning down a singular procedure or securing code access. Bucher (2017) shows that users' folk theories—the "algorithmic imaginary"—are themselves empirical traces of otherwise hidden systems, and Lange, Lenglet, and Seyfert (2019) extend ethnography to financial algorithms as "objects of ignorance." Rahman (2021) demonstrates how worker reactivity to an "invisible cage" of opaque evaluations becomes a window onto the system producing it.

The audit tradition formalizes external probing. Sandvig and colleagues (2014) catalogued code, sock-puppet, scraping, crowdsourced, and noninvasive audits, a taxonomy systematized by Metaxa and colleagues (2021) and reviewed empirically by Bandy (2021) across 62 studies. Lam and colleagues (2023) broaden this with "sociotechnical audits" that measure algorithm and user as mutually constituting. Recent work tempers audit optimism: Mosnar and colleagues (2025) document poor reproducibility and short-term validity in TikTok sock-puppet audits, given platform change and methodological sensitivity.

Convergence across traditions points toward triangulation as a unifying principle. Foster (2026) proposes multi-source triangulation with interpretable machine learning for structurally missing or adversarial data, formalizing the cross-signal consistency logic that underlies both ethnographic and audit-based approaches to opacity. DeVito (2021) similarly treats adaptive folk theorization as a method for tracking systems that change faster than any single observation can capture.
