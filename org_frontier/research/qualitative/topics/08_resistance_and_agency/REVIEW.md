# Resistance, Gaming, and Labour Agency

Algorithmic management governs platform labour through opaque, third-party intermediation, yet workers retain agency that surfaces in tactical workarounds, gaming, and out-of-app activity. Early fieldwork established the asymmetry that motivates such tactics: Uber's design withholds information and steers drivers while denying them the levers an employer relationship would imply (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016). Labour-process analysis confirmed that platform control is multi-faceted, combining a panoptic infrastructure, information asymmetry, and obfuscated performance metrics, and found mostly individual rather than collective expressions of agency (Veen et al., 2019).

A central insight is that control and resistance co-constitute one another. Workers have the most latitude early in the labour process and must shift tactics as their leverage narrows, while everyday customer interactions let them manipulate ratings the platform cannot fully observe (Cameron & Rahman, 2022). Gaming the system functions as everyday resistance in the Scottian sense: migrant couriers in Turin disarticulate and repurpose gamification both to meet survival needs and to seed nascent activism (Iazzolino et al., 2023), and migrant riders in China deploy a repertoire of dodge-, leverage-, and subvert-algorithm tactics inside a continuous "tug-of-war" with platform updates (Huang, 2025).

Several typologies organise this agency. Uber drivers split into "grinding" and "oppositional" player modes mapped onto distinct work games (Vasudevan & Chan, 2022); warehouse workers extend work-game tactics into physical, labour-tracking environments (Cheon et al., 2025); and freelancers pre-empt opaque algorithms through "anticipatory compliance" that paradoxically co-constructs algorithmic power (Bucher et al., 2021). Making-out varies systematically with how platforms distribute decision rights across three control contexts (Alasoini et al., 2023). Out-of-app activity warrants its own category: Swedish gig workers' "opportunistic agency" bypasses competitive bidding for instrumental gain (Öborn & Sandström, 2024), echoing the khep contractual rides that Dhaka drivers use to subvert allocation (Lata, 2025).

Resistance also reaches collective and Foucauldian registers. Gamification "from below" combines infrapolitics with strikes and unionisation among Indonesian couriers (Novianto, 2024), while drivers govern themselves through enduring, subverting, or exiting under the politics of demand (McDaid et al., 2023). A recent synthesis frames these informal and formal acts as resilience-building against algorithmic disruption (Williams et al., 2026). Across this work, agency appears partial and contingent: tactics open fissures in algorithmic power without dissolving it, and platforms iteratively reabsorb the workarounds they provoke.
