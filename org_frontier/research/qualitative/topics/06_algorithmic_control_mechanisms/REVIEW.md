# Mechanisms of Algorithmic Control at Work

The dominant framework for algorithmic control at work derives from labor process theory, recast for digital intermediaries. Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin (2020) extend Edwards' (1979) "contested terrain" thesis, arguing that algorithms function as a new arena of struggle between employers seeking to direct, evaluate, and discipline labor and workers seeking to resist. They consolidate the mechanisms into six instruments, the "6 Rs": restricting and recommending (direction), recording and rating (evaluation), and replacing and rewarding (discipline). Subsequent work has tested and extended this scheme. Hirsch and colleagues (2023) validate it through a mixed top-down/bottom-up design and propose a refined "5RSM" conceptualization applicable to both platform and traditional settings, signaling that the original taxonomy was empirically robust yet incomplete.

A second foundational strand locates control in information and power asymmetries. Rosenblat and Stark (2016) show that Uber's app produces asymmetries that are fundamental to indirect control: the platform sees driver behavior in fine detail while withholding the logics of pricing, dispatch, and deactivation. Möhlmann, Zalmanson, Henfridsson, and Gregory (2021) formalize a complementary distinction between algorithmic matching and algorithmic control, demonstrating how opacity in both generates tensions over work execution, compensation, and belonging. Rahman (2021) sharpens the evaluative mechanism with the "invisible cage": when rating criteria are opaque and shifting, workers cannot align behavior with success, producing reactivity ranging from experimentation to constrained activity.

Empirical studies map how these instruments vary and intensify. Griesbach, Reich, Elliott-Negri, and Milkman (2019) find cross-platform variation in food delivery, with Instacart exercising "algorithmic despotism" through stringent regulation of time and activity. Duggan, Carbery, McDonnell, and Sherman (2023) develop "algorithmic HRM control" as an omnipresent system distinguished by pervasive technology and the influence of non-organizational parties, chiefly rating customers.

The contested-terrain framing foregrounds resistance as constitutive of control. Cameron and Rahman (2022) show that latitude for resistance is highest early in the labor process and contracts at each subsequent stage, and that workers exploit visibility asymmetries to manipulate ratings. Bucher, Schou, and Waldkirch (2021) document "anticipatory compliance," whereby workers pacify the algorithm through undervaluing work and staying "under the radar," thereby co-constructing its power. Cini (2023) extends the analysis to collective mobilization across crowdwork and on-demand delivery.

Recent synthesis consolidates the field. Jarrahi and colleagues (2021) theorize opacity at technical and organizational levels and its effect on workplace power structures, Dedema and Rosenbaum (2024) catalog information asymmetry, power asymmetry, and system manipulation as core socio-technical issues, and Zhang and colleagues (2025) frame algorithmic management as a "contradictory unity" of control and autonomy, transparency and opacity.
