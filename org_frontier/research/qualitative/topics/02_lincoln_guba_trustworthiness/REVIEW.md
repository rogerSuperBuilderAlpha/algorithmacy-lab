# Lincoln-Guba Trustworthiness Criteria

The trustworthiness framework originates in Lincoln and Guba's *Naturalistic Inquiry*, which reframed positivist rigor standards into four parallel constructs suited to constructivist inquiry: credibility (paralleling internal validity), transferability (external validity), dependability (reliability), and confirmability (objectivity) (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). Each construct attaches to operational techniques: credibility through prolonged engagement, persistent observation, triangulation, peer debriefing, negative case analysis, and member checking; transferability through thick description that lets readers judge applicability; dependability through audit trails and external audit; confirmability through reflexivity and audit of the data-to-interpretation chain (Krefting, 1991; Shenton, 2004). A fifth set of authenticity criteria (fairness; ontological, educative, catalytic, and tactical authenticity) was added to address the transformative aims of constructivist evaluation (Amin et al., 2020; Johnson & Rasulova, 2017).

These constructs remain the dominant quality vocabulary across health, education, and social science. Shenton (2004) operationalized each criterion as a checklist for novice researchers, while Nowell et al. (2017) mapped the four criteria onto the phases of thematic analysis, specifying concrete documentation practices at each step. Application studies continue to demonstrate the framework's portability, including ethnographic work on indigenous social protection systems where the parallel criteria were systematically adapted (Enworo, 2023). Recent overviews consolidate the standard pairing of technique to criterion: triangulation and prolonged engagement for credibility, thick description for transferability, audit trails for dependability, and reflexive journaling with peer debriefing and member checking for confirmability (Ahmed, 2024).

The framework also draws sustained critique. Morse (2015) argues that the post-hoc, evaluative character of trustworthiness strategies displaces rigor to the end of inquiry, and recommends returning to the social-science terms reliability and validity built in during data construction. A science and technology studies reading contends that the four criteria retain residual realist assumptions and are not fully naturalistic, proposing reconstruction through STS accounts of scientific practice (Sharifzadeh, 2024). Member checking attracts particular scrutiny, since participant agreement may conflate validation with social comfort.

Transferability has seen the most active recent theoretical development. Stalmeijer et al. (2024) recast it as multidimensional, distinguishing applicability, resonance, and theoretical engagement and assigning reflexive techniques to each. The companion three-dimensional model formalizes these axes and warns against outcome-focused use of theory that confirms prior constructs (Brown et al., 2025). This trajectory signals a shift from treating the four criteria as a static checklist toward treating them as situated, reflexive judgments calibrated to a study's methodology and aims.
