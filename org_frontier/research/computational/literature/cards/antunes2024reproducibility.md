## Summary
This is a survey addressing how reproducible research can be effectively achieved within high-performance computing (HPC). It first untangles the contested terminology of reproducibility, replicability, and repeatability, documenting how the ACM swapped its definitions of "reproducibility" and "replicability" after 2020 (on NISO advice) to align computer science with the National Academies of Sciences definitions and other fields. It then surveys the "reproducibility crisis" across many disciplines (medicine, psychology, neuroscience, AI, networking, image processing, computer science itself), citing empirical reproducibility rates and notable scandals. The authors catalog general causes of reproducibility loss (open-science failures, poor documentation, statistics abuses like p-hacking and HARKing, scientific-culture incentives, software environments, workflow complexity, software-engineering gaps) and HPC-specific causes (parallel non-determinism plus floating-point non-associativity, PRNGs in parallel Monte Carlo, compiler optimization such as -O3/FMA/AVX, hardware heterogeneity, quantum computing, machine learning, and silent/soft errors). They then review proposed solutions: versioning/archiving (Git, Zenodo, Software Heritage), literate programming and FAIR principles, workflow systems, dependency managers (with Guix recommended), virtual machines vs. containers (with Apptainer/Singularity recommended for HPC over Docker), floating-point reproducibility algorithms, record-and-replay tools, and silent-error mitigation (ECC, redundancy, checksums, ML detectors). The paper closes with open problems and argues the main remaining obstacle is the scientific culture and epistemological training of computer scientists.

## Key facts it relies on
- ACM pre-2020 definitions (called D1): Repeatability = same team, same setup; Reproducibility = different team, different setup; Replicability = different team, same setup. After 2020 (D2, current), ACM swapped reproducibility and replicability so that Reproducibility = different team, same experimental setup (independent group obtains same result using the author's own artifacts) and Replicability = different team, different experimental setup (artifacts developed completely independently); the change followed NISO advice and matches the National Academies of Sciences (2019) definitions.
- In the authors' small literature sample, eight articles (published 2009-2019) used the old D1 definitions and four used the new D2 definitions; they note computer science was one of the only fields still using the pre-2020 definitions (per Barba, 2018), which was an argument for the ACM switch.
- Baker's 2016 Nature survey of 1576 scientists found ~90% believed there was a significant or slight reproducibility crisis and only 3% were convinced there was no crisis; Baker presented 14 contributing factors and 11 solutions.
- Reported reproducibility/replication rates cited: Collberg and Proebsting found only ~30% of 601 computer science papers reproducible; Errington et al. (2021) found a 46% success rate over 112 cancer-study attempts; Open Science Collaboration (2015) found psychology reproducibility of 30-50%; Stodden et al. (2018) obtained artifacts from only 44% of 204 papers and reproduced results in 26% of cases; Kurkowski et al. found <15% of MANET simulation papers reproducible; Vandewalle et al. examined 134 image-processing papers, finding 9% had code and 33% had data; Ioannidis (2015) claimed 85% of research funding is wasted.
- HPC reproducibility loss is driven by floating-point non-associativity combined with parallel non-determinism; exascale systems perform 10^18 floating-point operations per second, the Frontier supercomputer has 8,699,904 cores, and at this scale mean time before failure (MTBF) drops to a few hours. Hunold (2015) found 94% of parallel-computing researchers believed reproducibility of articles in that domain should be improved.
- For parallel Monte Carlo, the paper stresses PRNGs are fully deterministic and must have their initial statuses mastered/saved; it lists high-quality PRNGs (Philox/Threefry, MRG32k3a, WELL, PCG, Mersenne Twister with period 2^19937 - 1, SFMT with period up to 2^216091 - 1) and warns against seeding with time(NULL); Hellekalek (1998) warned "Don't trust parallel Monte Carlo."
- Silent/soft errors: Schroeder et al. (2009) found DRAM error rates of 25,000-70,000 errors per billion device hours per Mbit and that over 8% of DIMMs experience errors annually, with hard errors dominating; Baumann (2005) estimated ~50000 FIT for non-ECC SRAM and ECC reduces error rate by 1000x; Wang et al. (2018) neural-network detector identified >89% of silent data corruptions with <2% false-positive rate.
- Recommended tooling: Guix is presented as "probably the best tool for handling dependencies" (functional package manager, hash-identified builds), and Apptainer (formerly Singularity) is recommended for HPC containers because it runs with user privileges (no daemon/root, unlike Docker which needs admin privileges and raises security concerns) and supports GPU, MPI, and InfiniBand; multiple studies found containers (Singularity/Shifter/Charliecloud) achieve native or near-native performance while Type 2 hypervisor VMs (e.g., VirtualBox) are unsuitable for HPC.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper takes explicit normative positions: the authors argue that for classical deterministic computing the ACM repeatability definition's "stated precision" is inappropriate because bitwise-identical results are needed for debugging, and they assert that open-source Linux is "mandatory" for reproducible research (excluding Windows/macOS) — these are stated as the authors' opinions/recommendations rather than field consensus.
- The authors present the opposition fairly but disagree with it: Drummond (2018, 2019) argues sharing source code is unnecessary, is driven by intuition not facts, and that prioritizing replication over novelty is harmful; Fanelli (2018) argues the reproducibility crisis is exaggerated or false and demotivates young researchers. Barba (2018) criticized Drummond's 2009 definitions as "essentially flawed."
- The survey is explicitly HPC-focused and acknowledges trade-offs rather than universal fixes: achieving repeatability in HPC often costs performance (disabling AVX/FMA/-O3 optimizations), and the authors concede that heavily optimized large parallel simulations remain the main unsolved reproducibility problem; containers are noted to be designed for deployment not archiving, so even identical Dockerfiles may yield slightly different images.
- Several cited rates and overheads are drawn from individual prior studies of varying scope (e.g., record-and-replay tool overheads ranging from 1.6x to 36-147x; VM/container overhead figures), and the paper itself flags that an up-to-date study of Apptainer would be useful to confirm continued near-native performance.

Reproducibility / replicability / repeatability definitions; ACM and NISO standardization; reproducibility crisis across disciplines; open science and artifact sharing; documentation; statistics (p-hacking, HARKing, p-values); scientific culture and publish-or-perish; software environments and dependency hell; scientific workflows; software engineering; parallel computing and floating-point non-associativity; PRNGs and parallel Monte Carlo; compiler optimization (FMA, AVX, -O3); hardware heterogeneity; quantum computing reproducibility; machine learning and GPUs; silent/soft errors and bit flips; versioning and archiving (Git, Zenodo, Software Heritage); literate programming (Jupyter, RStudio, Org-mode, Sweave); FAIR principles; workflow systems (Galaxy, Nextflow, Pegasus, Snakemake, etc.); dependency managers (apt, pip, Conda, venv, Nix, Guix); virtual machines and hypervisors; containers (Docker, Apptainer/Singularity, Charliecloud, Shifter, Sarus, Podman); floating-point reproducibility algorithms; record-and-replay debugging tools; ECC/redundancy/checksums for silent errors; HPC; exascale computing.
