## Summary
The paper asks whether human groups have a robust, measurable general factor of collective intelligence (CI) analogous to individual "g," and what predicts it. The authors accumulate data from 22 studies (5,279 individuals in 1,356 groups of 2 to 7 people) all run on a single online platform (POGS, the Platform for Online Group Studies) administering a battery of up to eight task types, then apply two-stage meta-analytic confirmatory factor analysis to test for a single CI factor. They find strong support for a one-factor CI structure (standardized loadings 0.27 to 0.52, average variance extracted 19.6%), and CI scores computed from any seven tasks predict performance on the held-out eighth task (average leave-one-out Pearson r = 0.40). Exploring predictors, they find CI is most strongly predicted by group collaboration process (skill congruence, strategy, effort), followed by individual member skill, then group composition; proportion of women predicts CI, mediated by average social perceptiveness, while age diversity has a negative effect. They argue CI captures a group's capability (potential) rather than a single performance outcome, and that the relative weight of process versus individual skill varies systematically by task type.

## Key facts it relies on
- Dataset: 22 studies, 5,279 individuals in 1,356 groups, with group sizes ranging from 2 to 7 individuals; all administered via the POGS platform across populations from university students to crowd workers, military personnel, and online gamers.
- Three task types (Brainstorm Object, Typing Text, Unscramble Words) were used in all studies; six studies administered all eight task types; the largest single study (Field Sample 1) had 254 groups and administered seven tasks.
- Average interitem correlation across tasks was 0.27 (range 0.12 to 0.50); single-factor model standardized loadings ranged 0.27 to 0.52 (all P < 0.001), with average variance extracted of 19.6%.
- Leave-one-out cross-validation: CI scores computed from seven tasks predict the left-out task with average Pearson r = 0.40 (range 0.26 to 0.53; all P < 0.001), with effect sizes between medium and large.
- CI scores are computed as the average weighted z-score of all tasks (using meta-analytic factor loadings), then multiplied by 15 and added to 100, by convention with individual intelligence tests.
- Three collaboration process measures (skill congruence, strategy, effort) were derived from POGS process data, drawing on Hackman's seminal group-performance work; skill congruence and strategy are strong positive predictors of performance, while effort is not a significant predictor.
- Random forest variable-importance analysis: the largest share of CI variance is explained by collaboration process measures, then individual member skill (group mean and maximum), then group size, then social perceptiveness, then group composition (proportion female and age diversity).
- Process-vs-skill weight varies by task: over 51% of explained variation on Sudoku is due to individual member skill, whereas 55% of variation on Unscrambling Words is due to group processes.
- Proportion of women significantly predicts CI, mediated by average social perceptiveness (measured via the Reading the Mind in the Eyes test); age diversity has a negative effect on CI.
- The work builds directly on Woolley et al. (2010, Science 330, 686-688), where a single factor predicted over 40% of the variance across tasks; data and replication code are deposited in Harvard Dataverse (doi 10.7910/DVN/RAKAOG).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors acknowledge that prior studies have questioned the CI-factor results and reported disparate findings on the strength of individual skill in predicting group CI (citing Credé & Howardson 2017, Barlow & Dennis 2016, Bates & Gupta 2017); they argue these divergences are likely explained by task selection rather than absence of a CI factor.
- The predictor analysis (group composition, process, skill predicting CI) is explicitly described as exploratory, not confirmatory.
- The authors note that methodological choices constraining group interaction (e.g., restricting how many members record answers) would obscure group capability on process-reliant tasks and depress inter-task correlations, implying CI estimates are sensitive to study design.
- They concede there is still no detailed causal model of the mechanisms underlying CI, paralleling the unresolved biological basis of individual "g"; the CI factor remains a statistical observation requiring further theory-building.
- Scope: all data come from one platform (POGS) with computer-mediated tasks; the paper frames CI as capability/potential distinct from realized field performance, which is influenced by outside factors such as competition, opportunities, and resources.
