## Summary
This is the introductory/editorial article to a topical collection (Philosophical Psychology 38:5) on the foundations of joint action. The authors survey current debates over *minimal* forms of shared (or collective) intentionality, arguing that progress on the nature of collective intentional phenomena requires starting from the simpler forms that young children, and presumably our hominin ancestors, could realize. They review the phylogenetic origins of human cooperation, developmental evidence (joint attention, declarative pointing, the still-face effect) that infants engage in shared intentionality without cognitively demanding capacities, and the role of shared intentionality in social cognition and communication. They then mount a three-part critique of "standard accounts" of collective intentionality (Bratman, Gilbert, Searle): these accounts assume overly sophisticated cognitive capacities (e.g., common knowledge/recursive mindreading), face circularity and infinite-regress problems, and omit implicit forms such as a tacit "sense of commitment." The article closes with summaries of the seven contributions to the special issue (Butterfill, Satne, Salice, Schmitz, Zahavi, Vincini, Seemann).

## Key facts it relies on
- The paper is an introduction to a topical collection in *Philosophical Psychology*, 2025, vol. 38, no. 5, pp. 1897–1908; open access (CC-BY), published online 22 Apr 2025.
- Phylogenetic framing: approximately 400,000 years ago, *homo heidelbergensis* was presumably able to join a conspecific to hunt a deer and share the success and meal (Tomasello, 2016), before any large linguistic cultural group existed; the Cooperative Foraging Hypothesis (Sterelny, 2012) treats collective hunting of megafauna as a decisive evolutionary advantage.
- Candidate grounds for human cooperation discussed: inherited kin-based altruism (Henrich & Muthukrishna, 2021), mind-shaping (Zawidzki, 2013), and mind-reading (Chung, 2021).
- Developmental contrast: human infants point declaratively simply to share attention (no extrinsic reward), whereas chimpanzees and other primates only point imperatively to obtain food/objects (Tomasello, 2019); this distinguishes humans from other primates early.
- The Dyadic Consciousness Hypothesis (Tronick et al., 1998) posits a "dyadic state of consciousness" and predicts infant distress when the global interactive event is interrupted; this grounds the still-face literature, in which infants from about 2–3 months of age react with frowning, gazing away, losing postural control when an adult abruptly stops interacting (Li et al., 2019; Mesman et al., 2009).
- Three critical perspectives against standard accounts (Bratman 1993; Gilbert 1990; Searle 1990): (1) they assume sophisticated capacities (common knowledge requiring recursive mindreading) that infants/young children lack; (2) they face circularity and infinite regress — Bratman's content presupposes collective intentions (Petersson, 2007), Gilbert's joint commitments are themselves joint actions and his plural subject requires a joint commitment requiring a plural subject (Tollefsen, 2002; Tuomela, 1992); (3) they leave out implicit forms such as a "sense of commitment" (Michael et al., 2016).
- Proposed remedies to circularity/regress: affective "precursors" such as "plural self-awareness" (Schmid, 2014) or a "minimal sense of us" (Crone, 2021).
- Communication is framed as a joint "social act" (Cornejo 2008; Eilan 2020; Husserl 1973; Schmid 2005): an individual gesture is a "communicative bid" (Vasil et al., 2020) that becomes communication only when reciprocated through uptake.
- Author contributions are specified by page: KC pp. 3–5, MG pp. 1–3, SV pp. 6–8; Vincini's contribution was supported by the Alexander von Humboldt Foundation (Jan 2021–Mar 2023).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper itself notes that Tomasello and colleagues have been criticized for claiming that shared intentionality requires multi-level recursive mindreading, since infants who lack such capacity clearly participate in joint attention; it cites further objections to Tomasello's approach in De Waal (2009) and Moll et al. (2020).
- This is a survey/editorial, not an empirical or formal study: it advances no new experiments, datasets, or theorems of its own; its claims are programmatic and rest on cited prior work.
- The authors flag deep unresolved theoretical disagreement in the field — an "ever-growing number and mounting diversity of accounts" (Butterfill & Sinigaglia, 2022) — and the contributors disagree among themselves (e.g., Butterfill defends plural, aggregate, and reductive strategies as each viable; Zahavi argues token-identical shared-emotion views are "incoherent and metaphysically problematic").
- The phylogenetic claims (e.g., *homo heidelbergensis* hunting cooperation ~400,000 years ago) are explicitly presented as presumptive/hypothetical ("presumably," "might have been"), grounded in secondary sources rather than direct evidence.
