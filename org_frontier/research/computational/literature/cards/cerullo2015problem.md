## Summary
This Perspective paper argues that Tononi's Integrated Information Theory (IIT), which equates consciousness with integrated information (Φ), fails at its stated goal of quantifying consciousness. Cerullo's central theoretical objection is that IIT rests on what he calls the "principle of information exclusion" (the conjecture that what is absent in perception is more significant to consciousness than what is present), which Tononi treats as self-evident but never justifies with arguments beyond intuition. To challenge IIT's empirical support, the author constructs a deliberately trivial rival, "Circular Coordinated Message Theory" (CCMT), based on information circulation in feedback loops, and shows it reproduces IIT's predictions for split-brain syndrome and the cerebellum, undermining IIT's claim to explanatory power. He further argues that because IIT rejects computational functionalism, it is vulnerable to fading/dancing qualia arguments, and that because intuitively nonconscious systems (photodiodes, XOR gate grids, expander graphs) can have arbitrarily high Φ, IIT measures "protoconsciousness" (panexperientialism) rather than the cognitive consciousness studied by neuroscientists. The paper concludes that IIT at best addresses Chalmers and Aaronson's "Pretty Hard Problem" of consciousness, not the hard problem, and that it is a "theory of partial-panexperientialism" that does not help understand or predict subjective experience.

## Key facts it relies on
- IIT, developed by Giulio Tononi over the last decade, postulates that consciousness is equal to integrated information (Φ); Tononi defines integrated information as "the amount of information generated by a complex of elements, above and beyond the information generated by its parts" (Tononi 2008, p. 216).
- The author names IIT's principal theoretical justification the "principle of information exclusion" — the conjecture that in perception what is absent may be more significant to consciousness than what is present — and argues Tononi gives no argument for it beyond calling it self-evident.
- In IIT 3.0 (Oizumi, Albantakis & Tononi 2014), Tononi attempts to derive eight postulates from five axioms claimed to come from phenomenology; information exclusion becomes the third axiom, the "axiom of information."
- The author proposes a deliberately trivial rival theory, Circular Coordinated Message Theory (CCMT), whose quantity Omicron (O) is the information circulation of the system as a whole minus that of a minimal partition; he shows CCMT makes the same predictions as IIT for split-brain syndrome and the cerebellum.
- Tononi states that after a "callosal" cut the corticothalamic complex splits into two separate complexes, but because of redundancy between hemispheres their Φ value is "not greatly reduced" compared to forming a single complex (Tononi 2008, p. 223).
- Aaronson showed an expander graph generates arbitrarily large Φ, and he and Tononi agreed that a network of XOR gates arranged in an n×n square grid could generate √n·Φ, so a simple XOR-gate arrangement performing no complex information processing can have higher Φ than the human brain.
- IIT rejects computational functionalism: Tononi claims "whether a system is conscious or not cannot be decided based on its input-output behavior only" (Tononi 2014, p. 21), that a feed-forward program running the same computations as a brain would not be conscious, and that IIT allows functionally identical philosophical zombies.
- Tononi states IIT implies even a binary photodiode "enjoys exactly 1 bit of consciousness" with a quale captured by a single q-arrow of length 1 bit (Tononi 2008, p. 236).
- The author classifies IIT as a form of panexperientialism (citing Kind, Rosenberg, Strawson), distinguished from panpsychism in that it attributes experience without cognition/mind; he labels IIT's target "protoconsciousness" or "noncognitive-consciousness," contrasting it with Aaronson's "cognitive-consciousness."
- Schwitzgebel's critique is invoked: under IIT plus the axiom of exclusion, a person would immediately lose consciousness if a tiny organism were incorporated into their brain, because the organism-plus-brain system would form a larger conscious entity taking priority over the brain alone.

## Critical notes from the literature
- This is a Perspective/critique paper, not original empirical or mathematical work; its arguments are largely philosophical and depend on attributing the "principle of information exclusion" to Tononi as IIT's load-bearing (and, the author claims, unjustified) assumption — a framing Tononi did not himself adopt.
- The author concedes the CCMT rival is "trivial" by design; its purpose is to show IIT's split-brain and cerebellum predictions are not uniquely IIT's, not to advance CCMT as a serious theory.
- The author acknowledges Aaronson and Tononi may be using "consciousness" in different senses (cognitive vs. noncognitive), so their claims about photodiodes and XOR grids "are not incompatible," meaning the disagreement may be partly terminological rather than a clean refutation.
- The paper notes that neither IIT, computational functionalism, nor identity theory solves Chalmers's hard problem (why any physical process is conscious); IIT is said to address only the "Pretty Hard Problem" (predicting which systems are conscious), so the critique does not claim IIT is uniquely deficient on the hard problem.
- The author had previously published a related critique (Cerullo 2011, "Integrated Information Theory: A Promising but Ultimately Incomplete Theory of Consciousness"), indicating this is a continuation of his own prior position rather than a neutral survey.
