## Summary
This paper presents version 3.0 of Integrated Information Theory (IIT) of consciousness, a principled framework that proceeds from the phenomenology of experience to the physical mechanisms that could generate it. IIT begins with five phenomenological axioms taken as self-evident truths about consciousness — existence, composition, information, integration, and exclusion — and translates each into a corresponding ontological postulate specifying conditions that physical mechanisms (such as neurons or logic gates) in a state must satisfy to generate experience. From these postulates the authors build a step-by-step, bottom-up mathematical formalism applied first at the level of individual mechanisms (yielding "concepts" defined by maximally irreducible cause-effect repertoires, MICE, with quantity phi^Max) and then at the level of systems of mechanisms (yielding a maximally irreducible conceptual structure, MICS or "quale," with quantity Phi^Max). The central identity proposed is that an experience is identical to a MICS, whose constellation of concepts specifies the quality of the experience and whose Phi^Max value specifies its quantity. The framework is illustrated throughout with small deterministic logic-gate networks (OR, AND, XOR), using the earth mover's distance (EMD) as the distance measure in concept space. From the theory the authors derive several results: systems can condense into a major complex plus non-overlapping minor complexes; simple systems can be minimally conscious while complicated systems can be unconscious; a complex can generate a MICS even if its elements are inactive; and feed-forward systems functionally equivalent to conscious complexes can be unconscious "zombies."

## Key facts it relies on
- IIT 3.0 is built on five phenomenological axioms — existence, composition, information, integration, exclusion — each translated into a corresponding ontological postulate about physical mechanisms; axioms are self-evident truths that "with Descartes, cannot be doubted and do not need proof," whereas postulates are unproven assumptions about the physical substrates of consciousness.
- The paper situates 3.0 relative to prior versions: IIT 1.0 (the original formulation) dealt exclusively with stationary systems, and IIT 2.0 showed how integrated information could be calculated in a top-down manner for a system in a state; 3.0 is described as a substantial advance at several different levels.
- A concept is defined as a maximally irreducible cause-effect repertoire (MICE, or quale sensu stricto) specified by a single mechanism, with an associated integration/irreducibility value phi^Max ("small phi"); a complex generates a maximally irreducible conceptual structure (MICS, or quale sensu lato) with value Phi^Max ("big phi").
- The central identity of IIT: an experience is identical to the MICS generated by a complex of elements in a state — the constellation of concepts specifies the quality of the experience and its irreducibility Phi^Max specifies its quantity, and the structure exists intrinsically (from its own intrinsic perspective) without need of an external observer.
- Cause-effect information (cei) of a mechanism is defined as the minimum of cause information (ci) and effect information (ei) — the "intrinsic information bottleneck principle"; in the worked ABC example with state 100, ci = 0.33, ei = 0.25, so cei = min(ci, ei) = 0.25.
- Distance between probability distributions is measured with the earth mover's distance (EMD), also known as the Wasserstein distance, which serves as the metric of concept space in IIT 3.0 (Box 1, Distance D).
- Integrated information phi of a mechanism is computed across the minimum information partition (MIP), the partition that makes the least difference to the cause and effect repertoires; in the worked ABC example phi_cause^MIP = 0.5 and phi_effect^MIP = 0.25, giving phi^MIP = min = 0.25.
- The exclusion postulate enforces a "causal version of Occam's razor" — only one cause and one effect (the maximally irreducible ones) exist; for the neuron example the core cause turns out to be "the strong synapses S1,S2," and at the system level only one set of elements (a complex, the maximum of Phi^Max over elements, space, and time) can be conscious.
- For the candidate set ABC the concept space has dimension 16 (8 axes for past states and 8 for future states); of the power set of ABC's mechanisms, only AC fails to generate a concept because its phi^Max = 0, while all others generate non-zero concepts (Figure 10).
- Background conditions, candidate set, transition probability matrix (TPM), purview, power set, and null concept (the unconstrained cause-effect repertoire p^uc with phi = 0) are formally defined building blocks (Box 1); the TPM is obtained by perturbing the system into all its possible states.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly note that IIT's approach is "the opposite of the approach usually taken in neuroscience": rather than starting from neural mechanisms and asking which give rise to consciousness, IIT takes phenomenology as primary — a methodological stance they defend but acknowledge differs from standard neural-correlates-of-consciousness research.
- The formalism is demonstrated only on small, simple systems of discrete logic gates / linear threshold units (e.g., the six-element ABCDEF / three-element ABC examples); the paper presents these as illustrations of principle rather than large-scale applications, and many computations rely on full perturbation of the system into all possible states (exponential cost).
- The choice of EMD as the distance measure in concept space is presented as a choice motivated by the intrinsic perspective; the paper defers detailed justification and discussion of EMD as "the current distance measure of choice" to the Supplementary Material (Text S2), indicating it is an assumption rather than a derived necessity.
- The postulates themselves are, by the authors' own definition, "unproven assumptions" about the physical substrates of consciousness; the entire mechanistic framework rests on accepting these postulates as the correct formalization of the phenomenological axioms.
- A striking and potentially counterintuitive consequence the authors flag is that a feed-forward system can be functionally equivalent to a conscious complex yet be an unconscious "zombie," and that a complex can be conscious even when its elements are inactive — claims that follow from the formalism but are presented as results to be taken on the theory's own terms.
