## Summary
This short letter, signed by a large consortium of consciousness researchers, argues that Integrated Information Theory (IIT) should be labeled pseudoscience and pushes back against media coverage that celebrated IIT as a "leading" and empirically tested theory of consciousness. The authors note that this coverage sprang from a public event in which the authors of a large-scale adversarial collaboration shared findings reported as empirically testing and partially supporting IIT, communicated directly to journalists and the public prior to the preprint being available and thus prior to peer review. The letter's central methodological objection is that, by design, those studies only tested idiosyncratic predictions made by certain theorists that are not logically related to the core ideas of IIT, so the findings do not support claims that the theory itself was meaningfully tested or holds a "dominant" or "well-established" status. The authors contend that, given IIT's panpsychist commitments and what they characterize as untestability of the theory as a whole, the pseudoscience label should apply until the full theory is empirically testable. They emphasize that IIT's perceived status carries ethical stakes for clinical practice with coma patients, AI sentience, stem cell/organoid research, and abortion debates, and frame their statement as a duty to protect the public from scientific misinformation. The piece is explicitly described as a non-final draft, originally intended as a "Letter to the Editor" for a general-public audience and being revised into a longer article.

- The media coverage that prompted the letter sprang from a public event where authors of a large-scale adversarial collaboration shared findings reported as empirically testing and partially supporting IIT; the message was communicated to journalists and the public prior to the preprint being available, and hence prior to peer review.
- The core methodological claim: "by design the studies only tested some idiosyncratic predictions made by certain theorists, which are not really logically related to the core ideas of IIT," and one of the authors himself acknowledges this.
- The letter states that according to IIT, "an inactive grid of connected logic gates that are not performing any useful computation can be conscious—possibly even more so than humans."
- It claims that according to the theory, organoids created out of petri-dishes, as well as human fetuses at very early stages of development, are likely conscious, and that on some interpretations even plants may be conscious.
- IIT has been labeled by some scientists as pseudoscience, and has been called "untestable, unscientific, 'magicalist', or a 'departure from science as we know it'."
- The authors argue the pseudoscience label should apply "until the theory as a whole—not just some hand-picked auxiliary components trivially shared by many others or already known to be true—is empirically testable."
- The authors state their consensus "is not that IIT and its variants decidedly lack intellectual merit," but that a fair and truthful perspective on the theory's status is essential given the stakes.
- The letter lists ethical implications of IIT being proven or perceived as true: direct impact on clinical practice for coma patients, AI sentience and its regulation, stem cell, animal and organoid testing, and abortion.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors explicitly state this is "not the final version" and a draft being revised following community feedback, so its arguments are presented as provisional rather than fully developed.
- The letter itself acknowledges that the signatories' consensus is not that IIT "decidedly lack[s] intellectual merit," tempering the strong "pseudoscience" framing.
- The piece is a position/opinion statement (a Letter to the Editor for the general public), not an empirical study or systematic review; its claims about what IIT predicts rest on cited prior work rather than original analysis.
- The letter notes that claims of IIT's "dominance" have been questioned in the scientific community (citing prior surveys and bibliometric work) but have been repeatedly broadcast to the public by IIT proponents.
- A related cited reference raises financial conflicts of interest in an open letter to the ARC-COGITATE research group, indicating contested context around the adversarial collaboration that triggered the media coverage.
