## Summary
The paper asks whether and how oscillatory synchronization emerges between two brains during genuine, live social interaction, rather than during isolated perception or externally-paced coordination. Using a dual-video plus dual-EEG hyperscanning setup, the authors recorded 9 dyads of adults engaged in spontaneous imitation of meaningless hand movements, where partners freely alternated the roles of model and imitator. Fine-grained video coding labeled each moment as behaviorally synchronous/non-synchronous and imitative/non-imitative, and interbrain phase synchronization was quantified with the phase locking value (PLV) between every pair of electrodes across the two helmets, using nonlinear methods and cluster-based permutation statistics. The central finding is that states of interactional synchrony correlate with the emergence of an interbrain synchronizing network in the alpha-mu band (8-12 Hz) between the right centroparietal regions of both partners, with these regions acting symmetrically as functional hubs. In higher frequency bands (beta, gamma) the synchronization became asymmetrical between model and imitator, interpreted as a top-down modulation reflecting the two roles. A surrogate-data control confirmed the alpha-mu effect was tied to the actual behavioral timing, and the authors frame this as the first dual-EEG demonstration of brain-to-brain coupling during spontaneous nonverbal social interaction.

## Key facts it relies on
- 22 healthy young adults (5 female-female and 6 male-male pairs) of mean age 24.5 years (SD = 2.8) participated; two dyads were excluded (exceeding 3 SD from the mean symmetry index), leaving 9 dyads (18 participants) for analysis.
- EEG was recorded with two 32-active-electrode Acticap helmets (10/20 system), digitized at 500 Hz with both subjects on the same amplifier to guarantee millisecond-range synchrony; four electrodes (T7, T8, CP9, CP10) were rejected for artifacts.
- Four analyzed frequency bands: theta (4-7 Hz), alpha-mu (8-12 Hz), beta (13-30 Hz), and gamma (31-48 Hz).
- Interbrain coupling was measured by the phase locking value (PLV), equal to 1 for perfect phase locking and 0 for total desynchronization (one minus the circular variance of phase differences), computed on 800 ms windows for each cross-helmet electrode pair; cluster statistics were corrected for multiple comparisons via nonparametric permutation, controlling FWER at p = 0.05.
- Inter-observer reliability (Cohen's kappa, on 25% of recordings): 0.83 for imitation, 0.91 for synchrony, 0.82 for model/imitator roles; mean role-symmetry index across the 9 dyads was near zero (M = 0.02, SD = 0.14), indicating good turn-taking.
- Behaviorally during spontaneous imitation, participants spent M = 64.69% of time imitating and were synchronized M = 77.93% of the time (synchrony-with-imitation = 51.27%).
- Sync vs. NSync contrast: symmetrical alpha-mu PLV increase between right parietal regions of model (CP6, P8) and imitator (CP6, P4, P8) (CS = +6.7, p<0.05); beta cluster between model central region (FC1, Cz) and imitator parieto-occipital region (CS = +6.4, p<0.05); gamma cluster between a wide model frontal-central area and imitator parietal area (CS = +17.4, p<0.001).
- Surrogate-data test (shuffling behavioral Sync/NSync timing) confirmed the experimental PLV contrast exceeded surrogate for the alpha-mu rhythm (Wilcoxon T = 5, p<0.05).
- Imitative vs. non-imitative (Im vs. NIm) episodes showed no significant interbrain differences (CS<5.0, p>0.05); Induced Imitation vs. No-View-Motion baseline yielded a significant effect only in theta, a symmetrical PLV increase between the two right parieto-occipital regions (CS = +19.0, p<0.005).

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors situate their contribution against prior hyperscanning work that did not achieve true interbrain coupling during live interaction: Tognoli et al. (2007) detected the "phi complex" (9.2-11.5 Hz) per-subject but did not directly test interbrain synchronization; Lindenberger et al. (2009) found theta phase synchronization between guitarists but coordination was mediated by an external metronome.
- Scope is narrow: a single nonverbal imitation paradigm with only 9 analyzed dyads of right-handed young adults; results are scalp-EEG cluster statistics, not source-localized, so anatomical attributions (e.g., right temporoparietal junction) are inferred from prior fMRI literature rather than measured here.
- The paper notes its own interpretive caveat that the theta-only effect in the Induced Imitation vs. baseline contrast may arise because subjects moved hands continuously in both conditions, eliminating motor regions from the contrast; the authors explicitly state "far more work is needed."
- The asymmetry in higher bands is interpreted as top-down model/imitator role modulation, but this is offered as a possibility ("possibly reflecting") consistent with prior beta/gamma literature, not a directly tested causal claim.
