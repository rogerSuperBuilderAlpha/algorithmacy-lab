## Summary
This document is a National Academies brief, "Improving Reproducibility and Replicability in Research," that summarizes highlights from three reports, with its primary section covering the congressionally requested report *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science* (2019). The central contribution is a terminological distinction: reproducibility is defined narrowly as computational reproducibility—obtaining consistent computational results using the same input data, computational steps, methods, code, and conditions of analysis—while replicability means obtaining consistent results across studies that each collect their own new data to answer the same question. The brief argues that, given transparent reporting and available digital artifacts (data and code), results should be computationally reproducible, whereas even a rigorously conducted study may fail to replicate. It distinguishes "potentially helpful" sources of non-replicability (inherent, uncharacterized uncertainty in the system, intrinsic natural variation, limits of current knowledge and technology) from "unhelpful" ones (flaws in design, conduct, or communication arising from lack of knowledge, perverse incentives, sloppiness, or bias). The report recommends concrete steps for researchers, funders, journals, and the National Science Foundation to improve rigor, transparency, and the sharing of digital artifacts. All three summarized reports emphasize that openness and transparency about methods, data, and code are crucial to reliable, trusted science. The other two reports covered are *Open Science by Design* (2018) and *Fostering Integrity in Research* (2017).

## Key facts it relies on
- Reproducibility is defined as computational reproducibility: obtaining consistent computational results using the same input data, computational steps, methods, code, and conditions of analysis.
- Replicability is defined as obtaining consistent results across studies aimed at answering the same scientific question, each of which has obtained its own data; reproducing uses original data and code, while replicating involves new data collection with similar methods.
- A successful replication does not guarantee the original results were correct, nor does a single failed replication conclusively refute the original claims.
- Non-replicability has "potentially helpful" sources (inherent but uncharacterized uncertainties, intrinsic variation/complexity in nature, scope of current knowledge, limits of current technology) that are a normal part of science and may lead to new discoveries.
- Non-replicability has "unhelpful" sources (shortcomings in design, conduct, and communication, from lack of knowledge, perverse incentives, sloppiness, or bias) that reduce the efficiency of scientific progress.
- The report recommends researchers include a clear, specific, complete description of methods/instruments/materials/procedures/measurements; data analysis and exclusion/inclusion decisions; analytic decisions and whether the study is exploratory or confirmatory; expected constraints on generality; reporting of precision or statistical power; and discussion of uncertainty.
- The National Science Foundation (NSF) should facilitate transparent sharing of digital artifacts (data and code) for NSF-funded studies, including developing criteria for trusted open repositories and endorsing/considering code and data repositories for long-term archiving.
- This brief consolidates three reports: *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science* (2019), *Open Science by Design* (2018), and *Fostering Integrity in Research* (2017); *Open Science by Design* invokes the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The PDF itself is a short highlights brief, not the full ~200-page consensus report; numeric findings, formal report findings/recommendations numbering, and detailed disciplinary evidence are summarized rather than reproduced here.
- The report explicitly acknowledges that failure to replicate is not necessarily a problem: some non-replicability is a normal, even productive part of science (intrinsic variation, frontier uncertainty), complicating any simple "replication crisis" narrative.
- *Open Science by Design* notes persistent barriers—cost of open publication and data, and a culture/incentive system that inadequately rewards (and may discourage) open science practices.
- *Fostering Integrity in Research* notes that current transparency standards in many fields do not adequately support reproducibility/replicability, data practices vary by field, and professional rewards for preparing data and code for release are minimal.
