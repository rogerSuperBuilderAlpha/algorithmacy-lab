## Summary
This opinion paper philosophically examines the axiomatic method that underpins the integrated information theory (IIT) of consciousness, asking whether IIT's foundations are secure given its influence as a guiding framework for neural-basis research and consciousness-meter development. Bayne first analyzes what the "axiomatic method" involves, noting it is open to multiple interpretations of how axioms relate to IIT's postulates (deductive, abductive, epistemic, or merely heuristic). He then examines each of IIT's five axioms in turn (intrinsic existence, composition, information, integration, and exclusion), asking for each whether it means, whether it is genuinely self-evident/axiomatic, and whether it could impose a substantive constraint on a theory of consciousness. His central argument is a recurring dilemma: for each axiom, interpretations that plausibly qualify as self-evident fail to impose a substantive constraint, while interpretations that impose a substantive constraint are not plausibly self-evident. He concludes that none of the five alleged axioms can play the role IIT requires and that "the axiomatic foundations of IIT are shaky." The paper closes by sketching an alternative methodology, the natural kind approach, which treats consciousness like gold, water, or hepatitis by identifying clustering signs and the mechanisms that explain them.

## Key facts it relies on
- IIT in its examined form appeals to exactly **five axioms** (Oizumi et al. 2014): intrinsic existence, composition, information, integration, and exclusion; the paper treats each one separately.
- IIT defines axioms as "self-evident truths about consciousness—the only truths that, with Descartes, cannot be doubted and do not need proof" (Oizumi et al. 2014, 2); Tononi and Koch (2015, 5) add that ideally axioms are essential, complete, consistent, and independent.
- IIT sharply distinguishes axioms from postulates, where postulates are "assumptions, derived from axioms, about the physical substrates of consciousness ... which can be formalized and form the basis of the mathematical framework of IIT" (Oizumi et al. 2014, 4); the IIT literature describes the transition as "formalization," "translation," or "derivation."
- IIT is presented as a theory of phenomenal consciousness aiming to account for "what it is like" to "perceive a scene, to endure pain, [and] to entertain a thought" (Tononi et al. 2016, 450), and is ontologically reductive, claiming consciousness just is integrated information.
- Bayne notes IIT's panpsychist commitment, citing Tononi's claim that "even a binary photodiode is not completely unconscious" (Tononi 2008, 236), and uses this to challenge the composition and information axioms (e.g., why a simple entity's experience could not be unstructured).
- The information axiom is illustrated by Tononi's (2008) photodiode example: a photodiode discriminating between two alternatives generates log2(2) = 1 bit of information, whereas a human seeing a light screen discriminates among many more alternatives, generating many more bits.
- Bayne distinguishes three readings of the information axiom (INFORMATION1: level of consciousness as a function of range of discriminations; INFORMATION2: having a content requires capacity for a range of contents/holism; INFORMATION3: conceptual role semantics) and argues each either fails to be axiomatic or fails to constrain; he notes conceptual role semantics is a minority account of content (Loewer 1997).
- The integration axiom (experience is unitary, "irreducible to non-interdependent subsets," Tononi et al. 2016, 452) is parsed via representational unity (INTEGRATION1), gestalt unity (INTEGRATION2, after Dainton 2000), and phenomenal holism vs atomism (INTEGRATION3); Bayne cites counterexamples such as associative agnosia and co-occurring birdsong/leg-pain experiences that are not representationally unified.
- The exclusion axiom's claim that an "instant of consciousness" has a definite duration is undercut by IIT itself, since Tononi et al. state that duration "ranges from a few tens of milliseconds to a few hundred milliseconds"—i.e., they treat it as variable, so it cannot be an essential constraint.
- The natural kind approach (Shea and Bayne 2010; Shea 2012) is offered as an alternative, with hepatitis as the worked example: pursuing it led to identifying viruses whose presence explains why pre-theoretical signs cluster (Seeff 2009), enabling tests for the disease's distribution.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper is explicitly an "Opinion Paper" / philosophical critique, not an empirical study; its conclusions are arguments about the epistemic status of axioms rather than experimental results.
- Bayne acknowledges he leaves the axiom-to-postulate relationship for IIT's advocates to clarify, assuming only that the relationship is epistemic (axioms provide robust but perhaps not conclusive evidence); he concedes the abductive reading is "far more plausible" than the deductive one but argues it does not match how IIT has been presented.
- The critique relies on contested philosophical positions (e.g., externalist accounts of consciousness, Dretske 1995 / Hurley 1998 / Lycan 2001 / Byrne and Tye 2006; the possibility of vague conscious content, Papineau 1993 / Tye 1996), which Bayne himself notes are "controversial"—so the force of several objections depends on rejecting IIT's intuitions rather than refuting them.
- Bayne discloses he has himself argued for a restricted form of phenomenal holism (Bayne 2010, 2014), yet still judges INTEGRATION3 unlikely to qualify as an axiom; he also acknowledges comments from Giulio Tononi and Anil Seth among others.
- The paper concedes IIT's substantive merits could survive: it states IIT could be developed within the natural kind framework, and that IIT's natural explanation of why human consciousness is unified "is surely a mark in its favour," especially as many rival theories make no attempt to account for it.
