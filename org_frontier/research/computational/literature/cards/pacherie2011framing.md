## Summary
Pacherie asks what makes a joint action "intentionally joint," seeking a middle ground between minimalist views (a joint action is just a common effect of several agents' intentional individual actions) and maximalist views (joint actions must be intentional under a description as joint, requiring a shared intention). She critically reviews three philosophical accounts of shared intention — Tuomela and Miller, Searle, Bratman, and Gilbert — arguing that Bratman's constructivist account requires too much cognitive sophistication (full meta-representational mindreading) and Gilbert's joint-commitment account imposes more sui generis normativity (mutual obligations and entitlements) than is strictly needed. She then turns to economists' team-agency theory, concentrating on Michael Bacharach's version, in which shared agency is a matter of team-reasoning ("What should we do?" rather than "What should I do?"), team-reasoning depends on group identification, and group identification is the result of self-framing automatically induced by psychological mechanisms sensitive to features like common interest and strong interdependence. The main claim is that the framing route to team-agency is less normatively loaded than Gilbert's and less cognitively demanding than Bratman's (and than Sugden's assurance-based version), while still capturing a notion of joint action more substantial than the minimalist one. She concludes that Bacharach's theory does not make the other accounts superfluous but undermines their claims to give necessary and sufficient conditions, and she notes its limitations regarding how action alternatives and group preferences get worked out and how coordination toward a shared goal is actually achieved.

## Key facts it relies on
- Two conditions define joint action minimally: the "common effect requirement" (a common effect that no agent brought about alone) plus the "individual intentional action requirement" (each agent's contributing doing is intentional under some description, in Davidson's sense); Butterfill (2010) is cited as holding there can be joint actions without shared intentions.
- Bratman's account (Bratman 2009a,b) of shared intention has seven listed building blocks, with condition (2) — interlocking intentions, each intends the joint activity go in part by way of the others' relevant intentions — being most central; conditions (3), (4) and (6) can be derived from (2) plus the norms of individual planning, and (7) requires common knowledge of all conditions.
- Critique of Bratman: his analysis requires agents to have concepts of mental states and full-fledged meta-representational abilities (Tollefsen 2005; Pacherie and Dokic 2006), which would wrongly exclude small children, who, evidence indicates, engage in joint action before acquiring such sophistication (Rakoczy 2006, 2007; Tomasello and Carpenter 2007; Warneken et al. 2006; Warneken and Tomasello 2007).
- Gilbert's account (Gilbert 1997, 2009): "Persons P1 and P2 have a shared intention to do A if and only if they are jointly committed to intending as a body to do A" (Gilbert 1997: 73); joint commitment is basic/non-reducible and generates a sui generis social normativity of mutual obligations and entitlements, not derivable from moral norms or norms of individual rationality.
- Bacharach's team-reasoning slogan: "somebody 'team-reasons' if she works out the best possible feasible combination of actions for all the members of her team, then does her part in it" (2006: 121); team-agency theory replaces "What should I do?" with "What should we do?".
- In one-shot anonymous non-repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games played for money, between 40 and 50% of participants choose cooperate (Sally 1995); the paper presents the Prisoner's Dilemma payoff matrix (Cooperate/Cooperate 2,2; Defect/Defect 1,1; off-diagonals 3,0 and 0,3) and the Hi-Lo game (a,a = 2,2; b,b = 1,1; mismatches 0,0).
- Bacharach (2006: 82) defines two players as having common interests if there are at least two possible outcomes such that the interests of both are better served in one than the other; the two features most relevant to inducing a "we" frame are common interests and strong interdependence; in the Prisoner's Dilemma these are present only on the main diagonal, so a "we" frame is not guaranteed.
- Group identification draws on Brewer and Gardner (1996): people have personal, relational, and collective self-representations; self-conception is self-framing with characteristic instability and context-dependence; listed conditions promoting group identification include shared social/ad hoc category, face-to-face contact, "we" language, shared experience, common interests, common fate, interdependence, and a competing outside group.
- Bacharach's shared intention is stated as: P1 and P2 share an intention to A if (a) each has a collective self-conception as a team member (collective self-framing), (b) each reasons that A is the best choice for all team members (team-reasoning), and (c) each therefore intends to do his part of A (participatory intention).
- Sugden's (2003) assurance view is positioned as intermediate: team-reasoning is one "logic" among others whose endorsement is a matter of choice conditional on assurance (common reason to believe all members endorse and act on team reason), whereas for Bacharach assurance plays no essential role and framing is automatic.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The paper is explicitly non-exclusive: Pacherie states her aim is not to argue team-agency can only result from Bacharach-style framing, and concedes Bacharach's theory does not make Bratman's or Gilbert's accounts superfluous — it only casts doubt on their claims to give necessary and sufficient conditions for shared intention.
- Pacherie pushes back on Bacharach's claim that frame use is never a matter of choice: by analogy with attentional control over the duck-rabbit illusion, she argues agents may exert some voluntary control over self-framing once aware of its role; she suspects what Bacharach really opposes is that frame adoption be a matter of rational choice (illustrated by the meta-level Prisoner's Dilemma, whose payoff matrix is identical to the original, threatening infinite regress).
- Self-acknowledged limitations: Bacharach's theory takes group preferences for granted (Hakli et al. 2010) and does not say how action alternatives or group preferences are worked out in non-transparent real-life situations; it explains how people can rationally decide to pursue a shared goal but says little about how they actually coordinate actions toward it, requiring further mindreading skills and other shared-representation/action-prediction mechanisms (Sebanz et al. 2006; Sebanz and Knoblich 2009).
- Both Bratman's and Gilbert's accounts are argued to require some form of communication or prior interaction (to influence intentions or form joint commitments) and thus cannot explain shared agency where agents cannot communicate, which is a central motivation for team-agency theory; whether communication is a necessary precondition of shared agency is presented as questionable (Ostrom 1990; Ostrom et al. 1992).
- The cognitive minimalism of the framing route is qualified: detecting common interests and interdependence still presupposes representing others as animate, goal-directed, intentional agents (precursors of mindreading), and more robust mentalizing may be needed when subplan-meshing is non-trivial or when expectations about partners' actions are violated.
