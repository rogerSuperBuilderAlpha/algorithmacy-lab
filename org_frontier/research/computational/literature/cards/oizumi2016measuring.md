## Summary
The paper addresses how to compute integrated information (Φ), the quantity Integrated Information Theory (IIT) of consciousness uses to measure information generated by a system as a whole above and beyond its independent parts, in a way that is practically applicable to experimental neural data. The original measure Φ assumes a maximum entropy distribution over past states, which the authors argue precludes practical computation (no unique maximum entropy distribution for continuous variables, and the full transition probability matrix is nearly impossible to estimate experimentally). Building on Barrett and Seth's strategy of using the empirical distribution, the authors show that the two empirically-based measures Φ_I and Φ_H proposed by Barrett and Seth fail to satisfy the theoretical requirements that a measure of integrated information be lower-bounded by 0 and upper-bounded by the information generated by the whole system. They then derive a new measure, Φ*, by importing the information-theoretic concept of "mismatched decoding": Φ* is the difference between the actual mutual information between past and present states and the "hypothetical" mutual information I* obtained when the system is decoded using a partitioned (parts-independent) probability distribution. They prove Φ* is properly bounded from below and above, derive its analytical expression under a Gaussian approximation to make it computable in large systems, and apply Φ*, Φ_I, Φ_H, and mutual information to electrocorticogram (ECoG) recordings from awake monkeys, demonstrating that Φ* satisfies the theoretical requirements while Φ_I and Φ_H violate them even in real brain data.

## Key facts it relies on
- IIT (Tononi and colleagues) defines integrated information Φ as the amount of information a system generates as a whole above and beyond the information its parts generate independently; if parts are independent, no integrated information exists, and IIT predicts Φ tracks levels of consciousness.
- The paper focuses on the IIT 2.0 version of Φ because it is simpler and more feasible to compute than IIT 3.0; in IIT 2.0, Φ = I(max X^{t−τ}; X^t) − Σ_i I(max M_i^{t−τ}; M_i^t) (Eq 2), the whole-system mutual information minus the sum of within-part mutual information.
- Two theoretical requirements for a measure of integrated information: (1) it should not be negative (lower bound 0), and (2) it should not exceed the information generated by the whole system (upper bound); the original Φ satisfies 0 ≤ Φ ≤ I(max X^{t−τ}; X^t) (Eq 4), being expressible as a Kullback-Leibler divergence.
- The maximum entropy distribution assumption is impractical because it cannot be uniquely defined for continuous variables and requires knowing the full transition probability matrix p(X^t | X^{t−τ}) for all possible past states, which is nearly impossible to estimate experimentally.
- Barrett and Seth (ref [15]) proposed using the empirical distribution to define two measures, Φ_I (Eq 5) and Φ_H (Eq 6); Φ_H was independently derived in information geometry as "stochastic interaction." The paper shows these fail the bounds.
- Φ* is defined via mismatched decoding: Φ*(X^{t−τ}; X^t) = I(X^{t−τ}; X^t) − I*(X^{t−τ}; X^t) (Eq 9), where I* is the hypothetical mutual information from decoding with the partitioned distribution q(X^t | X^{t−τ}) = Π_i p(M_i^t | M_i^{t−τ}) (Eq 8); Φ* satisfies the bounds because I* ≥ 0 and I* ≤ I, and Φ* reduces to the original Φ under the maximum entropy distribution.
- The mismatched-decoding information I* (Eq 20) is maximized over a scalar parameter β; I*(β) is a convex function of β, and under the Gaussian assumption dI*(β)/dβ = 0 reduces to a quadratic equation in β so β is solved analytically without gradient ascent. I* for mismatched decoding was first derived in information theory (ref [17]) and first applied to neural data analysis in ref [19].
- Worked counterexamples using a linear regression / autoregressive model X^t = A X^{t−1} + E^t (Eq 13) with connectivity A (Eq 14) and noise covariance Σ(E) (Eq 15): when connection strength a=0 (no information, I=0), Φ* and Φ_I are 0 but Φ_H stays positive and increases with noise correlation c (violating the upper bound); when a=0.4 and c→1 (parts perfectly correlated), Φ_I becomes negative (e.g. Φ_I = −0.15 in Fig 4) (violating the lower bound) while Φ* decreases to 0.
- ECoG application: 128 ECoG electrodes were implanted subdurally in four adult macaque monkeys (left hemisphere, frontal/parietal/temporal/occipital lobes), 1 mm diameter platinum disc electrodes, 5 mm inter-electrode distance, 1 kHz sampling; analysis used 64 bipolar re-referenced signals with the "atomic partition" (all 64 channels assumed independent), Gaussian approximation, covariance estimated over a 2 s window with 2 s time step and averaged over 600 s. Time lag τ varied 1–500 ms; Φ* was positive and peaked around τ = 20 ms while Φ_I went negative at small τ and Φ_H stayed large even as mutual information I approached 0.

## Critical notes from the literature
- The authors acknowledge Φ* still requires choosing a system partition; IIT requires the minimum information partition (MIP), but the number of possible partitions grows exponentially with system size, so finding the MIP is an unresolved practical difficulty (they used a given atomic partition rather than the MIP).
- They note an unresolved theoretical problem of comparing integrated information across partitions of different sizes/symmetry; IIT 2.0's proposed normalization factor can be negative for continuous variables (since entropy can be negative), so a different normalization is needed and the best scheme is unclear.
- The measure is based on IIT 2.0, which quantifies only information flow from present to past; IIT 3.0 additionally considers present-to-future flow. Because mutual information is symmetric in time, Φ* does not asymmetrically quantify directional integration, and how to do so with an empirical distribution is left as an open question.
- The authors point out that prior work (Barrett and Seth [15]) failed to detect the problems of Φ_I and Φ_H because they fixed the covariance of the Gaussian noise E to 0 and did not vary it; the violations become apparent only when noise covariance is large.
- Φ* measures global integration over the whole system and is distinguished from bivariate measures like Granger causality and transfer entropy, which measure local pairwise interactions; the authors flag clarifying the relationship between Φ* and such connectivity measures as future work.
