# Organizational Coordination and Dependency Theory

Two structural descriptions anchor the study of coordination as the management of interdependence. Thompson's typology distinguishes pooled, sequential, and reciprocal interdependence, mapping each to a dominant mechanism: standardization and rules, coordination by plan, and coordination by mutual adjustment, with coordination cost and required communication rising across the three (Hoekzema, 2020). Malone and Crowston (1994) generalized this insight into coordination theory, treating coordination as the process of managing dependencies among activities and cataloguing recurrent dependency types (shared-resource, producer/consumer, simultaneity, task/subtask) together with candidate mechanisms drawn across computer science, organization theory, and economics. The decade-on retrospective documents the framework's diffusion and its use for task analysis and process design (Crowston, Rubleske, & Howison, 2006).

Recent work treats these structural categories as resources to be re-specified rather than fixed labels. Sailer, Loscher, and Kaiser (2023) show how patterning across a routine cluster governs flexibility, recasting Thompson's reciprocal case as "coordinated interdependence" maintained through ongoing relational work. Hoekzema (2020) similarly argues that coordinating interdependent routines is a continuous process of matching degrees of interdependence to modes of coordination, integrating routine-ecology and routine-cluster accounts.

Empirical and design studies sharpen the dependency-management lens. Task structure conditions whether collective effort yields collective intelligence, marking interdependence as a boundary condition on group performance (Riedl, Kim, Gupta, Malone, & Woolley, 2021). In distributed software development, team-external dependencies drive coordination overhead that internal mechanisms cannot absorb (Scheerer, Bick, Hildenbrand, & Heinzl, 2020). Within employment relationships, task interdependence moderates how idiosyncratic task arrangements translate into demand-ability fit (Liu, Hornung, Rousseau, & Lu, 2025). Across firm boundaries, attribute-based accounts link dependency structure to the choice of supply-chain coordination and cooperation mechanisms (Xu & Beamon, 2006).

A formal turn revisits the typology's necessity claims. Ju (2026) connects distributed-systems theory to organizational science, proving that coordination is required when a task specification is non-monotonic, mapping Thompson's categories onto this criterion, and estimating across 65 workflows and over 13,000 occupational tasks that much coordination effort is avoidable. Human-AI settings extend the agenda: representational integration emerges as an organizing process bridging the cognitive asymmetry between embodied human and codified machine knowledge, reorganizing task allocation and adjustment (Yokoi et al., 2026). Together these strands keep Thompson's intensity ordering and the Malone-Crowston dependency catalogue as the structural backbone while relocating coordination into process, formal necessity, and machine partners.
