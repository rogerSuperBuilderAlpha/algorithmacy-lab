# Cooperative-Game Structure of Joint Action

The cooperative-game tradition formalizes joint action as a characteristic function v that assigns a worth to every coalition S of a player set N, abstracting away from the strategic moves through which that worth is produced. Within this frame the central question becomes how the value of acting together is divided. The Shapley value answers it by averaging each player's marginal contribution v(S∪{i}) − v(S) across all coalition-formation orders, the unique allocation satisfying efficiency, symmetry, the null-player property, and additivity (Shapley, 1953). That axiomatic uniqueness is what gives the construct its standing as a normative account of credit for contribution to a joint product (Thomson, 2019; Li, 2024). The construct generalizes readily: precedence constraints, coalition (level) structures, and restricted worths each yield modified Shapley values that encode how the architecture of cooperation, beyond mere membership, shapes individual entitlement (Faigle & Kern, 1992; Besner, 2024).

A parallel strand asks what makes joint action joint at all. Bratman's planning theory builds shared intention from interlocking individual intentions plus mutual responsiveness, deliberately avoiding any irreducible group subject, while Gilbert grounds it in joint commitment and the directed obligations it creates (Janković & Ludwig, 2018). Alonso (2016) argues both the motivational and the normative roles are independently essential, locating shared intention in a structure of individual reliance attitudes. These accounts concern the constitution of joint agency, leaving the division-of-value question that cooperative games answer largely implicit. Recent work probes whether such accounts demand excessive cognitive sophistication, seeking minimal forms of shared intentionality observable in infants and non-human animals (Crone, Gab & Vincini, 2025).

Team-reasoning theory supplies the bridge. Bacharach (2006) models agents who reframe a decision from "what should I do?" to "what should we do?", deriving each member's action as a component of the profile that best serves a group objective; Sugden (2015) develops this as intentional cooperation for mutual benefit, and Pacherie (2011) presents it as a less normatively loaded route to shared intention than Bratman's or Gilbert's. Team reasoning thus connects the constitutive question (how a "we" forms and reasons) to the evaluative apparatus of cooperative games (what the "we" achieves and how it is apportioned).

The computational frontier reanimates these formalisms as credit-assignment machinery. Shapley-based attribution now allocates a global reward among agents in multi-agent reinforcement learning and explains feature contributions in machine learning (Rozemberczki et al., 2022; Wang, 2025), while critics note that axiomatic justifications transfer imperfectly and that the broader Weber and Harsanyi allocation families offer richer structure (Il Idrissi, Fernandes Machado & Charpentier, 2025). For a program studying coordination through opaque algorithmic intermediaries, this convergence supplies an exact, axiomatically grounded vocabulary for the value and apportionment of joint action.
