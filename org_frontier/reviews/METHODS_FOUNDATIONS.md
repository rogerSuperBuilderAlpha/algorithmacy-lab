# Methods foundations — where this arm's procedure comes from

Two papers from organizational research methods supply the method. Both are paywalled (SAGE), so this
arm stores a summary and the citation, not the PDF (the lab's copyright rule: commit only open-access
PDFs; paywalled sources get a note and a DOI). Full entries are in each review's
`literature/references.bib`.

## Systematicity — the pipeline

**Simsek, Z., Fox, B. C., & Heavey, C. (2023). Systematicity in Organizational Research Literature
Reviews: A Framework and Assessment. *Organizational Research Methods*, 26(2), 292–321.**
doi:10.1177/10944281211008652.

The paper does two things. It develops a framework for *systematicity* as an encompassing orientation
toward explicit method in reviews, and it then measures the practice empirically — coding a sample of
**165 published reviews** across three journals (*Journal of Management*, *Academy of Management
Annals*, *International Journal of Management Reviews*) for the practices they report, and correlating
those practices with citation impact. That second move is the template this arm imitates: a body of
literature (here, review articles) treated as a dataset and measured.

The framework is Principles → Practices → Promises.

- **Six principles:** transparency, completeness (coverage), saturation, connectedness, universalism,
  coherence.
- **Seven practices:** envisioning (the orienting question), explicating (boundary conditions),
  executing (the search), evaluating, encoding, elaborating, expositing.
- **Four promises:** richness, reproducibility, trustworthiness, utility.

The practices give this arm's playbook its stages. Two details carry over directly: *executing*
prescribes backward/forward citation-tracking and an explicit stopping rule (they cite Booth's "five
relevant per hundred references scanned"), and *explicating* prescribes stating substantive and
procedural boundary conditions — the review's gates.

## Knowledge weaving — the questions

**Simsek, Z., Heavey, C., Fox, B. C., & Yu, T. (2022). Compelling Questions in Research: Seeing What
Everybody Has Seen and Thinking What Nobody Has Thought. *Journal of Management*.**
doi:10.1177/01492063211073068 (editorial).

An editorial that introduces *knowledge weaving*: a five-stage process for deriving research questions
from a literature by examining its knowledge claims — identify the claims, assess their developmental
status, prospect for alignment, evaluate their intrinsic goodness, prototype for an audience. It sorts
a field's knowledge claims into four types: **stylized facts** (what the field takes as established),
**key assumptions** (what it takes for granted), **enduring critiques** (its unresolved disputes), and
**substantive omissions** (what it has not addressed).

This arm uses the claim typology as its hypothesis source. A stylized fact, an assumption, a critique,
or an omission that a citation graph or a coded corpus can check becomes a testable hypothesis. The
process is qualitative in the original; this arm renders its output as falsifiable measurement.

## How the two combine

Knowledge weaving says *what to ask* of a literature (its claims). Systematicity says *how to answer*
rigorously (the practices, the boundary conditions, the reliability discipline). The arm's playbook
([`RESEARCH_PLAYBOOK.md`](RESEARCH_PLAYBOOK.md)) is the two joined: envision the question and its
knowledge claims, then run them through the systematicity pipeline to a reported result with
intercoder reliability.

## Note on the six papers this arm was distilled from

The arm was prompted by a set of Simsek/Heavey/Fox methods papers. Only the two above are templates
for *analyzing a body of literature*. The others are empirical studies on primary data (a 3D-printing
firm panel; a cohort of new ventures; an SME CEO/TMT survey) or a conceptual editorial on big-data
method — technique exemplars, not literature analyses — and are out of scope for this arm.
