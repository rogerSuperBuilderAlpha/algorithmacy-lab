# What Algorithmic-Management Research Asserts and What It Tests

## Research Summary

Algorithmic management has become one of the fastest-growing topics in the study of work, and it arrives
carrying a claim: that software now performs the direction, evaluation, and discipline of workers that
managers once performed, and that this constitutes a new form of control. This paper treats that
literature as a dataset and asks what kind of knowledge it produces. It codes a corpus of 66 sources,
harvested through two academic semantic-search connectors and screened to the algorithmic-management-of-
workers literature, on three dimensions: the knowledge-weaving type of each source's central claim, its
evidentiary base, and its focal outcome. Three independent coders applied the codebook to titles and
abstracts, reaching Fleiss' kappa of 0.75 to 1.00. The corpus concentrates on control and worker
experience (74 percent of sources) and rarely addresses productivity or firm performance (4.5 percent).
It is majority conceptual and qualitative (70 percent), with quantitative evidence a real but minority
footing (30 percent including mixed methods). Most sharply, the control claim is described rather than
measured: of the 22 sources whose focal outcome is control, none report quantitative evidence, while the
quantitative studies that exist measure worker reactions such as engagement and burnout. The field's
signature proposition functions as a stylized fact — a shared premise that motivates research without
itself being put to quantitative test.

## Interrogating a Literature's Knowledge Claims

A review can do more than summarize what a field has found. It can ask what kind of claims a field makes
and on what footing those claims rest. Simsek, Heavey, Fox, and Yu (2022) call the raw material of this
question a field's knowledge claims, and sort them into four types: stylized facts a field takes as
established, assumptions it takes for granted, critiques it argues over, and omissions it has not
addressed. Simsek, Fox, and Heavey (2023) supply the discipline for answering the question rigorously —
an explicit corpus boundary, a fixed codebook, independent coders, and a reported reliability statistic.
Joining the two turns a reading of a literature into a measurement of it.

Algorithmic management is a good candidate for this treatment. The topic grew from a handful of ethnographies
of ride-hailing and food delivery into a cross-disciplinary literature spanning human resource management,
organizational behavior, information systems, sociology, and labor law. Its early anchors framed the
phenomenon in the vocabulary of control. Algorithms at work were named a new contested terrain of control;
platform food delivery was analyzed as algorithmic despotism; app-based work was theorized as a distinctive
control system. That framing traveled. The question this paper asks is not whether the framing is right.
It is whether the field, having adopted the framing, has tested it — and where the field's empirical energy
actually goes.

Three hypotheses, registered before any source was coded, make the question falsifiable. H1 predicts that
the field's outcomes skew to control and worker experience and largely bypass performance. H2 predicts that
the evidence base is mostly conceptual and qualitative, with quantitative evidence uncommon. H3 predicts
that the control claim functions as a stylized fact: asserted widely, tested rarely, and resting more on
conceptual and qualitative sources than the corpus at large.

## Framework: Knowledge Weaving

The knowledge-weaving typology gives the study its central coding variable. Each source is assigned the
type of its principal claim about algorithmic management. A stylized fact asserts, as established, that
algorithmic management is a form of control over workers, or builds on a control effect as settled. An
assumption takes that framing for granted to study something else — an antecedent, a coping response, a
mediator such as burnout or engagement — without headlining the control claim. A critique disputes,
complicates, or bounds a prevailing claim: that control is partial, negotiated, resisted, dual, or that
the enabling side is neglected. An omission flags a gap or calls for research.

Two further variables carry the other hypotheses. Evidence records the source's primary basis —
conceptual, qualitative, quantitative, or mixed — with systematic literature reviews coded as conceptual,
since they analyze prior claims rather than new primary data. Outcome records the focal phenomenon —
control, worker experience, performance, or other. The three variables let the review separate what the
field claims (claim type), how it warrants the claim (evidence), and what it studies (outcome), and then
cross them.

## Method

The corpus was built through two academic semantic-search connectors rather than raw keyword search.
Six queries against a full-text scholarly index covered the field's spine — algorithmic management of
workers on digital labor platforms, algorithmic control of gig workers, algorithmic management as a new
form of control, systematic reviews of platform-work algorithmic management, effects on productivity and
performance, and worker experience and resistance. Three further queries against a second peer-reviewed
index recovered canonical and empirical sources the first channel missed, including the field's most
cited early anchors. The two channels were merged and deduplicated by DOI and normalized title.

The corpus boundary is one algorithm signal (an algorithm word stem, covering algorithmic management,
control, surveillance, governance, and the panopticon and Taylorism metaphors) together with one work
signal (worker, gig, platform work, labor, employee, crowdwork, and cognate terms). The conjunction keeps
the corpus to the algorithmic-management-of-workers literature and screens out generic algorithm-in-finance,
law-and-society, and firm-productivity hits that lack a worker referent; 11 candidates were screened out on
this rule and logged. The screened corpus is 66 sources, published 2019 to 2026, concentrated in 2022 to
2025.

Three coders applied the fixed codebook to each source's title and abstract, blind to one another and to
the hypotheses' predicted direction. Reliability was computed with Fleiss' kappa per categorical variable
and a majority-vote adjudicated dataset. The tests run on the adjudicated data.

## Results

### Reliability

Agreement was high across all three variables. Evidence type was unanimous on all 66 sources — kappa of
1.00 — because abstracts state their method plainly. Outcome reached kappa of 0.895 (92.9 percent
agreement). Claim type, the most interpretive variable, reached kappa of 0.747 (82.8 percent), which
Landis and Koch read as substantial. The distributions that follow are not one reader's construal.

| variable | Fleiss' kappa | agreement | interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| claim_type | 0.747 | 82.8% | substantial |
| evidence | 1.000 | 100.0% | almost perfect |
| outcome | 0.895 | 92.9% | almost perfect |

### The distribution of claim types

Before the hypotheses, the claim-type variable maps how the field warrants its statements. Assumptions
dominate: 30 of 66 sources take the control-or-reconfiguration framing for granted to study something
else. Critiques are the next largest group at 19 — sources that complicate the control narrative by
showing autonomy, resistance, duality, or enabling effects. Stylized facts, sources that assert control
as established, are 12. Pure omissions, research agendas whose central move is to name a gap, are 5.

| claim_type | sources | share |
|---|---|---|
| assumption | 30 | 45.5% |
| critique | 19 | 28.8% |
| stylized_fact | 12 | 18.2% |
| omission | 5 | 7.6% |

The shape matters for H3. The control claim is rarely the headline (12 sources); far more often it is the
unstated floor the rest of the study is built on (30 sources). A field that argues about a claim in its
critiques while assuming it in its empirics is a field treating that claim as settled.

### The outcome mix (H1)

The corpus concentrates on two outcomes and neglects a third.

| outcome | sources | share |
|---|---|---|
| worker_experience | 27 | 40.9% |
| control | 22 | 33.3% |
| other | 14 | 21.2% |
| performance | 3 | 4.5% |

Control and worker experience together are 49 of 66 sources, 74.2 percent. Performance is three sources,
and all three reach performance through a worker-level mechanism — algorithmic bias and HRM effectiveness,
gig-worker safety behavior, and proactive customer-service performance in a mixed-methods design — not
through a firm productivity measure. The question a managerial audience would ask first, whether algorithmic
management makes the operation more productive, is close to absent from the corpus. H1 is supported.

### The evidence base (H2)

The field advances mostly by argument and by cases.

| evidence | sources | share |
|---|---|---|
| conceptual | 25 | 37.9% |
| qualitative | 21 | 31.8% |
| quantitative | 16 | 24.2% |
| mixed | 4 | 6.1% |

Conceptual and qualitative work is 46 of 66 sources, 69.7 percent. Quantitative sources are 16, rising to
20 with the four mixed-methods studies — 30.3 percent. This is a substantial and, by publication year, a
growing minority, concentrated in survey studies of gig-worker psychology using structural equation
modeling. It is not yet the field's center of gravity. H2 is supported.

### Control described, reactions measured (H3)

The third test is the informative one. Crossing outcome with evidence shows where the field points its
instruments.

| outcome | conceptual | qualitative | quantitative | mixed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| control | 9 | 12 | 0 | 1 |
| worker_experience | 7 | 6 | 12 | 2 |
| performance | 0 | 0 | 2 | 1 |
| other | 9 | 3 | 2 | 0 |

Of the 22 sources whose focal outcome is control, none are quantitative and one is mixed. The other 21 are
conceptual or qualitative. The 12 sources that assert control as an established fact rest 91.7 percent on
conceptual or qualitative evidence, against a corpus-wide quantitative-plus-mixed share of 30.3 percent.
And the quantitative studies are not missing from the field — they are aimed elsewhere. Fourteen of the 20
quantitative-or-mixed sources study worker experience: engagement, burnout, technostress, thriving,
citizenship behavior. The field measures how workers respond to algorithmic control far more than it
measures algorithmic control.

This is what it means for the control claim to function as a stylized fact. Twelve sources assert it
outright and 28 more assume it to motivate a study of something else, so 40 of 66 sources treat the
control framing as given. The sources that headline it as established are the least likely in the corpus
to test it with numbers. H3 is supported, and in a sharper form than predicted: where the control proposition is
foregrounded it is almost never tested quantitatively — 21 of 22 control-outcome sources are conceptual
or qualitative, with a single mixed-methods exception.

## Discussion

Three findings compose one picture. The algorithmic-management literature is a literature of control and
experience, built mostly from concepts and cases, whose quantitative wing has grown up around worker
reactions rather than around its own founding claim. None of this makes the control framing wrong. The
early ethnographies of Uber, Deliveroo, and food-delivery platforms are careful and their descriptions of
algorithmic direction, evaluation, and discipline are detailed. The point is structural. A claim that
organizes a field deserves, at some stage, measurement of its reach — how much of the variance in what
workers do is algorithmic direction, in which settings the control is tight and in which it is loose, how
the control compares in strength to the managerial control it is said to replace. The corpus shows that
this measurement has not been the field's project. Its measurement effort went to the downstream question
of how workers feel and behave under a control that the upstream literature had already asserted.

For a research program that wants to formalize and quantify coordination and control, this is an opening
rather than a rebuke. The descriptive base is rich; the reactions are increasingly well measured; the
middle — a measured account of the control relation itself — is thin. The most cited sources in the corpus
are conceptual reviews and qualitative studies, which is typical of a young field, and the recent drift
toward survey work shows the field is capable of measurement. What it has not yet done is turn that
capability on the claim that defines it.

The finding also refines what "algorithmic management is a new form of control" can mean as a warrant. In
this corpus it is a premise, not a result. That status is not a defect of any single paper — stylized
facts are how fields economize — but it does mark where a reader should be careful. When a source opens
from the control claim as established, the claim is doing framing work, and the corpus contains little
quantitative adjudication behind it.

## Limitations

The coders are three LLM agents applying a fixed codebook, not trained human raters. Agreement among the
agent passes is high, but high agreement among agents is not a substitute for independent human coding,
and the claim-type variable in particular involves judgment that human raters might resolve differently.
Coding used title and abstract only. A study that computes a control measure in its body but abstracts on
worker reactions would be coded to worker experience, so the H3 result is a claim about where sources
foreground their contribution, which is itself informative but is not a full-text audit. Coding systematic
literature reviews as conceptual is a defensible call that raises the conceptual share; treating them
otherwise would not change the H2 verdict but would move the number. The corpus is bounded by two
semantic-search connectors and by English-language indexing, and it oversamples human-resource-management
and organizational-behavior venues relative to the sociology and information-systems wings of the field.
The 4.5-percent performance share is an estimate for this corpus, not a census; operations-management and
platform-economics research on algorithmic efficiency uses a different vocabulary and largely sits outside
the search frame, so the true neglect of performance is bounded by what these connectors index. Within
those bounds the three results are clear and their reliability is measured.
