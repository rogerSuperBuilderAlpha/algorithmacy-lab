# How Gig and Platform-Work Reviews Motivate Themselves: Gap-Spotting, Problematization, and Citation Impact

## Research Summary

A review article has to say why it was worth writing. Sandberg and Alvesson (2011) named two ways of
doing this: gap-spotting, which locates something the literature has not yet done and offers to do it,
and problematization, which identifies an assumption the field takes for granted and disputes it. This
study codes 49 review, agenda-setting, and bibliometric-review articles on gig and platform work for
which of the two moves carries the paper. Three independent coders reached substantial-to-almost-perfect
agreement (Fleiss' κ = 0.72 on the motivation mode, 0.92 on whether an assumption is contested).
Gap-spotting carries 44 of the 49 reviews, 90 percent. Four problematize; one does neither. The share
of problematization does not rise across the decade — it falls, from 13 percent of pre-2024 reviews to
6 percent of the 2024-onward wave, as bibliometric and PRISMA reviews, a genre built on gap-spotting,
came to dominate output. The four problematizing reviews are cited far more per year than the
gap-spotting ones (median 28.6 versus 3.7; Mann-Whitney p = 0.022), consistent with Sandberg and
Alvesson's claim that problematization is the higher-impact move, though the four-case base makes the
size of that gap uncertain. The gig-work review literature grows mostly by cataloguing itself.

## Introduction

The gig economy arrived with a review problem attached. Within a few years of Uber and Deliveroo
becoming objects of study, the literature about them had grown large enough, and fast enough, that
synthesizing it became its own scholarly activity. The corpus assembled here spans 2017 to 2026 and is
already 49 reviews deep, and it undercounts, because the boundary excludes primary studies, book
reviews, and adjacent literatures. Reviews of gig work are now a genre.

Every one of them opens by explaining its warrant. That opening move is the object of this study.
Sandberg and Alvesson (2011) argued that management scholarship constructs research questions in two
broad ways. The common one is gap-spotting: the author surveys existing work, finds a corner that is
under-studied, a literature that is fragmented, or a construct that is under-specified, and positions
the contribution as filling that hole. The rarer one is problematization: the author identifies an
assumption the field holds without examining it and builds the contribution on challenging that
assumption. Their empirical point was that gap-spotting dominates, and their normative point was that
this is a loss, because problematization is what generates influential, field-shifting work.

Gig-work reviews are a good place to test both points. The literature is young, interdisciplinary, and
contested. Its central terms — gig work, platform work, the on-demand economy, crowdwork — are
notoriously unstable, and its two dominant framings, precarity and algorithmic control, are exactly the
kind of received views that invite problematization. A field this unsettled could plausibly motivate
its reviews either way: by mapping the sprawl and calling for order, or by contesting the framings that
organize it. This study measures which.

Three questions follow. First, does gap-spotting dominate, as Sandberg and Alvesson found for
management reviews generally (H1)? Second, as the field matures, does problematization become more
common — does a literature that has been mapped many times start contesting itself (H2)? Third, do
problematizing reviews earn more citations per year than gap-spotting ones, as the higher-impact claim
predicts (H3)? The hypotheses were registered before any coding. The method follows the systematicity
pipeline of Simsek, Fox, and Heavey (2023) and treats the review literature itself as the dataset.

The stakes reach past bookkeeping. How a field motivates its reviews shapes what the field can become.
A literature that only ever spots gaps accumulates coverage: more topics, more contexts, more
populations, each review adding cells to a growing map. A literature that problematizes revises its
own foundations: it changes what counts as a topic, redraws the boundaries, and occasionally makes the
old map unreadable. The balance between the two modes is a diagnostic of whether a field is filling in
a settled picture or still arguing about what the picture is. For gig and platform work — a domain a
newer research program might enter to build a construct rather than to catalogue one — the balance also
marks where the openings are. A move the field rewards but rarely makes is an opening.

## Framework

Gap-spotting and problematization are not a stylistic contrast; they are two logics of contribution.

Gap-spotting takes the existing literature as broadly sound and locates its edges. Sandberg and Alvesson
distinguished three sub-modes: confusion-spotting (the evidence conflicts), neglect-spotting (a topic,
context, or population is overlooked), and application-spotting (a theory has not been extended to a new
setting). Neglect-spotting is the workhorse. A systematic or bibliometric review is neglect-spotting
turned into a method: it counts the literature, clusters it, shows which cells are thin, and closes with
a research agenda that names the thin cells. The genre is honest and useful, and it is gap-spotting by
construction. A PRISMA flow diagram cannot problematize.

Problematization takes an assumption the literature shares and makes it the target. The move is to show
that a belief the field treats as settled — that gig work is a single category, that algorithmic
control is uniformly experienced, that platform workers cannot organize — is wrong, incomplete, or
concealing something, and then to rebuild on the corrected footing. Problematization is harder. It
requires standing outside the framing the field works within, and it risks the reception problem that
comes with telling colleagues their premises are off. Sandberg and Alvesson's wager was that this
difficulty is precisely why problematization pays: work that unsettles a premise reorganizes a
conversation, and reorganizing a conversation is what gets cited.

The knowledge-weaving typology of Simsek, Heavey, Fox, and Yu (2022) supplies the bridge to a testable
claim. A field's knowledge claims sort into stylized facts, key assumptions, enduring critiques, and
substantive omissions. Gap-spotting works the omissions; problematization works the assumptions. The
prevalence of each mode in a corpus of reviews is a measurable fact about how a field builds its
questions, and its association with citation impact is a measurable test of the higher-impact wager.

Reading the mode from an abstract is a defensible bet because the abstract is where a review states its
warrant to the widest audience. Authors compress into it the one reason the paper exists, and the
reason is usually a gap declared in a recognizable formula — "the literature remains fragmented," "no
review has examined," "we identify research gaps" — or an assumption contested in an equally
recognizable one — "contrary to the prevailing view," "we question the received distinction," "moving
away from problematic aggregations." The compression cuts both ways for measurement, and the direction
of the resulting error is worked out below; the point here is that the abstract is not a poor proxy for
the motivating move but its designated home. This study measures the motivating move as authors chose
to advertise it, which is also the version most readers ever encounter.

## Method

**Corpus.** The corpus is review, agenda-setting, systematic-review, bibliometric-review, and
meta-analytic articles whose subject is gig work, platform work, on-demand labor, crowdwork, digital
labor, or the algorithmic management of platform workers. A source qualifies if its title and abstract
present a synthesis, mapping, typology, or research agenda about the literature. Primary empirical
studies are excluded, as are book reviews, editorials without synthesis, and reviews of adjacent
literatures that are not about gig or platform work — sharing-economy-as-consumption, crowdfunding, and
open-innovation crowdsourcing. A source enters only if the connector also returned a citation count and
a publication year, both required for H3.

**Discovery.** Two academic semantic-search connectors supplied candidates: Consensus, over Semantic
Scholar, Scopus, PubMed, and ArXiv, which returns citation counts and years directly; and Scholar
Gateway, over Wiley, used as a review-type cross-check. Seven queries ran across the two — "gig economy
review," "platform work literature review," "gig work future research agenda," "on-demand economy
platform labor review," "gig economy bibliometric analysis," "algorithmic management gig workers
systematic review," and a crowdwork-and-sharing query. Each candidate was screened against the boundary
rule; primary studies and off-boundary reviews were dropped. Search stopped when new queries returned
no new in-boundary reviews with citation metadata. The screened union is 49 sources spanning 2017-2026.

**Coding.** The codebook fixes two variables. `motivation_mode` takes `gap_spotting`,
`problematization`, or `neither`, coded as the dominant move the contribution is built on.
`assumption_targeted` takes `yes` or `no` — whether the article explicitly names and contests an
in-field assumption. Three coders worked independently and blind to one another, each coding from title
and abstract only, each writing its own file. Reliability used Fleiss' κ per variable and a
majority-vote adjudicated dataset.

**Analysis.** H1 reads the `motivation_mode` distribution on the adjudicated data. H2 splits the corpus
at 2024 and compares the problematization share, and the `assumption_targeted` share, across the early
and late halves. H3 normalizes citations by age — total citations divided by years since publication,
with age floored at half a year — and compares problematizing and gap-spotting reviews with a
tie-corrected Mann-Whitney U test, reporting means and medians alongside.

## Results

**Reliability.** The three coders agreed substantially on the harder variable and almost perfectly on
the easier one.

| variable | Fleiss' κ | pairwise agreement | interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| motivation_mode | 0.721 | 93.2% | substantial |
| assumption_targeted | 0.916 | 98.6% | almost perfect |

The motivation call is the one that can slide, because a review that maps gaps and also complicates a
framing can be read either way; κ = 0.72 says the coders resolved that slide consistently. The 90/8/2
split below is not an artifact of one reader.

**H1 — gap-spotting dominates.** Forty-four of the 49 reviews motivate themselves by gap-spotting. Four
problematize. One does neither.

| motivation_mode | count | share |
|---|---|---|
| gap_spotting | 44 | 90% |
| problematization | 4 | 8% |
| neither | 1 | 2% |

The result reproduces Sandberg and Alvesson's finding in a literature they did not study, and does so
more sharply than their own sample. The gig-work review is, overwhelmingly, a gap-spotting instrument.
The bibliometric sub-genre is the clearest case: eighteen reviews in the corpus count a Scopus or Web of
Science pull, cluster the keywords, and close on a research agenda — the method is neglect-spotting
mechanized. Four sources contest an assumption. Duggan et al. (2019) disputes the "problematic
aggregation" of gig work into one category and rebuilds it as three. Keegan and Meijerink (2025)
challenges the assumption that human-resource frameworks can rely on a clean employee/freelancer
boundary, and works the "gray zone" that assumption hides. Lata and Liu (2022) contests the "mythical
autonomy" framing of on-demand work. Rafélis de Broves and Murgia (2024) starts from the received
expectation that platform workers cannot organize and shows why it failed. `assumption_targeted` tracks
these four exactly.

**H2 — problematization does not rise; it falls.** Splitting at 2024 sends the prediction backward.

| period | n | problematization share | assumption_targeted = yes |
|---|---|---|---|
| early (< 2024) | 15 | 13% (2/15) | 13% |
| late (≥ 2024) | 34 | 6% (2/34) | 6% |

The corpus more than doubled after 2023, and the growth is almost entirely gap-spotting. The post-2023
wave is dominated by bibliometric and PRISMA systematic reviews, a genre whose method forecloses
problematization. As that genre scaled, it diluted the problematizing share rather than lifting it. The
two problematizing reviews on the late side of the cut are Keegan and Meijerink (2025) and Rafélis de
Broves and Murgia (2024); the two on the early side are Duggan et al. (2019) and Lata and Liu (2022).
The field is not maturing into self-contestation. It is maturing into self-cataloguing.

**H3 — problematizers are cited more per year, on a base of four.** The four problematizing reviews draw
citations at several times the gap-spotting rate.

| motivation_mode | n | mean cites/yr | median cites/yr |
|---|---|---|---|
| gap_spotting | 44 | 8.19 | 3.67 |
| problematization | 4 | 41.34 | 28.56 |

The Mann-Whitney U test on the two citations-per-year distributions gives U = 27, z = −2.29, p = 0.022:
the problematizers rank higher than chance would place them. The direction matches the higher-impact
wager. The magnitude does not survive scrutiny of the base. Two of the four problematizers are
unusually visible pieces — Duggan et al. (2019) at roughly 104 citations per year and the Keegan and
Meijerink (2025) Annual Review chapter at roughly 37 — and they carry the mean. With four cases, the
test detects an association but cannot estimate its size, and cannot separate "problematization gets
cited" from "the two most prominent agenda-setting venues in this corpus happened to problematize." The
finding is a signal, not a measurement.

## Discussion

The headline is H1, and it is not a surprise so much as a confirmation carried into new territory. A
young, sprawling, interdisciplinary literature reviews itself the easy way. Ninety percent of the time
the warrant is a gap, and most often the gap is delivered by a method — a database query and a cluster
map — that can produce nothing else. The gig-work field has industrialized the gap-spotting review.

H2 is the more interesting result, because it was supposed to go the other way and the reason it does
not is structural. One might expect a field on its fifth or tenth review of the same corpus to run out
of unexamined corners and start contesting its framings. That is not what happened. Instead the
supply of reviews shifted toward the bibliometric genre, which answers "what has been done" and never
"what have we been assuming." The more a literature is mapped, the more gap-spotting it produces,
because mapping is gap-spotting. Maturity did not convert cataloguers into critics; it multiplied
cataloguers. If problematization is going to rise in this field, the pull will have to come from
somewhere other than the accumulation of reviews.

H3 keeps Sandberg and Alvesson's wager alive without settling it. The four problematizing reviews are
cited far more per year than the gap-spotting mass, and the two most-cited are the two that most
directly contest a premise — Duggan's attack on the aggregated construct and Keegan and Meijerink's on
the employment boundary. That is what the higher-impact claim predicts. But four is four. The honest
reading is that this literature offers a suggestive, underpowered case for the claim, and that a
larger corpus, or a field with more problematizers, would be needed to weigh it.

There is a methodological point in how these three results were reached. Each hypothesis was written to
be falsifiable before the corpus existed, and one of the three came back false in the most useful way —
H2 did not merely fail to rise, it reversed, and the reversal exposed a mechanism the original
prediction had missed. A framing that had treated review-writing as a single scholarly posture turned
out to hide a genre shift, and only the pre-registered contrast between early and late halves made the
shift legible. Counting is what separated a plausible story about a maturing field from what the field
actually did. That is the discipline the systematicity pipeline is for, and the challenged hypothesis
is worth more here than the two supported ones.

For a research program that means to build a new construct on this terrain, the pattern is a standing
invitation. A literature reviewed this often, and contested this rarely, has had its gaps named many
times over and its assumptions named almost never. The scarce and rewarded move is the one almost no
one is making.

## Limitations

The coders are LLM agents, not trained humans. Their agreement is high, but agents can share a
systematic reading bias that a human panel would not, and κ measures consistency, not correctness.

Motivation was coded from title and abstract. An abstract compresses the framing an introduction
carries, and a review can problematize in its introduction while its abstract reads as a gap-spotting
summary. That error runs one way: it moves problematizers into the gap-spotting count, so the true
problematization share is a floor, not a point estimate. The 90 percent should be read as an upper
bound on gap-spotting's dominance being overstated — the real figure could be lower, not higher.

The corpus is bounded by two connectors and by a citation snapshot. A review outside the connectors'
index is outside the corpus, DOIs were not uniformly resolved for connector-sourced items, and the
citation counts are a single July-2026 pull uncorrected for field or database coverage. The H3
problematizer cell is n = 4, and two old, highly cited pieces dominate it; the citation comparison
should be read accordingly. Finally, the H2 reversal is partly a genre shift — the rise of the
bibliometric review mechanically raises gap-spotting — so it reports a change in how the field reviews
itself as much as a change in scholarly posture.
