# What Platform-Governance Research Borrows: Parent Theories, Their Mode of Import, and a Shift Over Time

## Research Summary

Platform-governance research studies an object — the multi-sided digital platform — that no single
discipline owns. A field with a new object and no native theory borrows. This review measures the
borrowing. Eighty platform-governance sources published between 2002 and 2026 were coded by three
independent raters for the parent theory each imports, how it treats that theory, and whether it
combines more than one. Two-sided-market economics carries the early period, holding 60% of pre-2015
sources against 13% for institutional and ecosystem theory combined. The later period turns: the
economics share falls to 22% and ecosystem and complementor theory rises from two sources to eighteen.
Institutional theory, predicted to rise with it, stays at three sources across the whole corpus. A
third of the corpus imports a theory outside the standard platform-theory shelf, mostly labour-process
theory in the gig-work cluster. Ninety percent of sources run on a single theory. And most sources —
70% — present their import as an extension of the parent theory rather than a plain application.
Intercoder reliability is almost perfect on the parent-theory attribution (Fleiss' κ = 0.87) and
substantial on the other two variables. The picture is a young field graduating from platform
*economics* to platform *ecosystems* while borrowing one theory at a time and dressing each import as a
theoretical extension.

## Theory Borrowing in Organization Research

Organization theory borrows formal models from other fields. It always has. Population biology gave it
organizational ecology. Microeconomics gave it transaction-cost theory and agency theory. Economic
sociology gave it embeddedness. A field imports a parent theory when it meets a problem its own tools
cannot reach. The borrowing is a standing way the discipline builds theory, one of several, and it
reflects well or badly on the borrower depending on how it is done. Whose theory does the field reach
for, does the mix change as the field matures, does it apply the borrowed lens or work on the lens
itself, and does it hold one theory at a time or braid several.

The multi-sided digital platform is a good case for these questions. It arrived as an object of study
without a home discipline. Economists saw a two-sided market with cross-side network effects. Strategy
scholars saw a new competitive form. Information-systems researchers saw an architecture of core and
periphery held together by boundary resources. Sociologists of work saw a new apparatus of control over
labor. Each of these communities brought its own parent theory to the platform, and the platform
literature that resulted is a natural place to watch theory borrowing happen in real time — recent
enough that the whole arc is visible, active enough that the mix is still moving.

This review treats that literature as a dataset. The question is not whether platforms are two-sided
markets or ecosystems; it is which theories platform-governance researchers import to answer such
questions, and whether the answer has changed. The question is descriptive in the systematicity typology
(Simsek, Fox, & Heavey, 2023): the output is a measured distribution of borrowing behavior carrying an
intercoder reliability figure.

## Framework

Three claims about the literature's borrowing behavior were fixed before any coding, each written so a
number could falsify it.

The first is a claim about composition and its drift. A young field theorizing a market object should
reach first for the economics of that object, then broaden toward organizational and ecological theory
as it matures and as the object's non-market features — complementors, governance, legitimacy — come
into view. The prediction: transaction-cost economics and two-sided-market theory dominate the early
platform-governance work, and institutional and ecosystem theory rise in later years. The operational
test splits the parent-theory distribution at 2015, the point where platform research turns from
platform economics toward platform organization and ecosystem strategy.

The second is a claim about the mode of import. A field borrowing under time pressure tends to use the
parent theory as a ready tool, applying it to the platform case and leaving the theory itself
untouched. The prediction: most sources borrow-and-apply, rather than extend or critique the parent
theory.

The third is a claim about combination. Parallel single-lens studies accumulate faster than integrated
multi-theoretic ones, because a single import is cheaper to execute and to publish. The prediction:
single-theory imports dominate; sources rarely combine two or more parent theories.

The parent-theory scheme has eight values: transaction-cost economics, two-sided-market theory,
resource-dependence theory, network embeddedness, institutional theory, ecosystem theory, agency and
control theory, and an `other` category for any body of theory outside the seven or for a source with no
identifiable single parent. The scheme fixes the seven theories a reader of the platform literature
would expect and forces everything else into a residual, so the size of the residual is itself a
measurement.

## Method

**Corpus boundary.** A source is in scope if its title or abstract concerns a digital or economic
platform as an organizational or economic object — its governance, strategy, architecture, market
structure, ecosystem, complementors, or the management of platform-mediated work — and imports an
identifiable body of theory. Product platforms in manufacturing, IT middleware, and biological
ecosystems are out. The procedural gate is English-language, indexed by the Scholar Gateway
semantic-search connector, 2001 to present, with a substantive abstract.

**Search.** Eleven natural-language queries crossed platform governance, strategy, ecosystem, and work
with the candidate parent theories, including two period-restricted queries to recover early
platform-economics work that recency-biased search would miss. Candidates were deduplicated by DOI and
title and screened against the boundary rule. The platform-labor cluster returned by the connector was
large; to hold the corpus near the review arm's target size it was capped by keeping every
governance, strategy, economics, and ecosystem source and every pre-2015 source, then filling the
remaining slots from the labor cluster in year order. The screened-out set (roughly 730 candidates) is
logged. The final corpus is 80 sources, 15 published before 2015 and 65 in 2015 or later.

**Coding.** Three coders applied the fixed codebook blind to one another, each coding from the title and
abstract, each writing to its own file. The coders are LLM agents run in parallel — the arm's standard
harness — not trained human raters. Each source received a `parent_theory`, a `borrowing_mode` (apply,
extend, or critique), a `multi_theory` flag, and its year.

**Reliability and analysis.** Fleiss' κ was computed per variable and a majority-vote dataset
adjudicated the three passes. The adjudicated dataset was cross-tabulated against the three hypotheses:
the parent-theory distribution overall and split at 2015 for the first, the borrowing-mode distribution
for the second, the multi-theory rate for the third. The review is content-coding only; none of the
hypotheses is a cross-citation claim, so the citation-graph tooling was not used.

## Results

The three coders agreed closely. Parent-theory attribution reached Fleiss' κ = 0.872 (90.4% mean
pairwise agreement), which Landis and Koch read as almost perfect. Borrowing mode reached κ = 0.678 and
multi-theory κ = 0.760, both substantial. The reliability figure is what a single-coder review cannot
offer; it discharges the objection that one reader's judgment produced the distribution.

**Table 1. Parent-theory frequency (adjudicated, N = 80).**

| parent theory | sources | share |
|---|---|---|
| other | 28 | 35% |
| two-sided market | 21 | 26% |
| ecosystem | 20 | 25% |
| agency / control | 3 | 4% |
| institutional | 3 | 4% |
| transaction-cost economics | 2 | 3% |
| resource dependence | 2 | 3% |
| network embeddedness | 1 | 1% |

Three theories carry most of the corpus: an `other` residual, two-sided-market economics, and ecosystem
theory together are 86% of sources. Transaction-cost economics, the parent theory an organization
scholar might expect to dominate a governance literature, appears as the primary lens in only two
sources. Institutional theory, resource dependence, agency, and network embeddedness are each marginal.

| parent theory | pre-2015 (N=15) | 2015+ (N=65) |
|---|---|---|
| two-sided market | 8 (53%) | 13 (20%) |
| ecosystem | 2 (13%) | 18 (28%) |
| other | 3 (20%) | 25 (38%) |
| institutional | 0 | 3 (5%) |
| transaction-cost economics | 1 (7%) | 1 (2%) |
| agency / control | 0 | 3 (5%) |
| resource dependence | 0 | 2 (3%) |

The split at 2015 shows the drift the first hypothesis predicted, with one correction. Economics leads
early: two-sided-market theory alone is 8 of 15 pre-2015 sources, and with transaction-cost economics
the economics share is 60%, against 13% for institutional and ecosystem theory combined. In the later
period the economics share falls to 22% and the institutional-plus-ecosystem share rises to 32%. The
rise is ecosystem theory: two sources before 2015, eighteen after. Institutional theory does not rise —
it holds at three sources for the whole corpus and never exceeds 5% of a period. The first hypothesis is
supported in direction and in its economics-early leg, and qualified on its institutional leg: the
late-period turn is toward ecosystems and complementors, not toward institutions. The pre-2015 economics
foundations are the two-sided-market papers of Rochet and Tirole's tradition — platform competition,
pricing structure, envelopment — and the later ecosystem papers study complementor engagement, boundary
resources, orchestration, and the platform as meta-organization.

The second hypothesis is challenged, and the direction of the challenge is the interesting part. The
prediction was that platform research applies its borrowed theory. The coders read the reverse: 56 of 80
sources (70%) were coded as extending the parent theory — adding a construct, a boundary condition, or a
mechanism the parent theory did not have — against 23 (29%) that apply it and one that critiques it.
Platform-governance research, as its abstracts present it, is a literature that claims to rework the
theories it imports far more often than it claims to merely use them. A qualification belongs on this
number and is developed below, but the coded result is unambiguous and the three coders converged on it.

The third hypothesis is supported. Multi-theory imports are rare: 8 of 80 sources (10%) braid two or
more parent theories in their core argument; 90% run on a single lens. The field grows by accumulating
parallel single-theory studies.

One result falls outside the three hypotheses and deserves its own line. The largest single value in
Table 1 is the `other` residual, 35% of the corpus. These are sources whose primary theory is not one of
the seven expected platform theories — labour-process theory in the gig and algorithmic-management
cluster above all, joined by entrepreneurship, the resource-based view, stakeholder theory, and
structuration. Over a third of platform-governance research reaches past the standard platform-theory
shelf entirely. The residual is largest in the later period (38% of 2015+ sources), which is the same
period that produced the gig-work and algorithmic-control literature.

## Discussion

The corpus tells a coherent developmental story. Platform-governance research opens as applied platform
economics, borrowing the two-sided-market model that made the object legible in the first place. As the
field matures its attention moves from the platform as a market to the platform as an organized
ecosystem — a core owner orchestrating a periphery of complementors through boundary resources and
governance rules — and its borrowing follows, from economics to ecosystem theory. This is the ordinary
life cycle of a borrowing literature: reach first for the theory that names the object, then for the
theory that explains how the object is organized and held together.

The correction to the pre-registered story is that institutional theory did not join the late-period
rise. This is a genuine gap. A mature literature on the governance of platforms that increasingly face
regulation, contested legitimacy, and competing institutional logics might be expected to import
institutional theory heavily, and it does not — three sources in eighty. The opening is real: the
institutional analysis of platform governance is, on this corpus, a road largely not yet taken.

The challenge to the second hypothesis is the finding most worth dwelling on, and also the one most in
need of a caveat. Reading 70% of sources as theoretical extensions says
something about how platform research presents itself: it advertises novelty, positioning nearly every
import as a contribution back to the parent theory. But the abstract is exactly where a paper makes that
advertisement, and coding the mode of borrowing from the abstract will read a claim of extension wherever
an author makes one, whether or not the full paper delivers it. The challenge to the second hypothesis is
therefore a claim about how the literature *frames* its borrowing, not a verified claim about how much
theory it actually reworks. Framed that way it still matters. A literature in which almost every source
claims to extend its parent theory, while 90% of sources touch only one theory and a third reach outside
the canonical set, is a literature producing many small, parallel, self-described extensions with little
cumulative integration. That combination — high claimed novelty, low multi-theoretic integration — is a
recognizable pattern in a young field, and the numbers name it.

For a research program that imports its own formal apparatus into organization theory, the corpus offers
a plain lesson about borrowing well. The platform literature borrowed economics to make its object
legible and ecosystem theory to explain its organization, one theory at a time, each import framed as an
extension. The theories it underused — institutional theory above all, and transaction-cost economics,
which a governance literature might have leaned on far more than two sources' worth — mark where the same
literature left room. Borrowing a formal model is how organization theory has always built; doing it in a
way that integrates rather than accumulates is the harder part, and this corpus shows a field still on
the accumulating side of that line.

## Limitations

The three coders are LLM agents applying a fixed codebook, not trained human raters. Agreement is high —
κ = 0.87 on the parent-theory attribution — but agent reliability is not a substitute for independent
human coding, and every number here inherits that boundary.

The challenge to the second hypothesis rests on coding the mode of borrowing from abstracts, where
authors advertise novelty. The 70% extension rate should be read as how sources present their borrowing,
not as a measured rate of genuine theoretical rework; a full-text coding would likely move it toward
application.

The corpus is one semantic-search connector's neighborhood, English-language, screened to
platform-as-organization sources. The platform-labor cluster was large and was capped by a stated rule,
and a different cap would move the `other` share, which is the single largest cell. The pre-2015 window
is fifteen sources; the economics-early result rests on a small n, though the effect is large. And
`parent_theory` records one primary theory per source, so a secondary theory named only in the full text
is invisible to the coding, which can understate the multi-theory rate.

None of these bounds touches the shape of the main result. Economics leads early, ecosystem theory rises
late, institutional theory stays away, single-theory imports dominate, and the field frames its borrowing
as extension. The reliability figure and the pre-registered hypotheses are what let a reader take that
shape as measured rather than asserted.
