# Construct clarity and the case for "algorithmacy"

*Deep-research dossier · org_frontier/reviews/deep_research · sources verified against Scholar Gateway passages and publisher records, July 2026*

## The question and why it matters

A companion review in this repository (`org_frontier/reviews/new_form_construct_proliferation/`) found that
organization theory gives roughly one object — coordination that is neither a market nor a bureaucratic
hierarchy — on the order of eighteen distinct construct labels: platform, ecosystem, meta-organization,
community, partial organization, network organization, hybrid, open collaboration, heterarchy, holacracy,
adhocracy, and a long tail beyond. This is the jangle fallacy at the scale of a subfield: many names for
phenomena that may be the same thing.

The dissertation coins a new term, **algorithmacy**, and enters exactly this crowded space. The construct
is defined as *Φ-graded irreducible coordination*: a coordination form whose irreducibility is measured
by integrated information (IIT-4.0 Φ) computed over the minimum-information partition. A new coinage in an
already-inflated naming space carries an obvious burden. A reviewer will ask whether algorithmacy is a
genuine addition or an eighteenth synonym dressed in physics.

The metascience literature supplies the standard by which that question is answered. Two bodies of work
matter most: Suddaby's construct-clarity criteria for management and organization theory, and Gerring's
criterial framework for concept goodness in the social sciences. Around them sit the psychometric
literature on construct redundancy and the jingle-jangle fallacies, and a set of organization-theory
statements that name proliferation as a field problem. This dossier verifies each source, states the
criterion the dissertation should adopt from it, and then applies the consolidated standard to
algorithmacy — the criteria it satisfies by virtue of a positive, measurable defining mechanism, and the
criteria it must still earn.

## Part I — The clarity and concept-goodness standards

### Suddaby (2010) — the four components of construct clarity

**Citation (verified).** Suddaby, R. 2010. "Editor's Comments: Construct Clarity in Theories of Management
and Organization." *Academy of Management Review* 35(3): 346–357. DOI 10.5465/amr.35.3.zok346.

**Core argument (from the source and from works quoting it directly).** Suddaby defines clear constructs as
"robust [empirically stable] categories that distill phenomena into sharp distinctions that are
theoretically comprehensible to a community of researchers" (p. 346, quoted in Lindsay 2018). Clarity is
the precondition for strong theory, since a theory is a system of constructs related by propositions, and
theorists must evaluate the constructs before analyzing the relations (p. 347). Construct clarity has four
interrelated components: (1) **definitions** that capture essential properties, avoid tautology and
self-reference, and are parsimonious; (2) **scope conditions** — the contexts of time, space, and values
under which the construct does and does not apply; (3) **semantic relationships** to related constructs,
because "theoretical constructs do not exist in isolation, but are suspended in a complex web of references
to and relationships with other constructs" (p. 350, quoted in Atewologun et al. 2017); and (4)
**coherence** — consistency of the construct with the overall theoretical argument. A good definition also
discusses a construct's historical lineage: how and why the focal construct differs from prior concepts
and from others bearing the same name (Lindsay 2018, paraphrasing Suddaby). Clarity balances accuracy,
parsimony, and generality; it cannot maximize all three at once (p. 351).

**Verification note.** The primary article is confirmed by publisher record (AOM). Its four criteria and
page-level quotations are corroborated verbatim across four independent secondary sources in the Scholar
Gateway corpus: Sadeghiani et al. (2021, *J. Theory of Social Behaviour*, DOI 10.1111/jtsb.12329),
Lindsay (2018, *Accounting Perspectives*, DOI 10.1111/1911-3838.12188), Skilton (2011, *J. Supply Chain
Management*, DOI 10.1111/j.1745-493X.2011.03222.x), and Atewologun et al. (2017, *Int. J. Management
Reviews*, DOI 10.1111/ijmr.12148).

**Criterion the dissertation should adopt.** Present algorithmacy under all four Suddaby components
explicitly, and treat the fourth definitional demand — historical lineage, stating how the focal construct
differs from prior concepts bearing related names — as the section that answers the jangle charge head on.

### Gerring (1999) — eight criteria for a good concept

**Citation (verified).** Gerring, J. 1999. "What Makes a Concept Good? A Criterial Framework for
Understanding Concept Formation in the Social Sciences." *Polity* 31(3): 357–393. DOI 10.2307/3235246.

**Core argument (from the source, reproduced in a verbatim criterion table).** Gerring argues that concept
formation is governed by a set of criteria that trade off against one another, so concept choice is a
matter of maximizing across competing desiderata rather than satisfying a checklist. The eight criteria,
as tabulated by Jensen (2026) directly from Gerring:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Familiarity | How consonant is the concept with established usage? It should not depart radically from accepted meanings. |
| Resonance | Does the term catch attention and stay memorable? (e.g., "exit, voice, loyalty," "street-level bureaucrats"). |
| Parsimony | How short is the list of defining attributes? Reach should be achieved with as few terms as possible. |
| Coherence | How internally consistent are the instances and attributes? Are the dimensions logically interrelated? |
| Differentiation | How well bounded is the concept against neighboring concepts? |
| Depth | How many additional properties are shared by the instances the concept bundles? |
| Theoretical utility | How useful is the concept within a wider field of inferences — does it promote explanation and generalization? |
| Field utility | How useful is the concept within a field of related terms — does it map cleanly onto a set of objects? |

Coakley (2017) states the same eight and groups them: three concern acceptability to scholars (familiarity,
resonance, parsimony), five concern functional efficiency (coherence, differentiation, depth, theoretical
utility, field utility). Gerring holds that describing a phenomenon precisely "demands plenitude," so
concepts that are deep and differentiated are better suited to that task (Gerring 1999: 380, quoted in
Valsangiacomo 2021).

**Verification note.** Primary article confirmed by publisher record (University of Chicago Press / *Polity*)
and Semantic Scholar. The eight-criterion framework is reproduced verbatim in Jensen (2026, *Public
Administration Review*, DOI 10.1111/puar.70084, Table 5) and enumerated independently by Coakley (2017,
*Nations and Nationalism*, DOI 10.1111/nana.12349) and Valsangiacomo (2021, *Swiss Political Science
Review*, DOI 10.1111/spsr.12486). Note the near-synonym: Gerring's later book (2012) restates the set as
seven criteria (resonance, domain, consistency, fecundity, differentiation, causal utility,
operationalization), per Sadeghiani et al. (2021). The dissertation should cite the 1999 *Polity* article
for the eight-criterion version.

**Criterion the dissertation should adopt.** Use Gerring's eight as the scoring rubric for the head-to-head
comparison between algorithmacy and the ~18 incumbent labels. Differentiation, depth, and theoretical/field
utility are where a measured construct can outscore unmeasured ones; familiarity and resonance are where a
neologism starts at a deficit and must be managed.

### Thorndike (1904) and Kelley (1927) — the jingle and jangle fallacies

**Citations (verified via secondary attribution).** Thorndike, E. L. 1904. *An Introduction to the Theory of
Mental and Social Measurements* — origin of the **jingle fallacy**. Kelley, T. L. 1927. *Interpretation of
Educational Measurements* — origin of the **jangle fallacy**.

**Core argument.** The jingle fallacy treats two distinct phenomena as identical because they share a name.
The jangle fallacy treats one phenomenon as two because it carries two names. DeRue et al. (2012) summarize:
a jingle "involves conflating constructs that superficially appear similar but are fundamentally distinct,"
whereas a jangle "involves the presumption that similar constructs with different names are actually
different" (citing Block 1995; Kelley 1927; Marsh 1994). Both, DeRue et al. note, "typically occur when
independent researchers pay no mind to existing constructs in the field while inventing new ones." Hodson
(2021) locates the root cause in a mathematical fact the field tends to ignore: if within-construct and
between-construct indicator correlations are comparable, the latent factors correlate near-perfectly and
the constructs are redundant.

**Verification note.** Origins confirmed via Hodson (2021, DOI 10.1002/jts5.120), DeRue et al. (2012,
*Industrial and Organizational Psychology*, DOI 10.1111/j.1754-9434.2012.01465.x), and corroborating
reference works. The eighteen-name finding in the companion review is, in this vocabulary, a documented
jangle at subfield scale.

**Criterion the dissertation should adopt.** State plainly which fallacy algorithmacy risks — the jangle —
and answer it on the fallacy's own terms: not by asserting the name is new, but by identifying a defining
property (Φ over the MIP) that the incumbent labels do not carry and that in principle admits a
discriminant-validity test.

### Le, Schmidt, Harter & Lauver (2010) — empirical redundancy as a measurable fact

**Citation (verified).** Le, H., Schmidt, F. L., Harter, J. K., & Lauver, K. J. 2010. "The problem of
empirical redundancy of constructs in organizational research: An empirical investigation."
*Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 112(2): 112–125. DOI 10.1016/j.obhdp.2010.02.003.

**Core argument.** Conceptual distinctness does not guarantee empirical distinctness. Le et al. propose a
confirmatory-factor-analysis method, using three hierarchically nested models and measures collected at
short intervals, to estimate the true-score correlation between two constructs after correcting for random,
specific-factor, and transient measurement error. Applied to job satisfaction and organizational commitment
— two constructs almost everyone treats as distinct — the corrected lower-bound correlation was about .91
to .92, indicating empirical redundancy despite a clear conceptual distinction (Harter & Schmidt 2008;
Ree & Carretta 2018, both summarizing Le et al.). The lesson: a distinction that lives only in the
theorist's mind, and that respondents or cases do not reproduce, is of little use for building theory.

**Verification note.** Primary article confirmed via ScienceDirect and ERIC records. Findings corroborated
directly in the Scholar Gateway corpus by Harter & Schmidt (2008, *Industrial and Organizational
Psychology*, DOI 10.1111/j.1754-9434.2007.00004.x) and Ree & Carretta (2018, DOI 10.1111/ijsa.12219).

**Criterion the dissertation should adopt.** Distinctness is an empirical claim, not a definitional one.
Algorithmacy's differentiation from the incumbents becomes a testable proposition: two coordination forms
that receive different labels but return the same Φ verdict on the same partition structure are, on this
dimension, not distinct. The construct supplies its own redundancy test rather than resting on a naming
claim.

### Shaffer, DeGeest & Li (2016) — the discriminant-validity playbook

**Citation (verified).** Shaffer, J. A., DeGeest, D., & Li, A. 2016. "Tackling the Problem of Construct
Proliferation: A Guide to Assessing the Discriminant Validity of Conceptually Related Constructs."
*Organizational Research Methods* 19(1): 80–110. DOI 10.1177/1094428115598239.

**Core argument.** Construct proliferation — "the accumulation of ostensibly different but potentially
identical constructs representing organizational phenomena" — is a salient contemporary problem, and few
validation studies actually assess whether a new construct is empirically distinct from its neighbors.
Shaffer et al. give a step-by-step guide to establishing discriminant validity while accounting for three
sources of measurement error (random, specific-factor, transient), so that a proposed new construct must
demonstrate empirical distinctness rather than merely assert conceptual novelty.

**Verification note.** Primary article confirmed via SAGE, Groningen repository, and Semantic Scholar.
Cited as the proliferation-and-discriminant-validity reference by Terekhin & Aurora (2024, *J. Organizational
Behavior*, DOI 10.1002/job.2845) and Ree & Carretta (2018) within the corpus.

**Criterion the dissertation should adopt.** Adopt discriminant validity as the burden of proof for any new
construct in a crowded space, and name the specific test the eventual measurement work will run. The
Φ-over-MIP operationalization gives algorithmacy a concrete quantity to submit to such a test; that
possibility is itself a clarity asset the unmeasured labels lack.

### Organization theory names its own proliferation problem

**Bremer (2026).** "New organizational forms: Deconstructing and reconfiguring the literature." *European
Management Review* (early view). DOI 10.1111/emre.70060. A review of 180 articles finds the "new
organizational form" concept "ill-defined at the core and fuzzy at the edges" (quoting Foss 2002), used for
phenomena as varied as coworking spaces, app-based house calls, movie theaters, and the gig economy, and
attached to a growing roster of fashionable labels — Zappos alone has been cast as liberated, Holacracy,
Teal, purpose-driven, self-managing, less-hierarchical, flat, and agile. Bremer names the dynamic an
"intense label game" and notes the concept follows a "family resemblance structure" with no feature
strictly necessary or sufficient. The verified organization-theory statement that proliferation is real and
unresolved.

**Coulombel & Berkowitz (2024).** "One name for two concepts: A systematic literature review about
meta-organizations." *International Journal of Management Reviews* 27(2): 151–173. DOI 10.1111/ijmr.12385.
Even within a single label the field splits into two incompatible definitions (Ahrne & Brunsson's
organization-of-organizations versus Gulati et al.'s system-level-goal collective), which the authors argue
creates three risks: knowledge fragmentation (research in silos producing redundant or contradictory
findings), conceptual irrelevance (the term ceases to be a useful tool), and theoretical misfit (insights
transposed inappropriately across streams). Jingle and jangle operate simultaneously in the same subfield.

**Ree & Carretta (2018).** "Eight issues in studying organizational constructs." *International Journal of
Selection and Assessment* 26(2-4): 124–132. DOI 10.1111/ijsa.12219. States the mechanism directly: "This
proliferation of labels of constructs leads to confusion about the number and nature of constructs," and
new constructs are "frequently being advocated without sufficient construct validation to determine if they
[are] unique or if they replicate existing constructs."

**Criterion the dissertation should adopt.** The proliferation problem is acknowledged by the field, not
imposed on it from outside. Algorithmacy can be positioned as a response to that stated problem — a
construct built to be discriminable — rather than as one more entry in the label game.

## Part II — The consolidated standard

The sources converge on a compact standard. A construct entering a crowded naming space is clear and good,
rather than a jangle duplicate, to the extent that it satisfies the following.

1. **Positive definition (Suddaby 1; Gerring parsimony/coherence).** The construct is defined by the
   properties it *has* — a coordinating mechanism — not by what it is not. Definition by contrast is the
   companion review's recorded weakness in ~40% of new-form sources; a positive mechanism is the antidote.

2. **Differentiation (Gerring; Suddaby 3).** The construct is bounded against its neighbors by a stated
   feature the neighbors lack, and that boundary is specified as a semantic relationship, not left implicit.

3. **Empirical distinctness (Le et al.; Shaffer et al.).** Differentiation is a testable claim. The
   construct admits a discriminant-validity or redundancy test, and its distinctness survives correction for
   measurement error rather than living only in the theorist's mind.

4. **Depth (Gerring).** The construct bundles instances that share further properties beyond the defining
   one, so classifying a case under it is informative.

5. **Theoretical and field utility (Gerring; Suddaby coherence).** The construct earns inferences, supports
   generalization, and maps onto a recognizable set of cases.

6. **Scope conditions (Suddaby 2).** The contexts of application and non-application are stated.

7. **Familiarity and resonance (Gerring).** The term is comprehensible and memorable to its audience without
   departing wildly from accepted usage.

8. **Lineage and coherence (Suddaby 1 and 4).** The construct states how it differs from prior concepts,
   including those bearing related names, and sits consistently inside its own theoretical argument.

A jangle duplicate is a construct that scores well on familiarity and resonance — it sounds new and reads
well — while failing 2, 3, and 5: it is not bounded against neighbors, not shown to be empirically distinct,
and adds no inferential leverage. The eighteen new-form labels are, on the companion review's evidence, at
risk on precisely those dimensions.

## Part III — Applying the standard to algorithmacy

Algorithmacy is defined as Φ-graded irreducible coordination: a coordination form whose irreducibility is
measured by integrated information (IIT-4.0 Φ) over the minimum-information partition. Assessed against the
consolidated standard, the construct is strong on the dimensions that a measured mechanism buys, and unproven
on the dimensions that any new coinage must earn over time. The honest split follows.

### Criteria algorithmacy satisfies by virtue of a positive, measurable mechanism

**Positive definition (Standard 1) — satisfied.** Algorithmacy is defined by a property the form possesses:
irreducibility, graded by Φ over the MIP. The companion review found that the incumbent labels lean heavily
on contrast — coordination that is "beyond markets and hierarchies," "less hierarchical," "non-traditional."
Bremer (2026) confirms the pattern at the level of the parent concept: "new organizational form" is defined
as a configuration that *distinguishes it from established forms*, with no positive feature agreed as
necessary. Algorithmacy replaces the contrast with a mechanism and a number.

**Differentiation (Standard 2) — satisfied in principle.** The construct carries a boundary the ~18 labels do
not: a specific quantity computed by a specific procedure. Platform, ecosystem, and meta-organization are
distinguished from one another largely by the empirical setting a study happened to draw from, not by a
shared discriminating property. Φ over the MIP is a discriminating property, and it applies across settings.
This is Gerring differentiation grounded in an operation rather than an intuition.

**Depth (Standard 4) — satisfied, conditionally.** Because Φ is graded, algorithmacy does not merely sort
forms into a bin; it orders them and connects each to the partition structure that produced its value. A
form's Φ value carries further information — where the irreducibility sits, how a design change moves it —
so classifying a case under algorithmacy tells a researcher more than the label alone. Depth is real to the
extent the Φ computation is tractable and stable on the cases of interest, which the experiments in Paper 3
are designed to probe.

**Theoretical utility (Standard 5) — satisfied at the level of the research program.** A measured construct
earns inferences the unmeasured ones cannot: whether a coordination form is irreducible, by how much, and
which partition is the weak seam. That is the affirmative contribution — IIT enables a principled
exploration of irreducibility that the naming literature has no apparatus to attempt. The value is the
principled exploration the tool makes possible, not a claim that Φ is the only way to reach any given
verdict.

**Empirical distinctness as a well-posed question (Standard 3) — satisfied as a *test the construct admits*,
not yet as a *result*.** This is the decisive clarity advantage. Le et al. (2010) and Shaffer et al. (2016)
insist that distinctness be demonstrated empirically after correcting for measurement error. Most of the
eighteen labels cannot be submitted to such a test, because they carry no measured defining quantity — there
is nothing to correlate. Algorithmacy carries one. The construct supplies the very instrument by which its
own redundancy could be checked. Whether the result comes back "distinct" is an open empirical matter; that
the question is *answerable* is a property the incumbents lack.

**Lineage (part of Standard 8) — satisfied by construction.** The dissertation's genealogy work states where
Φ comes from and how algorithmacy differs from the labels it neighbors. Suddaby's demand that a definition
narrate its historical lineage is met directly.

### Criteria algorithmacy must still earn

**Familiarity and resonance (Standard 7) — not yet earned; a neologism's structural deficit.** Gerring warns
that a good concept should not depart radically from accepted usage. "Algorithmacy" is a coinage built on an
apparatus (IIT) foreign to organization theory. It starts at a familiarity deficit that platform and
ecosystem — everyday words — do not carry. Resonance is plausible (the term is short and memorable), but
resonance without familiarity can read as jargon. This is earned only through uptake: use by other scholars,
teaching, recurrence in the literature. It cannot be asserted at coinage.

**Scope conditions (Standard 6) — stated but not yet validated.** The construct's range of application —
which coordination forms, at what level, over what unit of analysis — is specified in the theory, but the
boundary where Φ-graded irreducibility stops being informative is an empirical question the field cases have
not yet settled. The companion review flags that no worker is measured and irreducibility is *explored*, not
established as necessary; the scope within which the measured claim holds is exactly what remains open.

**Empirical distinctness as a *result* (Standard 3, completion) — the central unfinished task.** The
construct admits the redundancy test; it has not passed one. Demonstrating that algorithmacy's Φ verdicts
diverge from what a cheaper proxy would return on the same cases — that the measured construct does work a
factorization heuristic does not — is the discriminant-validity study the standard demands and the
dissertation has not yet run. The affirmative thesis does not require Φ to *beat* every cheap test on every
case; it requires showing that the principled exploration Φ enables yields understanding the proxies do not.
That demonstration is future work, and the standard is candid that until it exists, distinctness is claimed
rather than shown.

**Field utility (Standard 5, completion) — pending adoption.** Gerring's field utility asks whether the
construct maps cleanly onto a set of recognized cases and is used across them. That is a social fact about a
research community, achievable only after the term circulates. At coinage it is aspiration.

### The honest verdict

Algorithmacy clears the clarity bar on the dimensions a positive, measured mechanism can buy at the moment of
coinage: positive definition, differentiation in principle, depth, theoretical utility, lineage, and — most
importantly — the status of a construct whose distinctness is a *well-posed empirical question* rather than a
naming assertion. On the metascience standard, that is precisely what separates a legitimate new construct
from a jangle duplicate: the duplicate cannot be tested against its neighbors because it carries no measured
defining property; algorithmacy can. The construct is not yet proven distinct, familiar, or field-adopted,
and the standard is explicit that those are earned through empirical work and uptake, not through definition.
The claim the dissertation can defend now is that algorithmacy is well-formed and discriminable-in-principle,
built to answer the proliferation problem the field has named, and equipped with the one thing the eighteen
incumbents lack — an operational quantity by which its novelty can be put to the test.

## How this feeds Paper 1 and Paper 2

**Paper 1 (the literature review that finds space for algorithmacy).** This dossier supplies the defensive
apparatus for the "space for a new construct" argument. The move is: (a) document the jangle — the ~18-label
proliferation, with Bremer (2026), Coulombel & Berkowitz (2024), and Ree & Carretta (2018) as the field's
own admissions that the problem is real; (b) install the standard — Suddaby's four components and Gerring's
eight criteria as the rubric a legitimate new construct must satisfy; (c) show the incumbents' weakness on
differentiation, empirical distinctness, and utility, using Le et al. (2010) and Shaffer et al. (2016) to
make distinctness an empirical rather than a definitional matter; and (d) introduce algorithmacy as the
construct that scores where the incumbents fail, while stating candidly which criteria remain to be earned.
Paper 1 should present algorithmacy under all four Suddaby headings, with a dedicated lineage passage that
answers the jangle charge on its own terms.

**Paper 2 (the affirmative case for Φ / IIT as the tool).** The construct-clarity argument is where Paper 2's
central claim gets its teeth. The reason Φ is a great tool for exploring the mediated triad is precisely the
clarity property this dossier isolates: Φ over the MIP is the operational quantity that turns "is this form
distinct?" from a naming dispute into an answerable question, and that lets irreducibility be graded and
explored rather than merely asserted. Paper 2 should frame the Shaffer et al. discriminant-validity standard
as the bar the construct is *built to be able to meet*, and position the Paper 3 experiments as the
principled exploration that a measured construct uniquely enables — the affirmative contribution — without
overclaiming that Φ is necessary or that it must beat every cheap proxy to be worth running.
