# One Object, Many Names: Measuring Construct Proliferation in the Literature on the New Organizational Form

## Research Summary

Organization theory has spent three decades naming an alternative to the bureaucratic firm, and the
names have multiplied faster than the agreements among them. This study treats that naming as data. It
codes 67 sources that theorize a form of organizing other than the market or the hierarchy and asks
three questions a number can answer: how many distinct construct labels the literature uses, whether it
defines the form negatively or by a positive mechanism, and whether the label-camps cite one another.
Three independent coders reach almost-perfect agreement on the construct label (Fleiss' κ = 0.966).
Eight closed-set labels are in active use and a residual bin resolves to roughly ten more, so the field
gives one recurring object on the order of eighteen names. A majority of sources (60%) define the form
by a positive coordinating mechanism rather than by contrast with hierarchy or market, reversing the
"neither market nor hierarchy" gloss. On the citation graph the camps connect through a single
meta-organization hub and are otherwise near-isolated: of the external papers that cite any source,
7,082 cite one camp and only two cite four. The contribution is as much methodological as substantive.
Construct proliferation has been documented in leadership, resilience, and work–nonwork balance by
running discriminant-validity tests on the shared *measures* of a construct, but a construct family
whose members share no measurement instrument — as organizational forms do not — lies outside that
method's reach. A coded corpus paired with a citation-assembly graph measures proliferation where
psychometrics cannot, and it returns a jangle fallacy at the scale of a subfield alongside a small
integrating core.

## Introduction

The jangle fallacy is the error of treating one thing under two names as two things. Thorndike named it
a century ago, and psychometricians have invoked it since to explain how a field can accumulate
constructs faster than it accumulates knowledge (Kelley, 1927). Management has its own version of the
worry. Suddaby (2010) told authors at the *Academy of Management Review* that "construct clarity" is
among the most common grounds for rejection and among the least well specified, and Cornelissen,
Höllerer, and Seidl (2026) have since catalogued the specific reasoning errors — a novelty fallacy, a
self-referential fallacy, a reification fallacy, a weak-analogy fallacy — through which management
constructs proliferate. The field knows it has a proliferation problem. What it rarely has is a measure
of one.

Organization theory is a strong candidate for the fallacy at scale. Since Powell (1990) set the network
form beside the market and the hierarchy, and through the wave of post-bureaucratic writing that
followed, the field has produced a steady supply of labels for coordination that runs neither through a
chain of command nor through a price. Platform, ecosystem, meta-organization, community, network
organization, partial organization, open collaboration, hybrid: each label carries its own review
essays, its own special issues, its own canonical citations. Whether these are distinct phenomena or one
phenomenon seen from several angles is rarely asked, because asking it requires stepping outside any
single label's literature and reading across all of them at once.

This study takes that outside position and measures what it sees. It states three conditions under which
a construct proliferates without penalty, renders each as a hypothesis a number can settle, and tests
all three against a screened corpus of the field's own writing. A construct proliferates freely when
three things hold together. The labels multiply. Each is defined loosely enough to overlap its
neighbors. And the camps do not read each other, so no one is positioned to notice the redundancy. The
first condition is a count of labels. The second is a coding of how each source establishes what its
form is. The third is a property of the citation graph. Measuring all three at once is what a study from
inside any single label's literature cannot do, and it is what turns a familiar complaint into a result
another researcher can reproduce.

The paper's contribution is methodological before it is substantive. Construct proliferation has been
documented carefully in several domains — leadership (Bormann & Rowold, 2018), organizational resilience
(Raetze, Duchek, Maynard, & Wu, 2021), and work–nonwork balance (Casper, Vaziri, Wayne, DeHauw, &
Greenhaus, 2018) — and the standard tool for documenting it is a discriminant-validity study that
correlates the *measures* attached to competing constructs and asks whether they are empirically
distinct (Shaffer, DeGeest, & Li, 2016). That tool is powerful and it is unavailable here. Organizational
forms are not psychological constructs with validated scales; there is no "platform inventory" to
correlate against a "meta-organization inventory." A construct family that shares no measurement
instrument lies outside the discriminant-validity method entirely. This study measures its proliferation
by a different route — a coded corpus of the labels the field uses, joined to a citation-assembly graph
that records whether the camps ever get read together — and in doing so it offers a procedure for a
class of proliferation the psychometric approach cannot reach.

Two clarifications frame what follows. First, the claim is not that the labeled forms are identical.
Platforms and communities and meta-organizations differ, and a careful theorist can draw the
differences. The claim is that they are *variations on one object* — coordination that is neither a
price system nor a command hierarchy — differentiated in each camp's private vocabulary, and that the
field has produced far more labels for that object than it has produced comparisons among them. Jangle
does not require sameness; it requires overlap plus inattention to the overlap. Second, the study's own
lab is a party to the problem it measures: the program that produced this review proposes a further
construct, algorithmacy, for a coordination form near this cluster. That is a reason to measure the
crowding before adding to it, and the discipline the measurement implies applies to the measurer.

## Theoretical background

### Construct proliferation and how it has been measured

Management's construct-clarity literature is largely prescriptive. Suddaby (2010) specifies what a clear
construct requires — definitions, scope conditions, semantic relationships, coherence — and Cornelissen
et al. (2026) specify the fallacies that clarity guards against. Bowling, Khazon, Alarcon, and colleagues
(2026), writing in industrial-organizational psychology, put the prescription at its bluntest with a
call for a moratorium: "no new constructs" until the field disciplines the ones it has. These are
arguments about how scholars *should* build constructs. They establish that proliferation is a
recognized pathology; they do not measure how far any particular literature has gone.

The measurement work lives mostly in psychology, and it has a characteristic method. Casper et al.
(2018) assembled 290 papers on work–nonwork balance, extracted 233 conceptual definitions, clustered
them into five types, and computed Euclidean distances to quantify how far the definitions had diverged.
Raetze et al. (2021) took 1,667 articles on organizational resilience, ran a bibliographic-coupling
analysis into ten historical clusters, and analyzed the subconstructs and definitions each cluster
carried. Shaffer et al. (2016), writing in this journal, generalized the underlying move into a
step-by-step procedure: to test whether two constructs are really distinct, collect their measures,
correlate them while modeling three sources of measurement error, and read the discriminant validity
off the disattenuated correlations. Their illustration comes from leadership, where competing styles
(transformational, ethical, authentic, servant) carry validated scales that can be correlated. The
method is now standard, and it is why proliferation critiques cluster in domains built on
psychometric scales.

That is precisely the boundary this study runs into and past. The new organizational form is not a
psychological attribute measured by a scale. No instrument operationalizes "ecosystem" as a set of
items that could be correlated with a set of items for "meta-organization." The discriminant-validity
method has nothing to correlate. What the field does leave behind is text — the labels it coins, the
way it defines them, and the citations that connect or fail to connect them — and text is measurable by
other means. Casper et al.'s definitional clustering and Raetze et al.'s bibliographic coupling both
point the way; this study joins a coding of *how each source differentiates its form* to a
*citation-assembly* measure of whether the labels are ever read together, and it applies the pair to a
construct family that has no scales at all.

### Three conditions, three hypotheses

The knowledge-weaving typology of Simsek, Heavey, Fox, and Yu (2022) sorts a field's knowledge claims
into stylized facts, key assumptions, enduring critiques, and substantive omissions, and treats each as
a source of testable questions. The three conditions for penalty-free proliferation map onto three such
claims, and the systematicity framework of Simsek, Fox, and Heavey (2023) supplies the pipeline that
answers them with a stated boundary, a reported search, independent coding, and a reliability figure.

**Many labels for one object (H1).** The stylized fact the field half-acknowledges is that it uses many
names. Rendered as a number, the claim is that more than eight distinct construct labels are in active
use for overlapping new-form phenomena. The operationalization is a count of the labels coded across the
corpus. Because a raw count is sensitive to the codebook's resolution, the study reports the count at
several thresholds — labels carried by at least five sources, by at least two, and by at least one — so
that the headline figure can be read against the rule that produced it.

**Defined by mechanism, not by contrast (H2).** The key assumption, usually stated as a compliment to
the field's lineage, is that the new form is "neither market nor hierarchy," defined against the two
classical modes. If that framing dominates, most sources should establish the form by contrast rather
than by a positive coordinating mechanism. The operationalization codes each source's differentia mode
as by-contrast or positive-mechanism, with a parent-form variable recording which baseline, if any, the
source invokes. The hypothesis predicts by-contrast in the majority; the study reports the split as it
falls.

**Camps that do not read each other (H3).** The substantive omission is that the camps proliferate in
isolation. If so, the citation graph built from the corpus should be block-diagonal by label — sources
cite within their own camp and ignore the others. The operationalization uses the coded label as a
cluster key and measures within-label against cross-label citation links, and it adds an
assembly-spanning count: of the external papers that cite any source in the corpus, how many reach one
camp, two, three, or four. The spanning count is the study's sharpest instrument, because it reads not
the seeds' own reference lists but the behavior of the entire downstream literature that cites them.

The hypotheses were fixed and committed before any source was harvested or coded. A hypothesis the data
contradict is reported as a finding, not repaired.

## Method

**Corpus boundary.** A source is in scope if its title or abstract proposes, adopts, or theorizes an
alternative to the bureaucratic firm as a distinct organizational form. Three gates exclude near-misses:
a product platform used only as a technical feature, a firm-strategy paper that borrows "ecosystem" as a
metaphor without theorizing a form, and an organizational-change paper with no form construct are all
out. The corpus is English-language and indexed by the Scholar Gateway semantic-search connector over
Semantic Scholar, Scopus, PubMed, and ArXiv. The boundary and its gates were stated before the search,
following the explicating practice of Simsek et al. (2023).

**Search.** Nine semantic queries paired "new organizational form" with each candidate label — platform,
meta-organization, ecosystem, partial organization, community, network organization, open collaboration,
post-bureaucratic and self-organizing forms, and hybrid organization. The union returned 108 unique
sources. Screening against the boundary dropped 41 — review essays, human-resources and personality
studies, policy analyses, product-development and firm-strategy papers, and other off-boundary items,
each logged with a reason in `literature/screened_out.jsonl` — leaving 67. The search is a designed
convenience sample of the label space, not a census of the field; its completeness is bounded by the
connector's coverage, a limitation addressed below.

**Coding.** A fixed codebook assigns four categorical variables to each source from its title and
abstract: label (the construct the source foregrounds, a closed set of nine plus `other`),
differentia_mode, parent_form, and claim_type (the knowledge-weaving type of the central claim). Three
independent coders applied the codebook blind to one another, and a majority-vote adjudicated dataset is
used for all downstream analysis. Reliability is Fleiss' κ per variable.

The coders are large-language-model agents running the same codebook under different seeds, not trained
human raters, and the choice deserves an argument rather than only a caveat. Using language models to
map conceptual overlap is an emerging and deliberately advocated method for exactly this problem: Wulff
and Mata (2025) propose large language models as a way out of the "jingle-jangle jungle," placing
constructs in a shared semantic space to surface redundancy, and Hanfstingl and colleagues (2025) find
natural-language-processing approaches among the most promising tools for detecting these fallacies at
scale. The model coders here are used in that spirit — as a transparent, reproducible application of a
fixed codebook whose every judgment is logged. Their agreement is high, and near-perfect on the label
variable the headline depends on (κ = 0.966), which shows the codebook is applied consistently. High
agreement among model passes is not, however, evidence that the codebook tracks the construct as a
human expert would; that check requires human coding. A human-validation study on a stratified subsample
is specified as the precondition for submission (see Limitations), and the present figures are reported
as the model-coded baseline it would validate.

**Citation graph.** For each of the 67 seeds, backward references and forward citers were harvested from
the Semantic Scholar Graph API. The adjudicated label is the cluster key. The cluster-to-cluster matrix,
the within-versus-cross link counts, and the assembly-spanning distribution follow from the harvested
edges. Semantic Scholar elides outbound references for many publishers, so the seed-to-seed matrix leans
on the inbound-citer channel; the assembly-spanning count, which uses the full set of downstream citers,
does not share that limitation and is the more robust of the two graph measures.

## Results

**Reliability.** Agreement was high on all four variables and near-perfect on the one the headline
depends on. The label variable reached κ = 0.966 (97.0% pairwise agreement); parent_form κ = 0.863;
differentia_mode κ = 0.791; claim_type κ = 0.738. By Landis and Koch's (1977) benchmarks the first three
are almost-perfect or substantial and the last is substantial. The count of construct labels is not an
artifact of a single reader.

**H1 — supported, and larger than predicted.** The corpus spreads across eight of the nine closed-set
labels, with a large residual tail.

| ecosystem | 7 |

Read at three thresholds, the count is robust to the counting rule. Seven labels are carried by at least
five sources; eight by at least two; and the `other` bin, on inspection, is not residue. Its 14 sources
resolve to at least ten further named forms — heterarchy, holacracy, adhocracy and the non-traditional
organization, the fractal social organization, the prosocial cooperative, the actor-oriented
architecture of collaboration, the corporate hub, the principle of distributed control, the alternative
enterprise, and dual forms of organizing. Counting the eight populated closed-set labels together with
the tail, the literature gives one object — coordination that is neither a price system nor a command
hierarchy — on the order of eighteen distinct construct labels. Even the most conservative reading, the
eight labels each carried by two or more sources, clears the pre-registered threshold of more than
eight; the full reading clears it more than twofold. This is the jangle fallacy at the scale of a
subfield.

**H2 — challenged.** The field does not, in the majority, define the new form by what it is not. Forty of
67 sources (60%) establish the form by a positive coordinating mechanism; 27 (40%) work by contrast to
hierarchy, market, or bureaucracy. The mechanisms are specific to each camp: modularity and design rules
for platforms and ecosystems, membership and the organization-of-organizations for meta-organizations,
combined institutional logics for hybrids, peer and commons governance for open collaboration. The
parent_form distribution corroborates the reversal. Thirty-two sources invoke no explicit
market-or-hierarchy baseline at all, against 19 that position against hierarchy, 12 against market, and 4
against network. The "neither market nor hierarchy" slogan is present in the literature but does not
describe how most of it defines its object.

**H3 — qualified.** The label-to-label citation matrix, built on deduplicated seed pairs, is not
block-diagonal.

Within-camp links total 19 and cross-camp links total 20, so the isolation predicted by H3 does not hold
in aggregate. What holds instead is a hub-and-spoke structure. Meta-organization is the hub: 10 links
inside its own camp and 15 out to ecosystem, platform, and the `other` tail. Platform reaches the graph
only through that hub, with no within-camp seed links of its own. The periphery is siloed as predicted —
community, network organization, open collaboration, partial organization, and hybrid each show at most
one off-diagonal link, and hybrid's two links are entirely internal.

The assembly-spanning test reads the wider literature the same way, and it is not subject to the
outbound-reference gap. Of the external papers that cite any seed, 7,082 cite seeds from a single camp,
321 span two camps, 30 span three, and only two span four. The redundancy across labels is almost never
assembled by anyone downstream. The verdict is qualified rather than challenged: proliferation-in-
isolation is real for the periphery and for the reading public, while a small meta-organization core
integrates a handful of adjacent constructs.

**Claim types.** The knowledge-weaving coding sharpens the picture. Twenty-seven of 67 sources treat
their form as an assumption — a coherent object taken for granted to build other analysis on — and 21
assert it as a stylized fact. Fourteen frame the form as filling an omission, a call for a new label.
Only five code as critique. A subfield that produces eighteen construct labels and mounts five critiques
of them adds names far faster than it examines them. The scarcity of critique is the mechanism by which
proliferation persists: little in the literature's own output pushes back on the supply of new terms.

## Discussion

The three measurements compose a coherent account of how a subfield proliferates constructs. It
generates many labels (H1). It defines most of them by a positive mechanism, which makes each look like
a self-standing discovery rather than a variation on "not a hierarchy" (H2). And apart from one hub, it
does not cite across the labels, so the redundancy stays invisible from inside any single one (H3). The
positive-mechanism finding is the connective tissue, and it is the study's least expected result. A
field that defined every alternative form negatively would at least share a common frame — the
market-hierarchy contrast — that invites comparison. Defining each form by its own mechanism removes
even that shared reference point, and the citation graph shows the consequence: camps that share almost
no vocabulary and cite almost none of the same work. The jangle fallacy here is not sloppiness. It is
the byproduct of each camp doing careful mechanism-level work in isolation from the others, which maps
onto the novelty fallacy Cornelissen et al. (2026) describe — a mechanism stated in a camp's private
vocabulary reads as a new construct rather than a restatement of a shared one.

**A method for proliferation without scales.** The study's methodological claim is that proliferation in
a construct family with no shared measure can still be measured, and measured in a way that recovers what
the psychometric method recovers plus something it cannot. Discriminant-validity studies establish that
two constructs' *measures* correlate too highly to be distinct (Shaffer et al., 2016); they are silent
about whether the two literatures ever cite each other, because measures do not carry that information.
The assembly-spanning count carries exactly that: it shows that of thousands of papers citing this
construct family, all but a handful read one camp and stop. Redundancy and isolation are different
pathologies — two constructs can be redundant yet well-integrated, or distinct yet siloed — and the
coded-corpus-plus-citation-graph design separates them where a discriminant-validity study cannot. For
construct families built on scales, the two methods are complementary; for families built on labels
alone, the graph-based method is the only one available.

**The hub, and what it shows is possible.** Meta-organization's position deserves attention. It is the
one construct that links the others, sitting between platform, ecosystem, and the heterodox tail.
Kretschmer, Leiponen, Schilling, and Vasudeva (2022) conceptualize platform ecosystems as
meta-organizations; Gawer (2014) reads technological platforms as evolving meta-organizations; Chen,
Tong, Tang, and Han (2022) treat the digital platform as an organizational form governed like a
meta-organization. The construct works as a partial lingua franca, and its centrality is a caution as
much as a comfort: it shows that integration across these labels is possible and has begun, which makes
the isolation of the periphery a choice the field could reverse rather than a fact about the phenomena.

**Implications for construct discipline.** The reversal on H2 revises how the literature should be
described to newcomers. The textbook gloss — "new forms are neither market nor hierarchy" — captures a
minority of the corpus and misreads the rest. Most sources are past the contrast and into mechanism.
That is progress at the level of the individual construct and, at the same time, the engine of
proliferation across constructs. The discipline the finding implies is not "stop specifying mechanisms";
it is "specify the mechanism *and* locate it against the mechanisms the neighboring camps have already
specified." Bowling et al.'s (2026) moratorium is one response to proliferation; the milder and more
constructive one the graph suggests is a citation obligation — a new form earns its label by the
cross-camp comparison it makes, not by the mechanism it names in isolation.

That obligation binds the program that produced this review. A research agenda that would add another
construct to this space — the present lab's algorithmacy among them — is held to the standard the
measurement sets: coining a name adds an eighteenth to a crowded list, while stating a mechanism precise
enough to distinguish the form from its incumbents, and citing across the camps those incumbents ignore,
earns the addition. The same discipline applies to any measure that claims to grade a form's
irreducibility across these camps; its worth is the cross-label comparison it makes possible, which the
citation graph shows the field has not yet made for itself.

## Limitations

The coders are language-model agents applying a fixed codebook, not trained human raters. Agreement
among the agent passes is high and near-perfect on the label variable, but it certifies consistency, not
correctness: the same codebook read by human experts could shift the boundary cases, and model coders
may share priors that a human panel would not. The precondition for submission is therefore a
human-validation study — a stratified subsample of the corpus coded independently by at least two human
raters on the same codebook, with human-versus-model agreement reported alongside the model κ. The
headline count is the quantity most robust to this check, because label assignment is the least
interpretive of the four variables (κ = 0.966); differentia_mode, which carries the H2 result, is the
variable most in need of it.

Three further bounds hold. The corpus is a designed sample from one semantic-search connector and from
English-language indexing; a different seed set would change the label mix, though the fact of
proliferation is robust to reasonable changes in the search. Semantic Scholar elides outbound references
for many publishers, so the seed-to-seed matrix undercounts links and the two partial-organization seeds
matched none, making that camp's apparent isolation partly a coverage artifact; the assembly-spanning
count, drawn from the full downstream citer set, does not share this bias and carries the H3 conclusion.
Finally, the label count depends on the codebook's closed set and on a judgment about the `other` tail: a
coarser codebook would report fewer camps and a finer one more. The direction of the result does not
depend on that choice, but the exact figure of eighteen does, which is why the count is reported at
several thresholds rather than as a single number.
