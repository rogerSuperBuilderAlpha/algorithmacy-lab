# Review — network / brokerage theory

**Verdict:** minor revisions
**Target:** `chapter_v3.md` (post-followup; panel 3)

## Step 0 — register

The chapter is working in its chosen register: first person, film first, theory carried through examples,
and concessions folded into the motion of the essay. I do not count that register against it. My review
asks whether §2 now gives the setting/selector distinction a network-theory footing that will survive a
reader who knows Simmel, Burt, Gould and Fernandez, Feld, Stovel and Shaw, Rivera, and Small.

## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and structure

### 1. The Feld repair fixed the internal contradiction but introduced a source problem

Current text:

> A focus, in his term, is an entity around which joint activities are organised, so that ties form among
> the people it gathers as a side effect.

and later:

> Feld's foci are not people.

**Verified.** I retrieved accessible text for Feld in this session. The article defines a focus as "a
social, psychological, legal, or physical entity around which joint activities are organized," with
examples including "workplaces, voluntary organizations, hangouts, families, etc." An Annual Review page
on space and tie formation quotes Feld 1981 at p. 1018: foci "may be many different things, including
persons, places, social positions, activities, and groups."

So the old "person, place or activity" contradiction is gone, but the new categorical sentence is too
strong. Feld's foci can include persons. The distinction the chapter needs is narrower: Feldian foci
organize joint activity; Rosenfeld, Thomas and Hausen's figures measure human intermediaries. Friends and
family are not wrong because a person can never be a focus. They are wrong, if they are wrong, because
the paper slides from an activity-organizing focus to an interpersonal mediator and then measures only
the mediator.

Paste-ready fix:

> Their own opening paragraph calls friends and family an intermediation in one sentence and "the
> essential network foci" in the next, citing Feld.[^24] Feld's foci are entities around which joint
> activity is organised; they can include people only in that role. Rosenfeld, Thomas and Hausen's
> figures measure human intermediaries, not the foci that organise repeated activity, so the arrival of a
> non-human intermediary remains untouched.

This keeps the *PNAS* exhibit, but makes it harder to swat away with Feld p. 1018.

### 2. The Rivera narrowing now basically holds, but "nobody" still carries more than the evidence can

Current text:

> Sociologists have reviewed the setting and the broker together before: Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi sort
> the causes of tie formation into assortative, relational, and proximity mechanisms, with Feld and Burt in
> the same reference list.[^20] What they sort by is what makes a tie form. What nobody has asked of both
> is the question these three put to them — what does the third know about the two parties it brings
> together?

**Verified.** I retrieved Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi's Annual Review page and an accessible PDF text. The
abstract says the review sorts dyad change into assortative mechanisms, relational mechanisms tied to
existing relationships and network positions, and proximity mechanisms tied to the social organization of
interaction. The body treats relational mechanisms under Simmelian triads, closure, intermediaries,
centrality, and brokerage, then turns to proximity and social foci. Feld, Burt, and Gould/Fernandez all
belong on that terrain.

The current chapter has made the right repair: it names Rivera et al. as prior art and shifts the claim
from "nobody set them side by side" to "nobody held them to this question." That is the surviving claim.
The only remaining risk is the bare universal "What nobody has asked." The chapter has searched a small
set of canonical statements, not the whole field. A network referee will accept a located gap more readily
than a universal absence claim.

Paste-ready fix:

> What I have not found is a version that asks the same question of both: what does the third know about
> the two parties it brings together?

If the author wants the stronger form, add a note that states the search boundary. The present note 20a
does this for three documents; it does not prove the field-wide negative.

### 3. The three-question apparatus is improved, but the Uber example still overstates what §5 later
proves

Current text:

> Does it act on everyone at once, or on two particular people? Could the encounter have happened without
> it? Does it know who these particular people are, and change what it does accordingly? Austin acts on
> the whole street, is one route among many, and reads types at most. The camera picks one person out of
> the crowd, is the only way that person reaches you, and knows nothing about anybody. Uber picks the
> pair, is the only route to the match, and reads both parties.

and §5:

> No published engineering account describes ride-hail dispatch as reading rider or driver identity in
> order to choose the pair; Uber's and Lyft's own descriptions run on collective wait time and marketplace
> value. So the evidence for reading sits on the pricing side, which is not the same as the pricing power
> over again.

**Verified as an internal contradiction.** No external source is needed for the conflict. §2 runs the
third question on Uber as if dispatch reads both parties to make the match. §5 says the evidence does not
show that. The repair is easy because the chapter's better claim is already in §5: the selector can read
the matched parties in order to price or remove them, even if the dispatch rule itself remains described
in aggregate terms.

Paste-ready fix:

> Uber picks the pair, is the only route to the match, and then prices the matched parties as identifiable
> parties.

If the author wants to keep "reads both parties," add "on price, at least" in §2. That small hedge will
make the later restraint look deliberate.

### 4. The Simmel limit is now strong enough

The current divide-et-impera paragraph is the repair the prior panel asked for:

> These are diagnostics and not a decision procedure, and they presuppose the two definitions above.
> They sort thirds that operate where people are already reachable. A third that works to destroy
> reachability never enters the field they sort...

That is exactly the right shape. It keeps the three questions from pretending to be a definition, and it
does not turn harm into the sorting criterion. The paragraph also protects §5: a selector's extractive
power is still a second question, not the thing that makes it a selector.

One small wording issue remains:

> On the first page of that chapter he names a fourth kind and puts it aside.

Simmel names the shared-relation case before the chapter has finished itemizing the other three forms. The
chapter's count is useful for the reader, but "fourth kind" can sound like Simmel's taxonomy rather than
the author's reconstruction.

Paste-ready fix:

> On the first page of that chapter he names another kind and puts it aside.

### 5. The broker paragraph is now sound, with one optional tightening

The Burt/Obstfeld problem has been fixed:

> Ronald Burt showed that two people who would each gain from what the other knows, and who are not
> connected, are worth money to whoever stands between them. David Obstfeld described a friendlier
> version, the third who introduces the two instead of playing them off.

**Verified.** Burt's structural-hole account rests on information and control benefits for the tertius
gaudens; the accessible Burt text I retrieved describes the broker as strategically moving information
between contacts and negotiating favorable terms. Stovel and Shaw's brokerage review also supports the
chapter's direction: brokerage lets disconnected or isolated individuals or groups interact, but it is
built from informal, personal relationships and micro-level relations.

The paragraph also now names the correct constant:

> What it does not argue about is that they are two identified parties...

That is the hinge the chapter needs. I would only soften this sentence:

> ...every measure that came out of it is a property of a point in a network — which a setting does not
> have.

"Every measure" is still a universal over a large methods literature. The sentence is true for
Gould/Fernandez-style brokerage and for the tradition the chapter cites. It does not need the universal.

Paste-ready fix:

> ...the measures in this line are properties of a point in a network — which a setting does not have.

### 6. Feld supplies less of "setting" than the paragraph first implies, and the current text mostly
knows it

The best sentence in the Feld paragraph is the one that follows the Feld sentence:

> The other half is the thing Lyn Lofland described, the code by which strangers in a city read each
> other's appearance and location and know who may be approached where.

That line saves the genealogy. Feld gives the activity-organizing focus. Lofland gives the code of
stranger approach. The chapter then combines them and calls the result a setting. That is clean.

The risk is in the transition:

> There is another way to hold the position, and Scott Feld named half of it in 1981.

This is much better than the earlier version. It still makes Feld the entry point for a concept whose
urban-conventional half is doing just as much work. Consider making the joint parentage explicit in the
definition itself.

Paste-ready fix:

> Feld supplies the reach; Lofland supplies the code. Put the two together and call the result a setting.

This is shorter than the current bridge and gives both sources their proper jobs.

## Part 2 — The four bans, and AI-slop audit

**Ban 1: metaphor doing argumentative work.** Clean in §2. "A setting has no node" is literal enough in
network prose, and the sentence explains the point before the reader has to infer it.

**Ban 2: throat-clearing.** Mostly clean. The closest case is:

> What I am claiming is narrower than it first looks, and it has prior art I should name.

The sentence does real work because it marks the post-Rivera narrowing. Keep the concession if the author
keeps the paragraph. If the paragraph is revised, it can become the first sentence of the claim itself:

> The claim is narrow, because the prior art is real.

**Ban 3: meta-commentary / forward-backward reference.** Clean in §2. The paragraph on the questions
names what the questions can and cannot do, but that is not a roadmap. It is a scope condition.

**Ban 4: unexplained jargon.** One small hit remains:

> The formal apparatus is built on an ordered triple of named actors, and every measure that came out of
> it is a property of a point in a network — which a setting does not have.

"Ordered triple" and "point in a network" are ordinary to this lens, but a film reader may need a half
beat. This can be fixed without adding a sentence:

> The formal apparatus is built on an ordered triple of named actors — broker, first party, second party
> — and the measures in this line are properties of a point in a network, which a setting does not have.

**AI-slop audit.** §2 has a few necessary negatives: "not a decision procedure," "do not do is demote,"
"not a failure." They are doing boundary work, not decorative antithesis. The phrase to watch is "what
nobody has asked," because it has the grand-negative shape and a field-wide burden. The Rivera repair
above solves the style problem and the evidence problem at the same time.
