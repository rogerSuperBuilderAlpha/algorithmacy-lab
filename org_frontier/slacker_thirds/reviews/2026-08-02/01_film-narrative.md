# Part 1 — Theoretical rigor and structure

## 1.1 The narrator debate is represented accurately, with one gap and one distortion

The map is right, and it is right in a way the previous draft was not. Bordwell's objection is correctly
enlarged from "no little storyteller" to "no sender at all." Chatman is correctly presented as arriving
with a narrator *already* depersonalised, which relocates the fight from personification to agency.
Slugan is correctly used to say that film studies is not two camps of equal size. The sentence

> So the argument is not about whether a camera has intentions. It is about whether an impersonal system
> counts as an agent.

is the cleanest one-sentence statement of the crux I have read in a non-specialist chapter. Keep it.

**The distortion.** Bordwell's function list arrives under this frame:

> What he grants in the same account is everything I need. Narration, on his own description, suppresses
> information, restricts what we know, generates curiosity, sets a tone.

In Bordwell that list is not a grant to your side. It is the *premise of his argument against you*: "No
trait we could assign to an implied author of a film could not more simply be ascribed to the narration
itself: it sometimes suppresses information, it often restricts our knowledge, it generates curiosity,
it creates a tone, and so on. To give every film a narrator or implied author is to indulge in an
anthropomorphic fiction." He lists the functions in order to show that nothing needs to be posited
behind them. Presenting the list as a concession, unmarked, is the one move in this section a Bordwell
reader will call unfair. Two further small things: the paraphrase drops his frequency hedges
("sometimes," "often"), which makes the narration sound more systematic than he wrote it; and "on the
ground that film narration 'presupposes a perceiver, but not any sender'" makes his *conclusion* into
its own ground — the ground is the preceding sentence, that narration is the organisation of a set of
cues.

And "everything I need" is refuted two paragraphs later by the chapter itself. If Bordwell granted
everything, there would be no reason to reach for the philosophers' fallback. See 1.2.

**The gap.** Poulaki relays a second Bordwell sentence on *Slacker* from the same page — "The story
action doesn't bring the characters face-to-face, and the narration doesn't bare unexpected connections
among them." That sentence *is* the chapter's story-world claim, in Bordwell's own words, and it is
sitting unused in your research file (`research/deep/2026-08-01_s4.md`, Finding 6). It would strengthen
the two-graphs move more than anything else available.

## 1.2 The minority admission is honest but under-specified, and it costs more than the chapter pays

> So I am siding with a minority inside film studies and taking the fallback the philosophers offer,
> that a story "is actually told by the author in his or her creation of the work." When I write "the
> camera," I mean whatever makes those choices.

Admirable, and I want to praise it before pressing it. But a reader cannot tell *which* minority. Three
positions are live in the paragraph and they point in opposite directions:

1. **Chatman's** — an impersonal composite of devices *is* a narrator. Per Slugan this predominates in
   film studies, so a chapter taking it is with the majority, not a minority.
2. **Bordwell's** — no sender at any level. Minority in film studies, and it *denies you a selector
   outright*.
3. **The philosophers' (Currie, Kania, Gaut, as Thomson-Jones reports them)** — no in-film narrator; the
   real author does the telling. Minority in film studies; not a minority among philosophers, which is
   where you took it from.

The quoted fallback is (3). So the sentence is defensible. But (3) has a price the chapter never puts on
the table: **the author is a person.** If the selecting in *Slacker* is Richard Linklater's selecting,
then §7's whole contrast — every link in the chain "was a person who could be telephoned," refusal was
"attributable," the Orion vice-president "said no, and Linklater knew whom to ask" — reaches back and
swallows the camera. The camera stops being an impersonal third and becomes a person's decision, frozen.

You have the answer already and it is better than the dodge. The camera's choosing is *finished, public,
and identical for every viewer*; Uber's is *live, private, and computed per party*. §7 nearly says this
about the landing page ("without knowing who you are"). §2's three questions get at the knowledge axis
but not the fixed/live axis. Naming it in §4 costs two sentences and it repairs the chapter's most
exposed joint. Text supplied in Part 3.

Relatedly: "whatever makes those choices" is a deliberate agnosticism, and an agnostic position is not a
minority position. As printed, the claim of minority allegiance and the refusal to specify sit awkwardly
in adjacent sentences.

## 1.3 The two-graphs move is sound; the ordering claim around it is not

The move itself — story-world chain versus presentation-order walk — is correct, useful, and correctly
owned. Note 43 does exactly what it should:

> Berg's own definition has the characters coming into contact with one another, and makes Linklater the
> agent of the linking; the distinction between story-world chain and presentation-order walk is mine.

**Berg is answerable for only the half attributed to him.** That is now right. Two refinements.

First, a free point your research pass found and the draft declined to take: Berg's general definition of
the daisy chain has "each character's story presented fully, in turn," which *Slacker*'s two-minute
vignettes are not. He files the film under a definition his own example fails. One clause, and it makes
your case: the taxonomy strains because the film's shape isn't where the taxonomy looks.

Second, and this is a real problem:

> No taxonomy in this literature sorts films by how scenes connect in presentation order, and the graph
> tradition dropped order deliberately

Berg's twelve categories include a whole family — Backwards, Repeated Action, Repeated Event, Hub and
Spoke, Jumbled — that sorts films precisely by presentation order against story chronology. Any reader
who has Berg open (and note 43 sends them there) will read your sentence as plainly false. Your own
research pass worked out the correct narrowing and the draft did not absorb it: Berg's ordering axis is
*temporal scrambling*, and *Slacker* runs forward through a single day, so his ordering categories were
never available to it. Say that. It is more interesting than the absence claim and it is true.

## 1.4 The Poulaki rebuttal engages half her position

You get three things right that the previous draft got wrong: she does not explain coherence without a
coordinating agent; her camera's agency is *descriptive*; the diegetic-encounter rebuttal ("nobody in
*Slacker* meets twice") does not touch her, because her interactions are informational. Correctly built.

But the paragraph does not engage the position that actually opposes you. Poulaki's claim is
**self-organisation**: a film coheres because its parts form a system, and a system organises itself,
with nothing standing outside to arrange it. Your rebuttal is:

> Her own floor is the answer: "A catalogue is not a system but a list of elements; a system needs
> relations between the elements in order to form itself." The relations here are the order of the
> visits, and somebody chose it.

The first half wins — *Slacker* has relations, so it clears her floor. The second half is asserted, not
argued, and it is the half she would contest. "Somebody chose it" is exactly what self-organisation
denies. The argument you need is one sentence long and §4 has already supplied its materials: nothing
inside the film generates the sequence. No character wants the next scene. No object travels. No cause
runs across a cut ("No character introduces another"). A self-organising system produces its own order
out of relations among its parts; here the sequence *is* the only relation, so it cannot have come from
the parts.

Two smaller notes on the same paragraph. **The billing does not match the treatment.** "The strongest
account against me" is followed by three clauses of dismantling, then two harvests in the chapter's
favour, and she ends the paragraph an ally. Either state her objection at the top so the harvest lands as
a reversal, or stop calling her the strongest opponent. And **her only engagement with *Slacker* is a
relay of Bordwell** — which is a lovely fact for your novelty claim and goes unused: the leading
complex-systems account of network films touches this film once, to quote someone who could not classify
it.

## 1.5 Two internal contradictions

**(a) "Always."**

> The transfer is always the film following one body until another crosses it.

§3 says otherwise, on your own evidence: Gaughen's three-mile jump, "the Old Anarchist's house to
Thirty-fifth and Mopac — played across a cut as though it were a walk." That instance is load-bearing in
§3 and it falsifies the "always" in §4. Cheap fix in Part 3.

**(b) "The only thing left."**

> Strip out every durable tie between characters, which here is easy because there are none, and the only
> thing left holding scene to scene is the film's decision about whom to follow next.

Two sentences later the chapter concedes the opposite: "That decision is constrained. … The camera can
only leave with someone the city has already put within reach of the lens." So what holds scene to scene
is a conjunction — the city's co-presence *and* the camera's choice among the co-present. That
conjunction is the chapter's whole thesis; the "only thing left" sentence throws away half of it for a
rhetorical beat. I would recast rather than delete: the exclusivity you want is that nothing in the
*story world* selects, not that nothing else contributes.

## 1.6 §6's evidential base has an unaddressed objection, and it is about this film

You concede the audio complication honestly, and the narrowing is right: what is learned is "bridging
shots *without an audio scaffold*." But look at what that concession does to the section's application.
*Slacker* is a film of nearly continuous talk and continuous ambient street sound. Its handoffs are the
most heavily audio-scaffolded transitions imaginable. A hostile reader with Ildirar, Levin, Schwan and
Smith open says: your own cited study shows first-time viewers manage sound-bridged cuts, so the silent
studies do not license your claim about *this* film's audience.

The answer exists and you should print it. What defeats a first-time viewer in *Slacker* is not the cut;
it is the abandonment. No audio scaffold repairs a film that drops its protagonist and never returns.
That is a distinction between *continuity* and *commitment*, and it is more precise than anything the
Kuleshov literature can give you.

## 1.7 Structure

§4's best sentence — "the camera coordinates *me*, serially, with one of them after another" — is
followed by the Poulaki paragraph and buried. Consider moving Poulaki to just after the Bordwell/Slugan
material (she belongs with the rival accounts) so §4 closes on its own claim. §6's ordering is sound;
Münsterberg → Benjamin → Hansen → measurement → craft → limit → Heiland is a good shape and I would not
touch it.

# Part 2 — The four bans, plus AI-slop audit

**Ban 1 (no metaphor doing argumentative work) — one clear violation.**

> His word is string, and the important property of a string is that it has an order.

This derives the chapter's ordered-walk claim from Bordwell's figure of speech, and it runs the figure
backwards. Bordwell writes "a wiggly, knotted string" to say the film *isn't* a network — his string is a
figure for tangle and disorder. You recruit it for orderedness. A specialist will see the seam. The
literal claim (the presentation has a sequence and somebody fixed it) does not need Bordwell's noun to
stand up.

**Ban 2 (no throat-clearing) — two.**

> Whether a camera can be said to choose anything is a real question and film scholars have fought over
> it.

The first clause announces that a question is a question.

> What connects one scene to the next is almost nothing, and the exactness matters.

"the exactness matters" announces a virtue the next three sentences then demonstrate. This is also the
repo style file's banned "self-narrating rigor."

**Ban 3 (no meta-commentary or forward reference) — one.**

> That decision is constrained, and the constraint matters later.

"matters later" is a forward reference to §5. The next sentence already states the constraint.

**Ban 4 (no unexplained jargon) — three, all in §6.**

- "Adding **diegetic sound** to the clips" — never defined.
- "cuts violating the **180-degree rule**" — never defined.
- "nobody showed **the effect** at all" — the Kuleshov effect is described by example but never named or
  defined, so "the effect" has no antecedent for a reader outside film studies. Given the chapter's
  audience includes organisational and platform researchers, this one actually bites.

Minor: "not a single jump cut" is glossed only by negation ("he does not mean there are no cuts"), which
tells a reader what the phrase doesn't mean and not what it does.

**AI-slop audit.** Clean. I ran the repo's own counts over §§4 and 6. Antithesis constructions are well
under the threshold and every one I found earns its contrast (`, not his`; `describes rather than
chooses`; `Proposed or not`). No filler transitions. No empty closers — "and reading is the part with a
century of teaching behind it" is a real claim, not a bow. Paragraph openings vary properly: scene
imperatives ("Watch what happens after the taxi"), long-sentence openings ("The best account of what
experienced viewers have…"), flat declaratives. No `has been / have been` agentless passives in either
section. The prose does not read as generated, and I would say so unprompted.

One tic worth naming because it recurs three times in §4 and nowhere else: the "X, and it is Y than it
looks" shape — "and it is stranger than it looks," "goes further than the one usually attributed to him,"
"is narrower than the claim usually made." Each is fine alone; three in one section starts to sound like
a mannerism of reveal.

### 2. §6 — Münsterberg's quoted sentence is about the theatre, not the photoplay

> Hugo Münsterberg answered in 1916, in the first book of film psychology: "our attention is constantly
> drawn in accordance with the intentions of the producers."

I retrieved *The Photoplay* (Project Gutenberg #15383, ch. IV, "Attention") and read the paragraph. It
runs: "To be sure, we may approach a **theater performance** with a voluntary purpose of our own… we may
be interested in a particular actor and may watch him with our **opera glass**… But such voluntary

producers." The next sentence: "Surely **the theater** has no lack of means to draw this involuntary
attention to any important point."

The doctrine you attribute to him he does hold about film, and more strongly. But the sentence you print
is about the stage, and you print it as the first book of film psychology's answer about film.

### 3. §6 — and Münsterberg is not a training theorist; he is the best rival §6 could have

His account of why film directs attention is that the photoplay reproduces the mind's own acts, which
means *no skill has to be acquired*. Verbatim, same chapter: "The close-up has objectified in our world

the power of any theater stage." And: "It is as if that outer world were woven into our mind and were
shaped not through its own laws but by the acts of our attention."

That is a nativist explanation, and it is contradicted by the study you cite four paragraphs later.
Ildirar and Ewing's first-time viewers "perceived them to be wholly separate from each other." If

gives §6 a hundred-year-old thesis that the measurements overturn, instead of a decorative epigraph.

# Verdict

**Major revisions.** Not because the argument is in trouble — it is not — but because three of this
lens's four source problems are the same species the last verification pass was run to eliminate, and one
of them is a quotation the project cannot show its work on. Fix the sixteen items above and this is a
strong chapter that a film-studies referee will take seriously.

# Biggest genuine strength

The chapter earns a distinction that film narrative theory has never had a reason to draw, and it draws
it on *Slacker* rather than importing it. The two-graphs move — a bare chain among the characters, an
ordered walk in the presentation — is a real contribution, and the chapter is scrupulous about which half
Berg owns and which half is its own. What makes it more than a taxonomy note is the sentence that turns
the whole apparatus:

> The camera does not coordinate the characters. The moon-landing man and his captive coordinate each
> other; the camera coordinates *me*, serially, with one of them after another.

That relocates the coordination from the story world to the viewer, which is what lets a 1991 art film
sit in the same argument as a dispatch algorithm without either one being a metaphor for the other. I
have not seen it done, and the absence searches in `2026-08-01_s4.md` are consistent with nobody having
done it.

# The one thing only the author can supply

Whether *Slacker*'s handoffs are always physical proximity, and how often they are not. Everything in
§4's relay claim rests on it — the "always" sentence, the "no character introduces another" sentence, the
answer to Poulaki about nothing generating the sequence from inside — and the published record cannot
settle it. Gaughen surveyed outdoor locations only, non-exhaustively, and found one three-mile jump.
Nobody has logged the transitions. Watching the disc once with a notepad and classifying all ~36 handoffs
(walks into frame / crosses the frame / passes an object / cut with no shared body) would give this
chapter a piece of primary evidence no reviewer could argue with, and would let §4 say "thirty of
thirty-six" instead of "always." That is an afternoon, and it is the highest-value afternoon available
to this draft.
