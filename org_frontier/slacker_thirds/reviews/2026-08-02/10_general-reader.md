# General-reader review — `chapter_v3.md`

**Verdict: minor revisions.** I read it straight through without wanting to stop, which is not true of
most film writing I've picked up. Everything below is local. Nothing needs new research, and the four
things I'd call serious are each one to three sentences of work.

## Step 0 — register

The first person is the best thing about this chapter's voice and I have no notes against it; the
"I" is load-bearing, the scenes come before the theses, and the concessions land in stride rather than
in a defensive crouch.
Every rewrite below is at least as plain and at least as active as what it replaces — where I couldn't

## Part 1 — rigor and structure, as a reader experiences them

### Is the setting/selector distinction clear on first reading?

Yes, at the definition. The bolded pair in §2 is the clearest writing in the chapter:

> **A setting puts people within reach of one another and supplies the conventions that let one
> approach another. It arranges no particular meeting.** … **a selector chooses: which two parties come
> together, or which one an audience meets next.**

I understood that instantly and never lost it. What I *did* lose, about ninety seconds later, is how to
score the three questions. Here is the passage:

> Three questions tell them apart. Does it act on everyone at once, or on two particular people? Could
> the encounter have happened without it? Does it know who these particular people are, and change what
> it does accordingly? Austin acts on the whole street, is one route among many, and reads types at
> most. The camera picks one person out of the crowd, is the only way that person reaches you, and knows
> nothing about anybody. Uber picks the pair, is the only route to the match, and reads both parties in
> detail.

I stopped and reread twice, and I still had to work it out on paper. The problem is arithmetic. Austin
answers "setting" three times out of three. Uber answers "selector" three times out of three. But the
camera — the chapter's own paradigm selector, the thing the whole film argument turns on — answers
*selector, selector, setting*. It "knows nothing about anybody," which is the setting answer to question
three. And then two paragraphs later Simmel's divide-and-rule third scores "as a setting" on two of
three and the chapter says so out loud. So the reader now knows the test can split, has seen it split
twice, and has never been told what to do when it splits. Is it best of three? Is one question decisive?

I think the chapter's actual position is that questions one and two decide the *kind* and question three
grades the *power* — §4's "the first kind in its oldest form: it arranges what reaches you and knows
nothing whatever about you" and the whole of §5 only make sense on that reading. But that rule is never
stated, and it is the load-bearing rule of the chapter's central apparatus. One sentence fixes it. This
is my top structural finding.

Second: the parallel paragraph would read on the first pass, not the second, if the three labels the
*next* paragraph supplies — "reach," "counterfactual," "knowledge" — were attached to the questions
themselves. Right now the reader has to build that mapping unaided and is then handed the finished
mapping one paragraph too late. Question two is the worst offender: "Could the encounter have happened
without it?" is answered by "is one route among many," which inverts the polarity, and I had to run it
twice to confirm that "one route among many" means "yes, it could have."

### Does the Uber material feel bolted on?

In the argument, no. §2 puts Uber in the three-question paragraph as the third worked example, so by the
time §5 arrives I've been told twice that it's coming and why. The distinction genuinely *needs* a
third case that scores selector on all three questions, and Uber is the obvious one. I never once
thought "this is a different book."

In the texture, a bit. §5 runs about 950 words with no film in it at all until its last paragraph. The
sentence that rescues it — "A camera can pass you over. It cannot price your passage and it cannot take
you off the street." — is excellent and arrives on page eight of the section. That is the one place in
the chapter where a general reader might set it down and not come back, and the fix costs zero words:
move that pair of sentences to the front of §5. Then the film is present in the section's first line,
the three powers arrive as an answer to a question the reader already has, and the closing paragraph
still works with a two-word patch.

The other thing that made §5 heavier than it needs to be is a pile-up of scruples. Four of them, in
sequence:

- "All I can say is that surge has not been shown to know who the two parties are…"
- "I will assert the differential pay and leave the personalisation where she leaves it."
- "The best evidence for this power is not where a reader would expect it…"
- "That is a preliminary posture rather than a final judgment."

Individually each is admirable and I'd keep three of them. Cumulatively they made me feel I was reading
an argument with an invisible referee rather than with me. The third is the one to move: it announces
where the evidence is before giving it, and it's also the closest thing in the chapter to throat-clearing
(see Part 2).

### Where I got bored

Five places, in descending order:

1. **§7, the Tzioumakis paragraph.** "The third tier's fall from 10.22 to 2.11 percent of the market"
   — I don't know what a tier is, I'm never told, and the paragraph exists to refute a story the chapter
   never told me. The cut ledger already ranks this as cut candidate #1 at ~85 words. As the reader it
   is aimed past, I endorse the cut. Move the refutation to a note and take the 85 words.
2. **§4, Bordwell → Chatman → Slugan.** Three scholars adjudicating whether a camera can choose. I
   understand why it's here. But the sentence that actually settles it for me — "When I write 'the
   camera,' I mean whatever makes those choices" — is buried at the end of a paragraph reporting who
   holds which position in a field I don't belong to. Lead with it.
3. **§4, Poulaki.** Billed as "the strongest account against me," and then it turns out her sentence is
   about a different film. A reader who isn't a film scholar wonders why the strongest opponent isn't
   about *Slacker*. The paragraph does eventually earn itself on the catalogue/system quotation, which
   is good, but it takes a while.
4. **§2, Burt → Obstfeld → the brokerage definition → the formal apparatus.** Four moves establishing
   one point: everyone in this literature assumes the third brokers a specific pair. Tight already;
   just noting that it's the driest stretch in the chapter's best section.
5. **§5's middle.** Covered above.

### Where I got lost

Three places, all fixable in a sentence each.

**(a) The Austin vacancy paragraph contradicts itself in front of me.**

> Median contract rent on census day 1990 was $346 and the rental vacancy rate was 11.6 percent. Cheap
> space buys unstructured time, and unstructured time is what this film is about. Dallas that year sat
> at 15.3 percent vacancy and Houston at 15.8, so Austin was the tighter market of the three; what it
> had was a great deal of room and 50,245 students at the university in the middle of it.

I read "cheap space buys unstructured time," believed it, and was then told Austin was the *tightest*
of the three markets — and then, in the same sentence, that "what it had was a great deal of room." Room
or tight? I had to go back to the numbers to work out that the honest position is: Austin was cheap in
absolute terms and slack by any normal standard, but not the slackest in Texas. That's a fine position.
The order of presentation just buries it. Claim, evidence, counter-evidence, re-assertion, in eighty
words, reads as a wobble rather than as scruple.

**(b) "She said no."**

§7 ends beautifully — "That last sentence is the modern counterpart of the Orion vice-president who
said no. She said no, and Linklater knew whom to ask." — except that *she* has no antecedent. Earlier
the chapter wrote "a named vice-president at Orion," which withholds the name. In a paragraph whose
entire thesis is that these were people you could telephone, withholding the name is self-defeating,
and it leaves the closing pronoun hanging. Your own research file has her: Susan Blodgett, vice-president
of Orion's home video division. Print it. This is the single highest-value one-word change in the
chapter: it converts an abstraction ("a named vice-president") into the exact thing the section claims
has been lost.

**(c) The oblique-strategies card.**

> *Slacker*'s politics were mistaken for apathy, and the film headed the charge off itself on one of the
> oblique-strategies cards a passerby hands out in it.

I have seen the film and I still stopped here. The sentence asserts that the film answered the charge on
a card, and then never says what the card said. Two things go missing at once: what "oblique strategies"
are (Brian Eno's deck of prompts — a reader who doesn't know Eno gets nothing), and the content of the
answer, which is the whole point of the sentence. I understand from `research/deep/2026-08-01_s4.md`
that the project has a standing rule against printing any line of *Slacker* dialogue in quotation marks
without the disc, because the published record splits on the wording. That rule is right. But it forbids
*quoting*, not *paraphrasing*, and paraphrase costs nothing here. As it stands the chapter's last
substantive gesture is a promissory note it doesn't cash.

### Two suspicions

**The Poulaki "setting" is a pun the chapter cashes as evidence.** "What links *Gomorrah*, on her
reading, is 'primarily the setting … and the character of Maria' — a setting and a selector, side by
side, inside her own analysis." Her "setting" is the ordinary film-critical word for the place a film is
set. The chapter's "setting" is a technical term inherited from Feld. Reading her sentence as an
independent corroboration of the chapter's distinction looked, for a second, like a word trick, and a
hostile reader will say so. The defence is available and easy — Scampia genuinely does the Feldian work
in her account — but it has to be on the page.

**The Uber median/mean sentence sounds statistically backwards.** "the company's median cut rose from
25 to 29 percent while the mean held at 25, the increase concentrated on the highest-fare trips." If the
increase falls on the highest fares, my instinct says the *mean* should be the one that moves. I checked
`research/deep/2026-08-01_s5.md`, which quotes Binns et al. §5.1 verbatim, and the chapter is faithful
to the source — the paper says both things. But the chapter hands the reader a paradox and doesn't
resolve it, and your own research note already contains the resolution ("the median is doing all the
work"). One clause tells me what the combination *means*: the typical driver is losing more of every
fare while the average across all trips stays flat, which is exactly how the loss stays invisible.

### The best sentence, and the worst

**Best:** "Every link in the chain that carried *Slacker* to an audience was a person who could be
telephoned." It's the whole of §7 in fourteen words, it's concrete, it's got a verb doing real work, and
it sets up the Amazon page eight paragraphs later without announcing that it's doing so. Runner-up, and
it's close: "Nobody is owed a return."

**Worst:** "Schegloff also closes the escape route at the other end, though: in co-presence a member
cannot simply decline to answer, since the excusing inference is unavailable and cold-shouldering gets
read in instead." Three unexplained technical terms in twenty-eight words — "co-presence," "the excusing
inference," "cold-shouldering gets read in" — in a paragraph that is otherwise about a man on a sidewalk
who won't stop talking about the moon. I reread it, worked out that it means "on the phone you can
pretend you were out; on a sidewalk you can't," and resented the trip.

## Part 2 — the four bans, and the slop audit

### Ban 1 — no metaphor doing argumentative work. **Passes.**

I went looking and couldn't find a violation. "Daisy chain" and "wiggly, knotted string" are quoted from
sources and immediately cashed for their literal property ("the important property of a string is that
it has an order") — that is exactly the licensed use. "*Slacker* takes away the alibi" is followed by
two literal sentences saying what the alibi was. "Starved by a division applying a default rather than
killed by the bankruptcy" is figurative but the facts are all on the page around it.

The closest call is "Cheap space buys unstructured time," where "buys" *is* the claim and no literal
version accompanies it. I'd let it stand — the census numbers immediately before and the students
immediately after do the literal work — but it's the one to watch if a stricter reader objects.

### Ban 2 — no throat-clearing. **One violation, two borderline.**

- **Violation:** "The best evidence for this power is not where a reader would expect it: no published
  engineering account describes…" The first clause exists only to announce that the second is coming.
  Rewrite in Part 3.
- Borderline: "I want to draw that difference in the right place, though, because…" — announces a
  carefulness before performing it. Your own repo style file names this species ("self-narrating
  honesty and rigor"). It's mild and it's doing a real transition, so I'd keep it.
- Borderline: "so I want one instance rather than a pattern. One instance is enough." Same species, but
  here it's making a genuine evidential claim about what the Gaughen survey can support, and the
  follow-through is immediate. Keep.

### Ban 3 — no meta-commentary. **Passes, with one judgment call.**

No "this chapter argues," no "in what follows," no back-references. The cut ledger shows three of these
were caught and killed in the long draft, and none crept back in. "What I am claiming is narrow" is
about the claim's altitude rather than about the chapter's structure, and it earns its place.

The judgment call: §4's "and later the landing page of a streaming service" and "and later Uber" are
soft forward references to §5 and §7. They don't name sections and they're doing real taxonomic work
inside their own sentence, so I'd allow them. Flagging so the call is deliberate.

### Ban 4 — no unexplained jargon. **This is where the chapter fails, and it fails repeatedly.**

Twelve terms a general reader does not own, none of them glossed:

| Term | Where | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| "a top of sixteen screens" | §1, §7 | I read it as a typo for "a total of." It's the widest-release figure and it's load-bearing in §7. |
| "the excusing inference" / "cold-shouldering gets read in" | §3 | Worst sentence in the chapter. |
| "nonterminal" | §3 | Partly glossed by "offering no natural exit." Survivable. |
| "observationally equivalent effects" / "measured elasticity" | §3 | The scruple sentence in the agglomeration paragraph. I skipped it. |
| "assortative matching" | §3 | Total loss. I have no idea what runs negatively with what. |
| "*divide et impera*" | §2 | Recoverable from context, but the sentence around it is the hardest in §2. |
| "the second window" | §7 | Industry term for home video. |
| "third tier" / "middle tier" | §7 | Never defined; the paragraph is unreadable without them. |
| "oblique strategies" | §7 | Discussed above. |
| "Enclosure" (section heading) | §7 | A historical metaphor the section never connects to. Minor. |

Nine of the twelve are one-clause fixes. The count matters more than any single case: a chapter that has
formally adopted this ban is currently breaking it about twice per thousand words, and every instance is
in a *supporting* clause rather than in the argument, which means the jargon is buying nothing.

### AI-slop audit

Low. Genuinely low. The rhythm varies, paragraphs open differently from one another, the short sentences
land where they're earned rather than on a metronome, and there is no filler transition anywhere in
5,400 words. I'd have said a person wrote this.

Two patterns worth naming:

**The corrective ending.** I counted roughly twenty-five instances of `not X` / `rather than` / `is not
a` across the chapter — about 4.6 per thousand words, which is just inside your repo's threshold of
five. But they aren't distributed evenly. §5 and §7 have clusters where three consecutive paragraphs
each *end* on a correction: "The remedy was disclosure, not reinstatement." / "That is a preliminary
posture rather than a final judgment." / "So the contrast is not between a cut and a match. It is
between a cut and a regime that prices and removes." Each is good. Three in a row starts to feel like a
tic, and the reader begins to anticipate the shape of the last sentence before reading it. Converting
one per section to a flat positive statement would fix it entirely.

**Two agentless passives**, both in sentences the chapter cares about:
- "The setting was named in 1981 and has been studied since" — two in one clause, in the sentence
  stating the novelty claim.
- "surge has not been shown to know who the two parties are" — in the chapter's own hedge, where naming
  the missing agent (nobody) is stronger.

Everything else is active and agented. The Amazon paragraph in particular ("It reads… It removes… And it
declines to explain…") is the analytic triplet used exactly right: three members, three distinct pieces
of content, three quotations, no cadence-padding.

## Verdict

**Minor revisions.** Four serious findings — the unstated scoring rule, the vacancy paragraph's
self-contradiction, the dangling "she," and the uncashed card — plus a jargon problem that is real but
that twelve short clauses would clear. None of it requires a new source. If §5's opening moves and the
Tzioumakis paragraph goes, the chapter gets shorter and better in the same pass, which is a nice thing
to be able to say about a draft that's over its ceiling.

## Biggest genuine strength

Scenes arrive before theses, everywhere, without exception, and the theses are then stated in words a
person would use. §4 is the model: the taxi, then the woman in the road, then the son mid-arrest, then
"Two abandonments in the opening minutes — the director, then a tragedy — teach the rule the rest of the
film runs on. Nobody is owed a return." That's a hundred words of film followed by a nine-word claim,
and I believed the claim because I'd just watched it happen. Most academic writing I try to read does
this backwards. The second strength, related: the chapter tells me what it does *not* know — that nobody
has run the transfer study, that Dubal's personalisation claim is inference, that only Boom's abstract
was read — and every one of those admissions made me trust the rest more rather than less. That is hard
to do without sounding either defensive or smug, and the chapter does it without doing either.

## The one thing only the author can supply

Whether the camera's *ignorance* is a limit or a virtue — and the chapter is one sentence away from
having to answer. §2's third question makes knowing-who-you-are the top of the scale, so the camera,
which "knows nothing whatever about you," sits at the bottom. §5 then shows what knowledge buys: pricing,
differential pay, deactivation, all of them bad. But §6 and §7 clearly *admire* the camera — it "took
nothing from them except a day, and gave the day back watchable," and the streaming landing page that
"chooses what you see next, without knowing who you are" is offered as the nearest surviving relative.
So does ignorance make a selector safe, or merely weak? A reader finishes the chapter unsure whether the
right political demand is *selectors that know less about us* or *selectors we can argue with*, and those
are different programmes. You say plainly that you can't answer what would make selectors contestable,
and that's fine. But you can say which of the two you think matters more, and only you can — it isn't in
any of the sources.
