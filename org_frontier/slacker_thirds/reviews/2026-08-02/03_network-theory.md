## VERDICT

**Major revisions** — confined almost entirely to §2, and mostly to one paragraph of it.

The single most important fix: **§2 rests its novelty claim on a literature search, and the literature
search has a counter-example in it.** Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi's 2010 *Annual Review of Sociology*
piece reviews dyad formation under three families of mechanism — assortative, relational, and
*proximity mechanisms that focus on the social organization of interaction* — and cites Feld 1981,
Feld 1982, Feld 1997 and Bossard on residential propinquity alongside Burt 1997, Burt 2004, Lazega and
Burt on *Structural Holes*, Burt and Knez on third-party effects, and Fernandez and Gould on brokerage.
That is the setting and the broker set side by side, in one review, explaining one outcome. The
chapter's sentence — "nobody has set the two side by side and held both to one test" — is false on its
first clause. It survives on the second, and the second is where the chapter's real contribution lives.
Narrow the sentence, name Rivera et al., and the section gets stronger rather than weaker.

## STEP 0 — REGISTER

The chapter is a first-person film-studies essay in the Hansen/Bordwell line: "I" steers, scenes come
before theses, concessions land in stride, questions get asked and answered on the spot. That is a
decision and I have not touched it. First person, contractions, em-dashes and analytic parallelism are
off the table as defects.

The bar I hold it to: the invariants that survive any register — named agents in the subject slot,
claim-first paragraphs, a claim stated at a grain its evidence can carry, and no universal quantifier
resting on a sample of three. Every rewrite below is shorter, more concrete and more first-personal
than what it replaces, or I have deleted it.

## PART 1 — THEORETICAL RIGOR AND STRUCTURE

### 1.1 The Simmel reading is the best-executed part of §2, with one word wrong

The opening move is right and it is well judged. Simmel's own text does name a third that is not a
pair-broker — the power outside the parties to which all stand in the same relation — on the first page
of "The Triad," and the chapter credits him with seeing it instead of claiming the sight for itself.
The three roles are correctly catalogued. The *tertius iungens* trap is avoided (note 15 says outright
that the coinage is Obstfeld's). This is a chapter that has been burned once and is now careful, and
the care shows.

Two things to fix.

**"and he moved on."** The chapter's own sentence two lines earlier is accurate — "He calls that a
group-forming mediation, says it belongs to a later discussion." Then it closes: "He saw that not every
third is a broker of pairs, and he moved on." *Moved on* re-imports the reading the verification pass
threw out. Simmel says the case "ist indes in späterem Zusammenhang zu behandeln" — he defers it and
says he will return to it. The difference matters to my lens specifically, because "he moved on" is the
chapter making Simmel abandon the terrain the chapter then claims. Say he deferred it. It costs three
words and removes a target.

**"a fourth kind."** Simmel names this third before the three roles are all on the page; calling it "a
fourth kind" imposes the chapter's own count on him. Harmless in itself, but it reads as though the
chapter is scoring Simmel's chapter for him. "another kind" does the same work with no imposition.

And one thing to add. Simmel's deferred third *unifies* the parties — "das gemeinsame Verhältnis…
stiftet eine Vereinheitlichung zwischen ihnen." The chapter's setting explicitly "arranges no
particular meeting." Those are not the same object, and a Simmel specialist will say so. The chapter
never claims they are, which is why this is a note rather than a finding — but half a clause conceding
the gap ("his third unifies where mine only puts within reach") pre-empts the objection entirely.

### 1.2 The Burt gloss describes Obstfeld's broker, not Burt's

> "Ronald Burt showed that two people who would each gain from what the other knows, and who are not
> connected, are worth money to whoever connects them."

The plainness is admirable. The economics is Obstfeld's. Burt's advantage runs through *standing
between* — the control benefit is precisely the tertius gaudens who does not connect them. Halevy,
Halali and Zlatev, whom the chapter cites two paragraphs later, put the distinction in one line I
pulled off their p. 217 and their discussion section: brokerage positions "can give rise to distinct
processes such as tertius gaudens (keeping alters apart and serving as an intermediary) or tertius
iungens (introducing and supporting direct collaboration between alters)."

As written, the chapter gives Burt the iungens payoff and then introduces Obstfeld as the "friendlier
version" — which leaves a reader who knows the literature wondering what Obstfeld added, since Burt
has already been made to do his job. The repair is one verb.

### 1.3 "The assumption underneath is constant" is not true, and the chapter's own citation says so

> "The assumption underneath is constant, and the field states it plainly: brokerage happens 'when one
> actor (the broker) is connected to two other actors (alters) who are not themselves connected.'"

The Kwon et al. quotation is accurate. The framing around it is not. Halevy, Halali and Zlatev,
printed p. 217, verbatim from the PDF I downloaded:

> "definitions of brokering assert that brokering may occur in both closed and open triads (i.e., when
> the two alters have preexisting ties or do not have preexisting ties, respectively; Obstfeld et al.,
> 2014; Simmel, 1950). By contrast, definitions of brokerage typically view the absence of a
> preexisting tie as a prerequisite to brokerage."

So the field is openly split on the disconnection condition, in the very review the chapter quotes for
its "inclusive approach" line six sentences later. Calling the assumption "constant" hands a referee a
free hit — and worse, it names the wrong assumption. What the chapter's argument actually needs is not
that the two alters are disconnected. It is that they are *two identified alters*. That assumption is
genuinely constant across Burt, Obstfeld, Gould–Fernandez, Kwon and Halevy, and it is the one a setting
violates. Naming the right constant makes the paragraph both true and more useful.

### 1.4 The best novelty warrant in the section lost its punchline in compression

> "The formal apparatus is built on an ordered triple, and every measure that came out of it is a
> property of a node."

This is the strongest sentence in §2 and it stops one clause short. The research behind the chapter
closes the point with "a setting has no node, so it cannot receive a score," and the chapter drops it.
Without that clause the sentence is a piece of description; with it, the sentence becomes a structural
argument that no fourth PDF can refute. That matters enormously here, because §2's other novelty
warrant *is* refutable by a fourth PDF (see 1.5). Restore the clause. It is nine words and it is the
hardest thing in the section to rebut.

While restoring it, narrow "every measure that came out of it." That is a universal over an
unbounded set, asserted in the same paragraph as another universal ("nobody has"). Two-mode and
group-level brokerage formulations exist in the methods literature and the project's own research file
records that a duality-of-persons-and-groups probe was never run. "every measure I can find" is
first-person, in register, and cannot be knocked over by one counterexample.

### 1.5 The novelty claim, at the grain the evidence supports — the load-bearing weak point

> "The setting was named in 1981 and has been studied since; the broker has a literature of its own;
> nobody has set the two side by side and held both to one test. The warrant is documentary and anyone
> with three PDFs can check it."

A hostile referee attacks this first, and I attacked it first. Four findings, in descending order of
damage.

**(a) The prior-art trap is sprung.** I pulled Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi 2010 from Crossref and
OpenAlex. Verbatim abstract:

> "Three sociological mechanisms are overviewed: assortative mechanisms that draw attention to the role
> of actors' attributes, relational mechanisms that emphasize the influence of existing relationships
> and network positions, and proximity mechanisms that focus on the social organization of interaction."

I then resolved 219 of their 222 referenced works through OpenAlex. The list contains Feld's "The
Focused Organization of Social Ties" (1981), Feld 1982, Feld 1997, Bossard's 1932 "Residential
Propinquity as a Factor in Marriage Selection," Burt 1997, Burt 2004, Burt and Knez's "Kinds of
Third-Party Effects on Trust," Lazega and Burt on *Structural Holes*, and Fernandez and Gould's
brokerage paper. That is the setting tradition and the brokerage tradition inside one review, sorted
into two of three families, explaining one outcome. "Nobody has set the two side by side" is false.

The claim does not die. Rivera et al. classify mechanisms of *dyad formation between the two proximate
parties*; they do not hold a setting and a broker to a common discriminating test, they have nothing to
say about a third that coordinates an audience rather than a transacting pair, and their proximity
family is a bin, not a diagnostic. So the surviving claim is narrower and better: nobody has held the
two to one common test, and the nearest thing to it — Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi's three-family
review — sorts them into adjacent bins without asking what distinguishes them. Say that. It is a
stronger sentence than the one it replaces because it names its nearest rival instead of waiting to be
handed it.

**(b) Feld's own first sentence names Simmel.** OpenAlex's deposited abstract for Feld 1981, verbatim
(OCR typos theirs): "Sociologists since Simmel have been interseted in social circles as essential
features of friendship networks… Implications for transitivity, network bridges, and density of
personal networks are discussed and presented as propositions." The founding statement of focus theory
opens in a Simmel lineage and derives propositions about *network bridges*. The chapter's picture of
two sealed traditions is not quite the picture the founding paper paints of itself. One clause fixes
it, and the clause is free: it makes the chapter look like it has read the literature it is claiming a
gap in.

**(c) The three empty sets are real. I reproduced all three.** Word-boundary regex over my own
extractions:

| Source | Terms | Count |

| Halevy, Halali & Zlatev 2019 (17,236 words) | Feld, foci, focus theory, homophily, propinquity | 0, 0, 0, 0, 0 |
| Halevy et al., same file | Obstfeld / Simmel / Uzzi / Rivera | 24 / 23 / 2 / 0 |
| Kalmijn & Flap 2001 (10,678 words) | broker, brokerage, matchmaker, intermediar*, "third part" | 0, 0, 0, 0, 0 |
| Finkel et al. 2012 (57,712 words) | Feld, foci, Simmel, brokerage | 0, 0, 0, 0 |

"intentionally took an inclusive approach" is verbatim in Halevy et al. Every factual sentence in the
chapter's warrant paragraph is correct as printed. **The evidence is sound; the inference is not.**
Three documents not citing each other's traditions is evidence about three documents. The chapter
writes "The warrant is documentary and anyone with three PDFs can check it" — which invites the check
and then selects the three PDFs. A referee's fourth PDF is Rivera et al., and it cuts the other way.

Note also that Kalmijn and Flap cite Feld six times (Feld 1982 at three places, Feld 1984 twice, plus
the reference entries). That does not contradict the chapter's sentence — they still never mention
brokerage — but it does mean the two literatures are not hermetic, only asymmetric. The honest and more
interesting version of the finding is that the traffic runs one way: focus-theory work knows the
network literature, and the brokerage reviews do not know focus theory.

**(d) Leg three of the warrant is also qualification two, and the two uses cancel.** Eleven lines apart:

> "Finkel and colleagues' review of online dating mentions neither Feld nor Simmel."

> "And Finkel's team draws something close to my distinction inside a single platform, between a site's
> access… and its matching."

Both are true. Together they demolish the inference the empty sets are supposed to license. Finkel et
al. drew a version of the setting/selector line *without citing Feld or Simmel* — which is a worked
demonstration that a paper can draw this distinction while leaving no lexical trace for the chapter's
regex to find. The chapter's own strongest concession is a counterexample to its own method, and
nothing in the section notices. This is not fatal, because the fix is to stop asking the empty sets to
carry the universal (see (a)) — but as long as the universal stands, this pairing is the second place a
referee lands.

### 1.6 The scope limit does not repair the divide-et-impera hole. It relocates it.

This is the question I was asked to press hardest, and my answer is that the current sentence papers
over rather than repairs — though the real repair is one sentence away and is *better* for the chapter
than the one it has.

The passage:

> "That third acts on everyone and knows nothing about particulars, so on two of my three questions it
> scores as a setting — while destroying reachability rather than supplying it. The questions tell you
> what kind of third you face. What it can do to you is a separate matter."

Three problems, escalating.

**First, "two of my three" understates the damage.** Question 2 is "Could the encounter have happened
without it?" For a general prohibition of associations that "do not exist even as wishes of the
elements separated by the prohibition," the answer is plainly yes — those encounters could have
happened, and would have. So question 2 also scores the prohibition as a setting, or else it is simply
inapplicable, because there is no encounter to run the counterfactual on. Either way the honest number
is three of three, or two of three with the third undefined. A referee who does the arithmetic finds
that the chapter softened it.

**Second, "what it can do to you is a separate matter" is the wrong defence here.** It is the right
defence for Uber: Uber is a selector, and the extraction is an additional fact about a selector. It is
not the right defence for the prohibition, because the prohibition does not fail a harm test — it fails
the chapter's own *definition*. The chapter defines a setting positively: "A setting puts people within
reach of one another and supplies the conventions that let one approach another." The prohibition does
the opposite of both halves. So the prohibition is not a harmful setting. It is not a setting at all,
and the three questions cannot see that, which means the three questions are not a test of the
definition they follow.

**Third — and this is the finding — that is a repairable and interesting fact about diagnostics, and
the chapter should say it in those words.** The questions are diagnostics for sorting thirds that
already satisfy the definition; they are not a decision procedure that replaces it. Stated that way,
the *divide et impera* case stops being an embarrassment and becomes a demonstration of what the
questions are for: they tell a setting from a selector once you know you are looking at a third that
supplies reach. Simmel's prohibiting superior never enters the domain. That is the repair, it is one
sentence, it is true, and it does not require the fourth discriminator the research file offered and
the chapter sensibly declined.

I want to be fair about what the current sentence gets right: refusing to make the test a harm test is
correct, and it is the reason §5 can do its work. The problem is only that it answers an objection
nobody was making while leaving the one on the table.

### 1.7 §2's Uber exemplar asserts what §5 retracts

> "Uber picks the pair, is the only route to the match, and reads both parties in detail." (§2)

> "no published engineering account describes ride-hail dispatch as reading rider or driver identity in
> order to choose the pair, and Uber's and Lyft's own descriptions run on collective wait time and
> marketplace value. The evidence sits on the pricing side." (§5)

§2 runs the third question on Uber and gets a confident yes; §5 says the evidence for that yes is not on
the matching side at all. A referee who reads both notices, and the cost is disproportionate, because
the Uber column of §2 is where the three questions are first shown working. Either hedge §2's third
column to what §5 will support, or move the exemplar to the pricing power, which is where the evidence
actually is. I would hedge: it costs four words and it makes §5's honesty look like design rather than
retreat.

### 1.8 Gould and Fernandez lose their names, and the paragraph goes agentless with them

Compression moved them to note 17. In a paragraph whose whole point is that the formal apparatus is
pair-scoped, "The formal apparatus is built on an ordered triple" is the one abstract-noun subject in a
section that otherwise names everybody — Simmel, Burt, Obstfeld, Feld, Small, Gose, Finkel, Rosenfeld,
Thomas, Hausen. Two sentences in a row take abstract subjects ("The assumption underneath is constant…"
/ "The formal apparatus is built…") and the paragraph audibly flattens. Four words buy the names back.

### 1.9 Structure of §2 — one thing worth reordering

The section currently runs: Simmel → brokerage after Simmel → Feld and the definitions → the three
questions → the scope limit → the novelty claim and its warrant → two qualifications → the *PNAS*
exhibit. That order is right, and the decision to state the scope limit *before* the novelty claim is
a good one — it means the reader meets the test's boundary before being asked to accept that the test
is new.

The one move I would make: the measurement point ("every measure… is a property of a node," plus the
restored "a setting has no node") currently sits in the brokerage paragraph, two paragraphs before the
novelty claim it is the best warrant for. Move it, or reach back to it in one clause when the novelty
claim lands. As things stand the chapter's most durable argument is filed under description and its
most fragile one carries the weight.

### 1.10 What survives untouched, and it is most of it

The Small and Gose handling is exemplary: it concedes that they call routine organisations brokers
outright, and then does the work — "the conditions they list for successful brokerage are all
properties of institutional norms, and not one requires the third to know which two people are
involved." That sentence is the chapter's method in miniature and it is better than the novelty claim
it is defending.

The *PNAS* paragraph is the best illustration in the chapter (see 2.4 for the one thing wrong with it).
"Their own opening paragraph calls friends and family an intermediation in one sentence and 'the
essential network foci' in the next" is a genuine catch, and the answer to Rosenfeld, Thomas and
Hausen's disintermediation thesis — every figure measures a human third, so the arrival of a non-human
one is untouched — is exactly right and exactly the length it should be.

The Gould–Fernandez naming trap ("Their 'coordinator' is a within-group pair-broker, not a setting") is
correctly parked in note 17. Notes are where a referee looks for that, and it is there.

## PART 2 — THE FOUR BANS, AND THE SLOP AUDIT

### 2.1 Ban 1 — no metaphor doing argumentative work

**Clean in §2.** Every comparison in the section illustrates a claim already stated literally, or is a
quotation (Bordwell's "wiggly, knotted string," Finkel's supermarkets). "The warrant is documentary and
anyone with three PDFs can check it" is not a metaphor; it is a procedure.

One borderline case outside §2, flagged because it is doing quiet argumentative work: "*Slacker*'s
camera is the first kind in its oldest form." *Oldest form* implies a developmental sequence from
camera to landing page that nothing in the chapter establishes. It reads as taxonomy and functions as
lineage. "the earliest instance I know of the first kind" says what is meant.

### 2.2 Ban 2 — no throat-clearing

**Clean.** I looked hard for sentences whose only job is to announce a point. "What I am claiming is
narrow" is not one — it is the claim about the claim's grain, and it is load-bearing. "Two
qualifications, both real" is a hinge carrying content in five words. The compression pass appears to
have caught the real offenders and the ledger records them.

### 2.3 Ban 3 — no meta-commentary or forward reference

**Two survivors, both small, both easy.**

> "That decision is constrained, and the constraint matters later." (§4)

Straight forward reference, and the following sentence already states the constraint. Delete the
clause.

> "Surge pricing is the wrong example here, and saying why sharpens the distinction." (§5)

The second half narrates the argument's own effect. The paragraph then goes ahead and sharpens the
distinction, so the announcement is redundant.

Also, mildly: "I want to draw that difference in the right place, though" (§5) announces care rather
than exercising it — the house style's "self-narrating rigor." The paragraph loses nothing if the "I
want to" is cut and the concession simply lands.

### 2.4 Ban 4 — no jargon left unexplained

**Almost clean, and then one fatal lapse — but the lapse is a contradiction, not an omission.**

Definitions on first use are done well: structural hole ("two people who would each gain from what the
other knows, and who are not connected"), setting and selector both in bold at first use, dérive
glossed inline ("a drift, which is movement without a chooser"), civil inattention glossed, *divide et
impera* run in plain English before the Latin.

The lapse. §2, defining Feld's term:

> "A focus, in his term, is a person, place or activity that people organise around, so that ties form
> among them as a side effect."

§2, ninety words after the novelty claim, rebutting Rosenfeld, Thomas and Hausen for calling friends
and family "the essential network foci":

> "Feld's foci are not people."

The chapter defines a focus as possibly a person and then rebuts a *PNAS* paper for treating a focus as
a person. That is one section, two paragraphs apart, and it takes a referee one read to find. Worse, it
takes down the chapter's best illustration with it: if a focus can be a person, Rosenfeld, Thomas and
Hausen made no error and the exhibit evaporates.

I could not settle which side is right. Feld 1981 is closed to me as it was to the project's research
pass — the outline lists "Feld 1981 itself, through a library" under §2 author-only and says
"Everything from it is currently second-hand," and the p. 1016 definition that circulates in five
quoting sources reads "a social, psychological, legal, or physical entity around which joint activities
are organized," which settles nothing about persons on its face. **This one needs the library, and it
needs it before print,** because the chapter is currently correcting a *PNAS* paper on the strength of a
definition it has never opened.

### 2.5 Slop audit — register-aware

**This is not slop-shaped prose and I am not going to pretend it is.** The paragraphs vary in length and
in opening shape; the section does not exit on an epigram every time; nominalization is close to absent;
agents are named. The measurable tics, per 1,000 body words:

**Em-dashes.** Forty is a lot, but the register carries it and most are doing the good job — staging a
qualification then a reveal ("a drift, which is movement without a chooser" is a comma clause; "on two
of my three questions it scores as a setting — while destroying reachability" is a dash earning its
keep). I flag only the paired-dash crutches, where a clause is bolted into mid-sentence and commas or a
full stop would do the same work: "Their own opening paragraph calls friends and family an
intermediation in one sentence and 'the essential network foci' in the next" is fine; but "the
complaints are boredom, tedium, monotony, pointlessness" style lists that then take a dash-parenthetical
in the same paragraph start to drum. Three or four conversions across the chapter, author's ear, not
mine.

**`rather than` at thirteen.** This is the chapter's signature corrective move and I understand where it
came from: a verification pass installed thirteen scope limits and each one wanted the same shape.
Individually every one is doing work. Collectively they are a metronome, and two of them sit close
enough to hear: "while destroying reachability rather than supplying it" (§2) and "A code that reads
types rather than persons" (§3). I would convert two or three to a plain positive plus a short second
sentence — that is the corpus's own rhythm fix (dense sentence, then a short one), and it will read as
more confident, not less careful.

**No performed-rigor phrases.** No "it is important to note," no "crucially," no "stated plainly." The
one exception is 2.3's "I want to draw that difference in the right place."

**No landing-line drumbeat in §2.** Sections 2, 3 and 4 end on a plain statement of fact ("Feld's foci
are not people." / "It did not decide which of them the film would follow." / "The relations here are
the order of the visits, and somebody chose it."). Two of the three are strong. The third is the
contradiction from 2.4 — which is worth noticing as a structural point: the sentence is placed where a
section-closing beat goes, and the beat is why nobody caught that it contradicts the definition.

## BIGGEST GENUINE STRENGTH

The chapter states its own claim at a smaller grain than the argument would let it get away with, and
then hands the reader the instrument to check it. "The warrant is documentary and anyone with three
PDFs can check it" is a sentence almost nobody writes, and the reason I could run three regexes against

The second strength, in my lens specifically: the Small and Gose handling. Conceding that the nearest

## THE ONE THING ONLY THE AUTHOR CAN SUPPLY

**Scott Feld, "The Focused Organization of Social Ties," *AJS* 86 (1981): 1015–1035, page 1016, from a
