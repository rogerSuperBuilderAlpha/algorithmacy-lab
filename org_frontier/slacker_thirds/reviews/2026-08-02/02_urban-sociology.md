## Part 1 — theoretical rigor and structure

### 1.1 Feld is credited with a mechanism whose output the film does not have

This is the deepest problem in my lens, and it is a concession the chapter can make cheaply.

§2 reads:

> There is another way to hold the position, and Scott Feld named the mechanism in 1981. A focus, in
> his term, is a person, place or activity that people organise around, so that ties form among them
> as a side effect.

I could not open Feld 1981 either (the outline records the same: "The article itself was never
opened"). What I did retrieve is the publisher-deposited abstract through Crossref and OpenAlex
(DOI 10.1086/227352, *AJS* 86:5, 1015–1035, March 1981), and it is enough to make the point:

> "This paper presents a theory of the social organization of **friendship ties**. The approach is
> based upon Homans's concepts of **activities, interactions, and sentiments** and upon the concept of
> extra-network foci **organizing social activities and interaction**. … The focus theory is [shown] to
> help explain patterns of friendships in the 1965-66 Detroit Area Study."

Three things follow, and the chapter concedes none of them.

**(a) Feld's output is friendship ties. *Slacker* has none.** §1 makes the film's tie-lessness the
whole puzzle — "Of the ninety-eight credited roles, almost none meet twice" — and §4 makes it a boast:
"The characters share no history, no reunions, no coincidences that pay off later." Feld's mechanism
runs Homans's chain: joint activity produces repeated interaction, repeated interaction produces
sentiment, sentiment is the tie. The chapter takes the first link and discards the rest, silently. A
referee who opens Feld will notice inside a page.

**(b) Feld's foci are bounded and recurrent. A metropolitan area is not one.** "Extra-network foci
organizing social activities" is a workplace, a congregation, a bowling league. Austin-in-1990 is not
a focus in Feld's sense; it is the population from which foci are drawn. And the film's own settings
*are* Feldian foci — the coffee shop, the Drag, the campus, the walk-ups, the bookstore. The chapter
has better Feld material on screen than the one it uses.

**(c) Half the definition isn't Feld's at all.** The chapter's setting "puts people within reach of
one another **and supplies the conventions that let one approach another**." Feld supplies the reach.
The conventions are Goffman's and Lofland's. Crediting the whole definition to a 1981 network paper
misdescribes the chapter's own genealogy in a section whose entire warrant is documentary.

The fix costs about forty-five words and it *strengthens* the novelty claim, because the honest
version — I am taking Feld's side-effect logic up a level and taking the conventions from somewhere
else — is exactly the move nobody has made. See R7.

### 1.2 Lofland has vanished, and two load-bearing sentences are now hers uncredited

`00_breakdown.md` listed "Lofland 1973 | The core mechanism: a city orders strangers by appearance and
location | **No**" in the survives-removal column. v3 removed her anyway, and her concept stayed:

- §2: "Austin acts on the whole street, is one route among many, and **reads types at most**."
- §3: "A **code that reads types rather than persons** is no neutral improver of odds."

That is appearential ordering — knowing strangers by how they look rather than who they are — and it
runs across two sections as an unattributed premise. The §3 research explains why she went (no page
could be opened this pass), and that is a fair reason not to *quote* her. It is not a reason to use
her idea without her name. A concept citation needs no page.

### 1.3 Civil inattention is used for the right *fact* and the wrong *relation*

> The mechanism is not politeness. Erving Goffman's civil inattention — the glance and withdrawal by
> which strangers in public leave each other alone — explains why most people on that sidewalk do not
> speak. What licenses the monologue is **the opposite rule**, and Emanuel Schegloff describes it…

The gloss of civil inattention is accurate and plain, and using it to explain non-engagement is right
(it is also what the July trap-register demanded: "Civil inattention explains non-engagement"). The
error is "the opposite rule."

Civil inattention and summons–answer are not opposites. They are **consecutive**. Goffman governs
whether an encounter may be opened at all; Schegloff governs what happens once it has been. They sit
at different points in the same sequence, and calling the second the opposite of the first is a level
slip that costs the paragraph its actual insight — which is that the monologue lives in the *seam*
between the two rules.

Two smaller things in the same gloss. Civil inattention is not merely leaving each other alone; it is
a shown-then-withdrawn recognition, which is precisely why breaching it registers as a move rather
than as noise. And the chapter's own scene undercuts the mechanism as stated: the man leaving the
coffee shop largely does **not** answer. If he does not answer, the summons–answer sequence never
completes, and the binding force in the scene is Schegloff's *second* passage — the one the chapter
demotes to a concession — not his first.

### 1.4 The Schegloff attribution is correct, and the concession is the best sentence in the section

Credit where it is due, because this is the part I was asked to check hardest.

> Schegloff also closes the escape route at the other end, though: in co-presence a member cannot
> simply decline to answer, since the excusing inference is unavailable and cold-shouldering gets read
> in instead. Both sides of an opening cost something. **That the cost falls harder on the person who
> wants out is my claim, not his.**

That is exactly right against the §3 research (Finding 1: "Schegloff establishes a cost on both sides
of an opening. If the chapter wants an asymmetry it must argue for it *against* Schegloff, not from
him"). The chapter states the counterweight, does not smooth it, and takes ownership of the residue in
seven words. Most chapters would have buried this in a footnote.

Three residual problems, all small:

- **"nonterminal, offering no natural exit" overstates the term.** Nonterminality means the
  summons–answer pair cannot be the *last* exchange — it projects further talk. It does not mean the
  encounter has no exit; Schegloff and Sacks spent a whole later paper on how closings get achieved.
  For a monologue of this length the chapter needs the absence of an *achieved* closing, not
  nonterminality. Two words fix it.
- **"the excusing inference is unavailable" is unglossed jargon** — a ban-4 hit. One clause: *you
  can't be assumed not to have heard.*
- **The source's limits travel with every other source in this chapter and not with this one.**
  Ildirar gets her sound complication, Gaughen gets his non-exhaustiveness, Poulaki gets *Gomorrah*,
  Small gets her four conditions. Schegloff's corpus is tape-recorded telephone calls to a police
  complaint desk, and his co-present illustrations are acquainted parties, not strangers. The chapter
  says none of it. That is an inconsistency of standard, and a referee who knows the paper will read
  the silence as ignorance rather than economy.

### 1.5 *Papachristou* is doing real work, but it is pointed at the weaker of two targets

The material is genuinely earned and it is not decoration — it replaces an assertion with a dated,
quotable instrument, which is what the July panel asked for. But as deployed it argues the small
version, and the source's own facts argue the large one.

The chapter's payoff is: "A code that reads types rather than persons is no neutral improver of odds."

The case's facts, which I retrieved and read (official U.S. Reports PDF, 405 U.S. 156–171):

> "The facts are stipulated. Papachristou and Calloway are white females. Melton and Johnson are
> black males." (at 158)
>
> "The arresting officers denied that the racial mixture in the car played any part in the decision to
> make the arrest." (at 159)

The ordinance read types. **The officer read persons** — these four, in this car, tonight. Run the
chapter's own three questions on him and he comes out a selector: he acts on two particular people
rather than everyone, he is the only thing standing between them and the rest of the evening, and he
reads them in detail and changes what he does accordingly. That is a *setting with a selector inside
it*, and it is the most interesting thing in §3 by some distance. It also complicates the apparatus in
the same productive way §2's *divide et impera* concession does — which is the chapter's established
move, so it will not read as a wobble.

Second problem, and it is the one a referee will raise first: **the ordinance was struck down in 1972
and the film is from 1990.** As written, "Until 1972 a vagrancy ordinance of the kind struck down in
*Papachristou*…" names an instrument that had been dead for eighteen years when Linklater rolled
camera. The section never says what stood in its place. I am not going to tell you what Austin's 1990
street-level ordinances were — I did not check, and the standing caution applies — but the chapter
must either say, or say plainly that what outlived the ordinance is the discretion rather than the
statute. May 2022 is already in the section doing exactly that job at the nightclub door; it just
needs the connective.

### 1.6 The three questions, stress-tested from the city side

Question 2 — "Could the encounter have happened without it?" — is close to vacuous when the third is a
city. Could the moon-landing man have met his victim without Austin? The counterfactual has no
content; he lives there. §7 states the same test in a form that survives: choices that "stop being one
route across a field the parties could cross by themselves, and start being the only route." That is
the usable version. Align §2 to it.

(The *divide et impera* concession is correctly handled and I have no objection to it. It is the right
kind of self-inflicted wound and it is placed where a reader will try the case.)

## Part 2 — the four bans, and an AI-slop audit of §3

**Ban 1 — metaphor doing argumentative work.** §3 is clean. "The American law of the street" is a
light figure but it names a real body of law and the next sentence is literal. The one sentence that
functions like a maxim standing in for a mechanism is **"Cheap space buys unstructured time, and
unstructured time is what this film is about"** — it is literal, so it passes the ban, but it is
unsupported by the numbers on either side of it (see 3.1 below), which is the same failure by a
different route.

**Ban 2 — throat-clearing.** Clean. §3 opens on two scenes and never announces itself.

**Ban 3 — meta-commentary.** Clean, with one borderline: "so I want one instance rather than a
pattern.[^35] **One instance is enough.**" The second sentence narrates the sufficiency of the
evidence instead of demonstrating it. It is a hair's breadth from "stating the virtue of stating it."
Four words fix it (R9). "That the cost falls harder … is my claim, not his" is attribution, not
meta-commentary — keep it.

**Ban 4 — unexplained jargon.** Three hits, one of them serious.

| Term | Where | Status |
|---|---|---|
| civil inattention | §3 | Glossed in-line. ✓ |
| **assortative matching** | §3, Mion & Naticchioni | **Unglossed, and the sentence is load-bearing** — it is the counterweight to the whole agglomeration paragraph, and a reader who doesn't own the term gets nothing from it |
| **the excusing inference** | §3, Schegloff | **Unglossed.** Conversation-analytic shorthand |
| observationally equivalent | §3 | Glossed by the surrounding clause. Borderline, acceptable |

**AI-slop audit, §3 only.** Antithesis-machine constructions run about eight per thousand words in
this section — high against the house target, though the register here is Hansen/Bordwell rather than
the dissertation, so I am calibrating loosely. Three of them are load-bearing corrections the
verification pass *won* and must stay: "market size rather than density as such," "reads types rather
than persons," "one instance rather than a pattern." Two are decoration and can go: **"The mechanism
is not politeness"** (the positive statement is stronger and R2 supplies it) and **"is no neutral
improver of odds"** (replaced in R3 by a claim rather than a negation).

Rhythm: five paragraphs in §3, four opening on a short flat subject-verb declarative — "The mechanism
is not politeness." / "The material base was ordinary." / "A setting that supplies conventions…" /
"One thing the city did not do…". That is the metronome the house style warns about. R2 and R3 already
break two of the four; no further action needed.

No filler transitions, no empty closers, no agentless passives found in §3. The section reads as
written by a person.

## Part 3 — ranked, paste-ready revisions

Ranked by how much damage the current sentence does if it goes to press.

### R1 — §3, the material base. **The census table contradicts the sentence it is cited for.** (fatal-adjacent; I verified this myself)

Current:

> Median contract rent on census day 1990 was $346 and the rental vacancy rate was 11.6 percent.[^29]
> **Cheap space buys unstructured time**, and unstructured time is what this film is about. Dallas that
> year sat at 15.3 percent vacancy and Houston at 15.8, so Austin was the tighter market of the three;
> what it had was a great deal of room and 50,245 students at the university in the middle of it.

I downloaded *1990 Census of Housing, General Housing Characteristics: Texas*, CH-1-45, sec. 1,
Table 1 — the chapter's own note 29 — and read the place rows. Median contract rent, 1990:

| Place | Median contract rent | Rental vacancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Austin city** | **$346** | **11.6** |
| Dallas city | $373 | 15.3 |
| Houston city | $324 | 15.8 |
| San Antonio city | $307 | 11.6 |
| Fort Worth city | $335 | 15.4 |

Austin was **more expensive than Houston, San Antonio and Fort Worth**, and its vacancy rate was
matched to the decimal by San Antonio. The chapter prints the one comparison that flatters the claim
(vacancy against the two crashed metros) and omits the one that kills it (rent against everybody). A
referee with the same PDF finds this in ten minutes, and it lands on the word "cheap," which the whole
paragraph is built to earn.

The argument does not actually need cheapness. It needs **slack** — empty units, no new building, no
pressure — and the table gives that outright.

Paste-ready:

> The material base was slack, though not cheap. Austin's median contract rent on census day 1990 was
> $346, above Houston's $324 and below Dallas's $373; what set the city apart was empty units. The
> rental vacancy rate was 11.6 percent — one rental in nine standing open — in a housing stock that had
> grown 48.0 percent since 1980 against a population up 34.6.[^27][^29] Slack space buys unstructured
> time, and unstructured time is what this film is about. Add 50,245 students at the university in the
> middle of the city and the sidewalks stay stocked with people who owe nobody an hour.[^30]

Net: −6 words, and the "great deal of room" claim now rests on the number that supports it.

### R2 — §3, Goffman and Schegloff as consecutive rules rather than opposite ones (serious)

Current:

> The mechanism is not politeness. Erving Goffman's civil inattention — the glance and withdrawal by
> which strangers in public leave each other alone — explains why most people on that sidewalk do not
> speak. What licenses the monologue is the opposite rule, and Emanuel Schegloff describes it: a
> summons gets an answer, and the sequence it starts is nonterminal, offering no natural exit. The
> moon-landing man exploits that. Schegloff also closes the escape route at the other end, though: in
> co-presence a member cannot simply decline to answer, since the excusing inference is unavailable and
> cold-shouldering gets read in instead. Both sides of an opening cost something. That the cost falls
> harder on the person who wants out is my claim, not his.

Paste-ready:

> Two rules run one after the other here, and the monologue lives in the seam. Erving Goffman's civil
> inattention — the glance given and withdrawn, by which strangers show they have seen each other and
> decline to make anything of it — governs whether a stranger may be addressed at all, and on that
> sidewalk the answer is normally no. Emanuel Schegloff governs what happens once the answer is yes: a
> summons that gets an answer opens a sequence that can't be the last thing said, and answering commits
> you to listening further.[^26] The moon-landing man never waits for a yes. He talks, and Schegloff's
> harder point does the rest — face to face you can't decline to answer without the decline being read,
> because you can't be assumed not to have heard. Both sides of an opening cost something. That the
> cost falls harder on the person who wants out is my claim, not his.

Fixes the level slip, glosses "the excusing inference," corrects "nonterminal … no natural exit" to
what nonterminality actually says, and puts the scene's real mechanism (he never answers) where the
scene is. Net: +22 words. §2 is 132 over budget; take it from there.

## Verdict

**Minor revisions.** Nothing in §3 is fabricated, and the section is markedly more honest than most
published urban-sociological writing about film. What it needs is: one number corrected against its
own source, one credit split between Feld and Goffman/Lofland, one relation ("opposite") changed to
the right one ("consecutive"), and *Papachristou* aimed at the target its own facts support. Every one
of those is a half-hour's work and none of them costs the chapter an argument. Two of them gain it one.

## Biggest genuine strength

The chapter states counterweights against itself and then keeps going, and it does it in the register
rather than in a footnote. "Both sides of an opening cost something. That the cost falls harder on the
person who wants out is my claim, not his" is the whole method in twenty-three words: name the source,
state what the source refuses you, take ownership of the residue, move. The same discipline shows in
"his survey is non-exhaustive and outdoor-only, and he says so," in "no measured elasticity can be
assigned to matching alone," and in the *divide et impera* paragraph that deliberately breaks the
chapter's own test. Sections built this way are very hard to referee down, which is the point.

Second, narrower: replacing the rent anecdote with primary counts was the right call, and the section
argues from a Supreme Court opinion rather than from a gesture on the sorting question. That is what
the July panel asked for and it was delivered.

## The one thing only the author can supply

**What Austin was cheap relative to — and therefore whether §3's material base is an argument about
price or about slack.** The census table will not settle it: on that table Austin is the *expensive*
end of urban Texas and the *loose* end of the rental market at the same time. The comparison that
would make "cheap" true is one only you can choose — rent against local wages, rent against the
coastal cities the film's audience was watching from, rent against what a part-time job at a coffee
shop covered in 1990. Pick the benchmark and print it, or drop the word and argue slack, which the
data hands you free.

Second, and it is the standing author-only item from the outline: **Feld 1981 itself, at page level.**
Everything the chapter's central term rests on is second-hand through an abstract and five quoting
sources, and the decision in 1.1 — whether the setting is Austin or Austin's foci — should be made
with his pages open.
