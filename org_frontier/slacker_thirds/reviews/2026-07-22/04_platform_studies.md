## Part 1 — The platform claims, audited

### 1.1 The Uber facts — one internal contradiction, otherwise at grain (§5)

**The paragraph that gets it right.** "In the years researchers studied it, a driver got an offer
with about fifteen seconds to take it and no destination shown: accept blind, or decline and be
scored for declining" — this is exactly Rosenblat and Stark's documented design (the ~15-second
acceptance window, hidden destination, acceptance-rate discipline), correctly fenced to the studied
period. "Uber has since put destinations and fares up front in many markets; what hasn't moved is
that only the algorithm sees the queue" — the July 3 fix (synthesis item 11), implemented cleanly.
The durable claim is the right one: queue visibility, not destination-hiding, is the asymmetry that
survives Uber's product changes.

**The sentence that takes it back.** Two paragraphs later: "the driver never sees the destination,
the queue, the logic, or the reason for a deactivation, and that asymmetry is itself the control."
Present tense, unhedged — and the chapter itself just said destinations are now shown up front in
many markets. A platform referee quotes these two sentences side by side and the Uber-hedge fix is
undone. The repair costs nothing: drop "the destination" from the list; the queue, the logic, and
the deactivation reason are the withholdings that hold in 2026. (See R1.) A smaller 2026 caution on
the same list: "the reason for a deactivation" is attackable at the margin — the EU's platform-work
rules and Uber's own review process have pried that door open in some jurisdictions. I would not
spend body words on it; if the author wants armor, "or much of the reason for a deactivation" buys
it for one word.

**"That asymmetry is itself the control"** is Rosenblat and Stark's actual thesis — information
asymmetry as the managerial instrument — deployed precisely. Keep.

**"Surge pricing is a nudge with a dollar figure"** — correct grain; Rosenblat and Stark document
surge heatmaps as behavioral steering. **"It can deactivate — delete a party from the crowd
altogether"** with the Kellogg, Valentine & Christin note — correct; deactivation sits under their
"replacing" mechanism. Both are July 3 accepted items (22), still intact.

**"Nothing in the design lets a rider and a driver settle anything between themselves"** — scoped
to design per the triage; holds. **"There's no one in either to argue with"** — defensible; the
support-ticket loops Rosenblat documents are the proof that arguing reaches no negotiating party.

**Stark & Pais quote** ("rankings but not ranks … accounts but no accountability"): pages are
already on the author checklist as a library check. One line of added instruction: verify the
*wording* along with the pages — quote-memory on this phrase varies ("no accountability" vs. "not
accountability"), and the Sociologica galley was unreachable when the panel last tried. Not a new
flag; a rider on an existing checklist item.

### 1.2 The club micro-thirds insertion (§5, new) — conceptually smudged, one word from clean

The new sentence: "So are the film's small engineering thirds: a doorman holding a guest list
that's missing Steve's name, a paid-entry stamp pressed onto a cousin's licked wrist, a barman
emptying the club at two. Each is visible and arguable, and two get gamed on screen."

The insertion's *purpose* is right and belongs in the chapter: it puts visible, arguable, gameable
control on screen exactly where the platform's control is proprietary. The problem is the label. Run
the three examples through the chapter's own §2 test:

- **The doorman** passes: he acts on particular parties (checks names), is the only route in, and
  reads the parties (the list). A genuine engineering third.
- **The stamp** half-passes: a stamp-check reads a credential, not a person — closer to sorting
  types than reading parties. And the gaming (the licked wrist) works *because* it reads types.
- **The barman at two** fails the test outright: closing time acts on the whole crowd uniformly,
  brokers no pair, reads no one. By §2's own criteria that is host-side behavior — a setting rule.

So two of three "small engineering thirds" are not engineers by the chapter's definition, and a
referee who has been handed the three-question test in §2 will run it here. Worse, the mislabel
muddies the composes/governs asymmetry the section just built: what the doorman, the stamp, and the
curfew actually share is the *governs* family — pricing entry, striking names, calling time — the
very powers §5 says no camera has. That is not a bug in the argument; it is a better version of it.
The club is where the film stages *governance* at watchable scale, and because it is watchable, it
gets gamed. That reading strengthens the public/proprietary point and feeds §7's contestability
question directly: visible governing thirds get argued with; the proprietary one doesn't.

The fix is one word plus an appositive (R2): call them the film's small *governing* thirds and name
the three powers. The doorman example then carries the engineering overlap on its own (he does pick
and read), and nothing in the composes/governs asymmetry is disturbed — the camera still only
composes; the film's governors are club-sized and filmable.

**Empirical status:** the guest list, the transferable hand stamp, and the 2 a.m. clear-out are all
real club-governance mechanisms, and stamp-transfer by licked wrist is documented club practice. But
the three scene details ("Steve's name," the cousin's wrist, the barman at two) and "two get gamed
on screen" (which two?) are film-content claims that our research files do not verify — the class of
claim the July 3 panel resolved to assert only from the disc. These are new since July 3 and must go
on the disc-check list (see closing note).

### 1.3 The dispatch-radius wince (§4)

"A dispatch algorithm is not limited to whoever happens to be standing nearby." A platform
specialist winces: proximity is precisely what ride dispatch runs on — nearest-driver matching
inside a geofenced radius is the core of the design, and driver folklore about the radius is half of
what Möhlmann's workers are sensemaking about. What the platform abolished is not *nearness* but the
*sightline*: the camera's pool is whoever shares the lens's reach; the dispatcher's pool is every
idle driver on the grid, seen at once. The preceding sentence ("its picks are drawn from whoever the
street has assembled within reach of the lens") already supplies the right contrast term. R3 gives a
+5-word repair that keeps the point and closes the wince.

### 1.4 "Two faces" and Ajunwa's tertius bifrons (§4/§5) — a collision worth one footnote line

§4 coins "The engineering third comes in two faces" (attention vs. labor). §5 then cites Ajunwa's
"The Algorithmic Capture of Employment and the Tertius Bifrons" for "Scholars have spotted Simmel
here before." A platform-literate reader will connect the two — and Ajunwa's two-faced third means
something else: the platform showing one face to workers (manager) and another to regulators (mere
intermediary). The chapter's two faces split the third's *object* (whose attention, whose labor);
Ajunwa's split its *audiences*. Undisambiguated, the chapter looks like it is either borrowing her
coinage without saying so or contradicting it. One sentence added to note 39 settles it at zero body
cost (R9). This is also the honest answer to the prior-art trap for §4's distinction: the nearest
rival coinage exists, is cited in the chapter, and differs — say how.

### 1.5 Möhlmann in §6 — correctly deployed

The citation sits where it belongs: on the testable-version sentence ("how fast a person builds a
working theory of an algorithm, the way platform workers do"). That is what Möhlmann, Salge, and
Marabelli actually studied — platform workers building working theories of an opaque algorithmic
manager, individually and together — and the chapter's second, uncited use ("platform workers
pooling theories of a dispatcher they can't see, making sense of the algorithm together") is a fair
gloss of the paper's collective-sensemaking half, with the note already attached one sentence
earlier. The hedge around the transfer claim ("nobody has shown that the skill a moviegoer brings to
a cut is the skill a driver brings to the dispatch queue, and only a real study could") is exactly
right and, to my knowledge, true — no such study exists. The July 3 transfer-prediction addition
(item 24) is implemented. No changes requested.

### 1.6 §7 streaming and the pirate-TV chain — at grain, two notes

The enclosure sequence now runs at the defensible grain the triage set: Perren's boom absorbed not
ended, "Most of" the specialty divisions, the chilly post-2022 market with Neon/A24 outbidding, and
Smits's brokers persisting and reorienting toward the streamer-buyers ("didn't vanish so much as
reorient" — correct Smits). Frey deployed correctly ("Viewers, it turns out, neither trust nor much
use the recommendations"), and the pivot that follows — the enclosure runs through acquisition and
licensing, not the recommendation row — is the right answer to Frey and the strongest
platform-studies move in the section. One small wobble: "already chosen what got acquired, licensed,
and surfaced. It works in the plumbing, not at the interface" — *surfacing* is arguably the
interface. Swap "surfaced" for "carried" and the plumbing sentence is airtight (R7).

Two verification notes, both new since July 3. (a) The pirate-TV sentence — "the Backpacker's
contraband tape headed for Kansas City pirate TV by dub and acquaintance" — is a film-content claim
not in our research files; disc-check. The sentence itself is well made. (b) The Orion→Sony Pictures
Classics lineage ("its Classics team left to found Sony Pictures Classics") was on the July 3
"verified by reviewers but not by us" list and now appears asserted, without a note. The bankruptcy
arithmetic holds (July 1991 release, December 1991 Chapter 11 filing = five months). The lineage
fact is almost certainly right — and the bibliography already contains its likely source: Barker,
"*Slacker*: Looking Back," written by the Sony Pictures Classics co-president who released the film
at Orion Classics. Check that the Barker piece states the lineage and hang a note on the sentence
(R10). No new bibliography entry needed.

## Closing note

**Biggest genuine strength, from this lens:** the chapter gets the algorithmic-management
literature *right* — rare in film-studies crossings. "That asymmetry is itself the control" is
Rosenblat and Stark's finding stated better than most management papers state it; the
composes/governs pair is a real conceptual contribution the platform literature could borrow back;
and the taxi-dispatcher sentence ("somewhere a dispatcher is picking fares off a radio") disarms
the obvious historical objection in one image. The telemetry paragraph (the studio's box-office
totals, months late, vs. this viewer fed back into this cut) is the cleanest statement of the
cinema/platform difference I know of at this length.

**What only the author can supply** — the disc-check list, updated with the new insertions this
panel round must add to the standing July 3 items:
1. Club sequence: the guest-list name ("Steve"?), the licked-wrist stamp transfer, the barman
   clearing the club at two, and *which two* get gamed on screen.
2. Video Backpacker: the exact wording of "much more powerful and useful than an actual event" and
   the died-in-the-street detail (rewind/hue).
3. The returning traveler's "or you just saw it on TV" line.
4. The pirate-TV chain: Kansas City, the dub route.
5. The PixelVision party scene: "no film" vs. "no tape" in the dialogue; and whether the final
   cliff-flung camera is Super-8 (then "another such camera" must soften).
6. The Barker piece for the Orion Classics → Sony Pictures Classics lineage (R10).

None of these can be fabricated by a reviewer, and the chapter's credibility with exactly the
readers it courts — people who know both the film and the platforms — depends on their being right.
