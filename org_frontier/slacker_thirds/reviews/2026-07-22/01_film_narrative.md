## Part 1 — Theoretical rigor through the film-narrative lens

### 1.1 The narration-theory spine is sound

**The Bordwell balk (§4).** Deployed accurately and, better, *productively*. The quote ("Another borderline
case, I think, is *Slacker* … This isn't a network so much as a wiggly, knotted string," *Poetics of
Cinema*, 215) was verified in the July 3 round; the chapter's move — reading the balk as evidence that the
film's coherence lives somewhere Bordwell's character-network frame can't see — is legitimate narratology,
not a gotcha. Bordwell's degrees-of-separation essay is about ties among *characters*; the chapter agrees
the characters have almost none and relocates the network to the narration. A specialist will not wince.

**The Bordwell/Chatman standoff (§4).** Both glosses are fair. "Warned against picturing the camera as a
little storyteller with intentions — that, he said, is a fiction we project onto the film" is *Narration in
the Fiction Film*'s anti-anthropomorphic argument in plain clothes. And "even if that something is only the
film's own organizing logic" is a defensible gloss of Chatman's cinematic narrator, which *Coming to Terms*
explicitly de-personifies (the narrator as the composite of the medium's communicating devices, not a
little person). The chapter then rests only on what both camps grant — the choices are systematic — which
is exactly the safe ground. This paragraph would survive a hostile narratologist.

**The star/chain claim (§4).** Sound as reworked in July. The obvious referee objection — *every* narrated
film is trivially a star with narration at the hub — is pre-answered by the strip-test sentence ("Strip out
every durable character-to-character tie … and what still holds the film together is the welding"): in an
ordinary ensemble both graphs are dense, and only here does the star do all the work. Ramírez Berg's "daisy
chain" is his actual term with *Slacker* as his actual example. The plain-language handling of what a
narratologist would call fabula/syuzhet ("in the story world" / "in how the film is put together") is a
register choice, not an evasion, and I endorse it.

**The Poulaki engagement (§4).** Fair to her at her level. Her *Screen* piece explains network-film
coherence through complexity — recurrence, motifs, self-organization — and the chapter concedes the
resonance before pressing the one thing recurrence cannot do: cause the joins. "A theme rhymes across the
cuts but doesn't cause them" is the right objection stated at the right grain. One caution to preserve:
Poulaki 2014 does not discuss *Slacker* (Bordwell's borderline case is the bridge into her territory), and
the chapter correctly never implies she does. Keep it that way through copyedit.

**Kuleshov (§6) — the one specialist wince.** "Lev Kuleshov showed that viewers reliably read…" overstates
a famously shaky demonstration: the original footage is lost, the accounts descend largely from Pudovkin,
and Prince and Hensley's 1992 reconstruction (*Cinema Journal* 31, no. 2) failed to replicate the effect.
Nothing in the argument needs "reliably" — the next sentence ("Continuity editing made that inference a
reflex") carries the real weight and stands on a century of practice, not on one experiment. Soften the
verb, drop the adverb (Part 3, R4). No new citation required at this word budget, though the author may add
one to the note if the collection's referees are fussy.

**"Hyperlink cinema" (§4).** Quart coined it (*Film Comment*, 2005); cited to Quart; the objection ("a
hyperlink waits for you to click, and nobody clicks here") is accurate and is one of the chapter's best
bridges to the platform argument. Clear.

**"The relay repeats some three dozen times" (§4).** Matches the ~35-segment scene inventory. Clear.

### 1.2 The six new insertions — audit against the film record

I checked every insertion against `research/slacker_transcript.md` and `research/scene_inventory.md`. This
is the good news of the round: five of six verify nearly verbatim, so the July 3 rule (no unverified
film-content assertions) is largely being honored, not breached. The exceptions, in severity order:

**(a) The ending camera — factual error.** Chapter: "The movie's last image comes from another such
camera, flung off a cliff…" following the PixelVision handoff. Scene inventory seg. 30 is the PixelVision
party; seg. 35 is "Super-8 carload to the cliff; camera thrown off." Two scenes, two formats — and the
inventory's own commentary (line 64) treats the PixelVision scene as *strengthening* "the ending's Super-8
reading," i.e., the research file already knew they were different cameras. "Another such camera" asserts
what the July 3 panel explicitly declined to assert ("Super-8 ending specifics… author checklist") and
gets it wrong. Fix in Part 3, R1. (The thematic point survives untouched — arguably improves: the handed-out
PixelVision holds no film, and the group that *did* load a camera, a different one, ends the movie.)

**(b) The pirate-TV tape — ownership misattributed, plus a placement smudge (§7).** Chapter: "the
Backpacker's contraband tape headed for Kansas City." Transcript 1090–1125: a visitor brings the hostage
tape — "So this is a dub of my copy. You think you'd want a tape of this?" — and the Backpacker supplies
the route: "Yeah, I have some friends in Kansas City. Pirate TV, man." The tape is the visitor's dub; the
Backpacker is the relay node. Small, but this chapter of all chapters should get the chain's links right.
Second, the insertion now sits between the video-stores sentence and "Every link could be argued with, and
was," so that sentence's antecedent smears across two chains (the real distribution chain and the fictional
pirate one). Reorder and reattribute in R2.

**(c) The Video Backpacker (§6) — verifies, with one word too strong.** "A video image is much more
powerful and useful than an actual event" — verbatim (transcript 1067–68). "Missing rewind and wrong hue" —
verbatim basis ("I can't press rewind… The hue was off and I couldn't adjust the hue"). But "saw a man
*die* in the street" outruns the monologue: the man comes barreling out of a bar and falls with a knife in
his back; his death is implied, never stated. "Knifed in the street" is both accurate and more concrete
(R3). Optional enrichment the author may like: the monologue's final grievance is "I didn't even see the
knife impact on the body. I missed that part" — the spectator complaining he missed the cut-in. I leave it
as an option; it would need a matching cut elsewhere.

**(d) "Saw it on TV" (§6) — scene frame slightly misdescribed.** Chapter: "Another character comes back
from traveling unable to say whether any of it really happened." Transcript 494–510: the speaker is
dissuading a friend who wants to leave the country — "I've traveled… And when you get back, you can't tell
whether it really happened to you or you just saw it on TV." It's a warning delivered in Austin, not a
homecoming scene. The quote is verbatim; the narrative wrapper isn't. R5 reframes it as the warning at
nearly the same length.

**(e) The breakthrough-day talker (§4) — quotes verbatim, location unverified.** "I told you I was having
a breakthrough day today" (1148), "there is no structure" (1157), "The underlying order is chaos, man"
(1158) — all verbatim. What the research files do *not* establish is "café": neither transcript nor
inventory places the scene. Drop "café" or verify it on the disc (R6). The move itself — the film voicing
the rival account so the chapter can answer it — is the strongest of the six insertions; "But somebody
chose where his scene goes" is the Poulaki paragraph's best sentence.

**(f) Ulysses (§4) and the club micro-thirds (§5) — verify cleanly.** The bridge scene: transcript 604–648
confirms the creek bridge (inventory seg. 15), the jilted friend, the coerced audition of the passage, and
both quoted phrases verbatim ("the last term of a preceding series," "a series originating in and repeated
to infinity"). The club thirds: guest list missing Steve's name ("The name's Steve. S-T-E-V-E." / "Sorry,
Steve. Don't see you," 1354–56), the stamp-on-licked-wrist trick with the cousin (1389–95, seg. 29), the
eviction (1444–47; the inventory calls it "the 2 a.m. eviction" — the *time* should be confirmed on the
disc, since my greps find no clock in the dialogue). "Two get gamed on screen" is defensible (the stamp
trick and the guest list) but a reader can't tell which two; no fix required at this budget, though the
author should be ready to answer.

### 1.3 Prior-art check on the insertions

- **Ulysses.** The project's own research file (`film_slacker.md`) records that Rob Stone's chapter on the
  film's form (chap. 4, "The Form and Content of Slack") *opens* by placing *Slacker* against Joyce's
  *Ulysses* as unfilmable modernist form. The chapter's reading is different in kind — Stone uses *Ulysses*
  as a formal analogue; the chapter reads the on-screen recitation as the film stating its own serial
  grammar — but a Linklater specialist will know Stone's opening, and the sentence currently carries no
  note. Add one (R7): notes don't count against the body ceiling, and citing Stone here converts a
  potential "you missed Stone" into "extends Stone." The author should also eyeball Stone pp. 74–103 at the
  library (already adjacent to the standing checklist) to confirm how far his Joyce discussion goes.
- **PixelVision/ending reflexivity.** That the film ends by handing cameras to its characters is a
  commonplace of *Slacker* criticism; the chapter claims no novelty and adds a genuinely new spin ("A film
  that ran on a hidden selector ends by handing the selection out"). Survives.
- **"Underlying order is chaos."** The line is quoted everywhere in the film's reception (and rhymes with
  Rosenbaum's "portrait of chaos," already in §1). Its *use* — as the film voicing the self-organization
  account the chapter must defeat — is fresh. Survives.

it. The residual exposure is small and factual, not conceptual: it lives entirely in §1.2(a)–(b) above.

## Closing

**Verdict, restated: minor revisions** — all ten fixes are line-level; nothing conceptual moves.

**The piece's biggest genuine strength.** Section 4's two-graph analysis — the story world a bare chain,
the narration a star with an off-screen hub, the camera's path welding one into the other — is real
narratology doing real work: it converts Bordwell's balk from an authority problem into the chapter's best
evidence, and this round's insertions complete it beautifully, with the film made to recite its own grammar
(the *Ulysses* bridge) and to voice its own rival (the breakthrough-day chaos monologue) so the argument
can answer a claim the movie itself raises. No other reading of *Slacker* I know does this.

**What only the author can supply.** The disc pass, which now has three specific jobs beyond the standing
two shot-level close readings: eyes on the finale to confirm the Super-8 camera as the source of the last
image (R1 asserts it on the inventory's authority); the breakthrough-day scene's location (R6); and the
clock on the club eviction ("at two" currently rests on the inventory's say-so, not on dialogue). Plus one
library page: Stone's chap. 4 opening (pp. 74–103, 2018 ed.), to see exactly how far his *Ulysses* framing
runs before the chapter's Ulysses sentence goes to print with the new note.
