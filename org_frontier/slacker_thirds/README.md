# Slacker and the structural third — pop-culture chapter

Book chapter for an edited collection on Richard Linklater's *Slacker* (1991). **Abstract accepted**
(see `abstract.md` — the accepted text, verbatim). Full paper due next; **3,000–5,000 words**.

## Thesis (from the accepted abstract)

*Slacker* has no protagonist and almost no repeated characters, yet audiences find it coherent. What holds
it together is a structural third — a position that coordinates people who never directly interact. The film
contains two kinds: a **hosting** third (Austin 1990: walkable downtown, subcultural density, civil
inattention) that supplies shared reference points, and an **engineering** third (Linklater's camera) that
actively brokers each coordination — selecting which face the viewer sees next, when a scene ends, how
handoffs are paced. The formal parallel to algorithmic platforms is specific: Uber's algorithm, like the
camera, selects, ranks, and filters among parties who never negotiate directly. The political economies
differ — cinema engineers for aesthetic ends, platforms for extraction. Cinema trained viewers to read
engineering thirds for a century before platforms industrialized the operation.
