# The Third Nobody Chose: *Slacker*, and a City That Coordinated Without Employing

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

## 1. The third nobody chose

*Slacker* moves about a hundred people through one day in Austin and gives none of them a story. They talk, leave the frame, and do not come back. Most never meet. Nothing returns and nothing converges, and audiences follow it without much trouble. Every published account of why says the same thing, that the camera joins what the characters do not, and then stops. It names the joiner without saying what kind of coordinator a camera is.

Sociology has four answers and only one of them fits. In the chapter of his *Soziologie* that opens the study of the triad, Simmel describes the non-partisan and the mediator, who repairs a relation between two identified parties; the *tertius gaudens*, who profits from standing between them; and *divide et impera*, who sets them against each other to rule both.[^1] All three act on a particular pair. All three know who the two parties are. A fourth appears on that chapter's opening page and is of another kind entirely. Isolated elements, Simmel writes, can be drawn together "by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them," his examples being an alliance against a common enemy and the invisible church. Then he sets it aside mid-paragraph. The group-forming mediation of a third element, he writes, "ist indes in späterem Zusammenhang zu behandeln" — is to be treated in a later context.[^2] This chapter works in that deferral.

The deferred third is the one *Slacker* runs on, and the film shows what it looks like from the first cut. A man off an overnight bus rides into Austin telling his taxi driver about the lives he did not choose, each alternative he turned down at the station having split off to continue somewhere as its own reality. The driver never answers. One cut later a woman lies struck in the street, and the strangers who gather manage the emergency out loud, one calling an ambulance, one reaching her son by telephone, one pressing a business card on a witness: "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it."[^3] Nothing comes of the offer. He leaves before the police arrive, and a bystander supplies the fact the scene has withheld: "Looks like some guy, uh, ran over his mother." Between the taxi and the accident there is no audible link at all. Two strangers who will never learn of each other stand one scene apart, unified by nothing but their common relation to a day, a city, and a camera.

Nothing in the brokerage tradition that grew out of Simmel reaches that. A broker knows both parties and stands between them, and every measure the tradition has built is a property of a node holding a position between two identified others. A sidewalk knows nobody. Neither does a cut. Both coordinate strangers, and neither of them brokers anything.

## 2. A day and a city, without contact

Josiah Royce, writing in 1913, described the same structure from inside it, from the position of the people a third has joined. His question was what makes a community rather than a crowd, and his answer does not require that the members meet. A community of memory is constituted, he writes, by the fact that each member "accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts"; a community of expectation by the same relation to the same future events.[^4] His example is two Maori, each of whom says that he came over in the canoe Tai-Nui. They are a community with respect to that past, and, Royce adds, "Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies."[^5]

Royce also holds that interpretation, the act by which such a community is held together, takes three terms and cannot be built from two. "Interpretation always involves a relation of three terms," he writes. "In the technical phrase, interpretation is a triadic relation. That is, you cannot express any complete process of interpreting by merely naming two terms."[^6] He blocks the obvious repair before anyone offers it: an active synthesis of perception and conception "does not by itself enable us to define a genuinely triadic classification."[^7] Two dyads laid end to end do not make the thing.

Two limits belong here, and both cost the argument something. Royce requires communication among contemporaries, not merely a shared past, so a community can be constituted without its members encountering one another but not without their being in communication at all.[^8] And Royce's interpreter is a mind. Interpretation for him is "a conversation, and not a lonely enterprise," and all three terms of his community are selves.[^9] Putting a camera or a dispatch system in the interpreter's chair is my extension and not his.

Set against that, the film's cleanest join is a join Royce would recognize and nobody in the film intended. In a café a man dictates aphorisms, casting himself as Dostoyevsky and his companion as Anna at work on *The Gambler*, when a passerby breaks in to ask after a friend: "You seen Gary around?" Nobody has seen Gary for months. The passerby moves on, and the second line of the next segment belongs to a different man entirely, standing somewhere else, who says he overheard that a friend has gone missing. The asker asked one man. Another man answered.

Answering costs the answerer the next few minutes, and this film's talkers know it. Emanuel Schegloff, working from tape recordings of calls to a police complaint desk in a middle-sized Midwestern city, found that a summons binds whoever takes it up. The answerer "commits himself to staying with the encounter," and a member of society "may not 'naively choose' not to answer a summons," because silence gets read, as a cold shoulder among other things. Schegloff extended the rule himself from telephones to strangers standing near one another, counting a courtesy phrase and a tap on the shoulder among summonses.[^10] The man who answered keeps his listener through a suppressed moon program, colonies on Mars since 1962, and a rain-forest drug that leaves "perfect employees," and hands him on to nobody. Then he stops a second man on the street with "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing," and releases him with a courtesy of his own: "you're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense." The street supplied the opening twice. It arranged nothing.

## 3. A city that coordinated without employing

That street is doing something specific, and it is easier to name by what it does not do. 1990 Austin held its people within reach of one another and organized none of their work.

The film's own transcript carries the evidence, and it refutes the version of this claim I expected to make. I predicted that the only people working in *Slacker* would be the ones staffing its coordinating positions, and coded the film to test it. Two coders worked all thirty-five segments from the same transcript without sight of each other's sheets, agreeing on thirty-four of thirty-five for work (κ = 0.96) and thirty-three of thirty-five for exchange (κ = 0.91).[^11] The coordinating half of the prediction held: every position that coordinates other people is staffed and audibly working, the taxi, both counters, the door, the staff clearing the room at closing. The exclusivity half failed five times over, and in each case the person working coordinates nothing at all: children selling Cokes at half price, a man working a street with shirts and magazines, a vendor running a two-for-one on newspapers, a store security supervisor handing off a shift, two officers on duty at the accident.

What survived the count is better than what it was asked to confirm. **Nobody in this film looks for a job.** Across all thirty-five segments, in both codings, not one person seeks, applies for, or arranges paid work. The only job search anywhere in the transcript is narrated on a postcard, in the third person, about a housemate who has already left town: "So now he's looking for a job that doesn't involve much work." The one direct question about getting one is deferred to "the true call." And the work that does appear divides in two with nothing between it: staffing a position that coordinates other people, or selling something hand to hand in the street. No workplace appears beyond one store and a few counters, and no employer is named except "the security in the store."

The material conditions were ordinary and they were slack. Across the 1980s the city added housing faster than people, 48.0 percent against 34.6, and that gap on its own shows less than it seems, since households were shrinking nationally over the same years.[^12] Not one multifamily building permit was issued anywhere in the Austin metropolitan area in December 1989, against 632 in Houston and 260 in Dallas that month, although by the following May both of those had themselves dropped to eight.[^13] One rental unit in nine stood empty, a vacancy rate of 11.6 percent, in a city whose university carried 50,245 students that autumn.[^14] Austin was not cheap. On census day 1990 the median contract rent was $346, which put it above Houston and San Antonio and behind only Dallas among the state's big cities.[^15] Slack rather than cheapness is what it had, and slack, in a city where a film could be shot for $23,000,[^16] is what lets a day like this one happen at all.

Two scholars have got here first and both cut against me. Mary Erickson argues that *Slacker* became emblematic of a generation, a city, and a mindset well beyond its observational origins, and that Austin's later transformation drives a nostalgic rereading in which audiences watch the film through what the city lost. Joshua Long treats it as constitutive of Austin's place identity outright: "Set on the streets of late 1980s Austin, *Slacker* came to define the subculture of overeducated, nonparticipant weirdos so prevalent in Austin."[^17] A chapter that ignored them would reproduce exactly the myth-making Erickson has anatomized. But a myth is not an objection to the argument of section 2 when a hundred thousand people accept it as part of their own past, which is that argument's definition of a community of memory.

By platform I mean throughout what this chapter has defined and nothing borrowed from economics: a third that holds parties within reach of one another and arranges none of their meetings. If the film showed its people at work, or if the 1990 figures showed a tight housing market and a production boom, the claim of this section fails.

## 4. The joining migrates

Across its runtime the film changes its mind about who does the joining. Early on it joins its own scenes and the characters barely help: a question about a missing friend, and a report that a housemate named Paul has vanished, closed with an invitation, "Why don't you come look? It's really weird." By the middle the characters have taken the work over, offering lifts and addresses and a van and a stamp and a camera. By dawn it is the cut again, over a recorded voice and a hail into an empty street. That change is measurable, and a second census measures it. On the same transcript and the same protocol, two questions were put to each of the thirty-four transitions between segments: does anything audible cross the seam, and does an act aimed by one character at another produce the next encounter? The two codings agree on 82 percent of the first question (κ = 0.75) and 91 percent of the second (κ = 0.84).[^18] Fifteen or sixteen transitions carry an audible link and four or five are equivocal, with the equivocal cases listed by segment pair in the notes.[^19] The instrument is a transcript of the film's subtitle track, and subtitles compress and paraphrase, so an afternoon with the disc may move these numbers.

Of the first fifteen transitions, four or five carry an audible link. Of the middle fifteen, eleven do. Of the last four, none that the transcript can show. The middle stacks them: a lift into town asked for and granted, an address pressed on a driver who wants no part of the trip, a guest list and a van offered to two women who accept, a stamp offered at one door and claimed again at the next, a camera handed to a stranger at a party.

Six transitions of the thirty-four are deliberate, identically in both codings, and five of those six sit in the middle third. Deliberate means an act aimed at another person that intends the next encounter, whether an offer, an invitation, an address, or an object handed over with a purpose; a character who announces his own next move and goes there alone does not qualify. Notice what the six have in common. Every one of them is an object or a claim that passes between hands and can be said aloud, spelled, demonstrated, copied, or demanded back. "How about a lift back into town?" "Yeah." "The address, man. Take me there. Drop me off." "Do I have a car? I have a van." And at the party, terms attached to a camera: "Shoot whatever you want. Pass it around … But I want my camera back, all right?"

When the characters hold the joining, the instrument is holdable. When the cut holds it, there is nothing to hold and nobody to address. Segments 33 and 34 are address with no addressee, a recorded voice meditating on mourning and freedom, a hail into the street, a man reciting a weapons give-away program to nobody. The cut has the film back, and nothing audible crosses any of the four transitions that carry it there.

Two questions can be asked of any third standing between strangers, and they are worth stating because they do *not* explain this. Neither of them moves while the joining migrates, so neither is what changed. Does the third hold people where they can summon one another without naming which two? Austin does, from the first frame to the last. Does it decide which particular party comes next, for a particular participant, in sequence? The film's cut does, absolutely, since every viewer since 1990 has met these hundred people in the same order.

## 5. What a third can do to you

Simmel gives the structure from outside and Royce the membership from inside, and neither of them says what the third can do to a member. The film answers at a door, and the answer has four parts.

"What's up, dude? I'm on the guest list." The door prices him instead: "Three dollars." He spells his name at the doorman, "The name's Steve. S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four," and gets it handed back: "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you." He argues the one fact he has, that the band on stage is his roommate's. The door does not move. Across the film's last stretch that door exercises four capacities, and they belong on the table by name. A third can price an exchange, evaluate a party, remove a party, and withhold its reason. The fourth is its own power and no version of the other three, because a contested party needs the reason before any argument about the price, the verdict, or the removal can begin.

The door prices, and a cover is named out loud at five dollars. It evaluates, checking a name offered in good faith against a list and refusing it. It removes, clearing the room at two in the morning with the reason said aloud and no destination named: "It's past 2:00. Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere. You can't be here. And I wanna go home." Through all of it the door arranges nothing. It never chooses which two people will meet, and the one deliberate handoff the census records there is a stranger's, not the doorman's. So the concession is unavoidable and it belongs in the body rather than in a reply to a referee. Pricing a passage and expelling a party are powers that owe nothing to selection. Choosing which two parties come together entails no right to charge either of them, and a market square, the least selective third there is, can still charge rent and call the police.

Platforms hold all four in writing. Amazon's licensing page for Prime Video, a living page quoted as retrieved, reads: "Unless otherwise indicated, removed titles (or titles not selected for licensing) may not be resubmitted or appealed. All content is selected at the sole discretion of Prime Video, and we cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not selected for licensing." The same page promises visibility in a rights holder's portal when a title is not selected, so the verdict arrives and the reasons do not.[^20] Evaluation is on the page too: once a title is live, performance metrics and viewing behavior decide which content is resonating, and content failing the engagement standards may be subject to removal. On the labour side the evidence is thinner. Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus surveyed 810 California drivers between April and July 2022, recruited through a union's networks, a convenience sample whose authors caution that people wanting to document a deactivation were likelier to respond; two-thirds reported having been deactivated at some point, and 30 percent of those were given no reason at all.[^21]

David Stark and David Pais put the general form of this plainly: on platforms, actors are co-opted, enrolled in the practices of algorithmic management without managerial authority having been delegated.[^22] The film stages the same enrollment with the forms intact. When the PixelVision camera goes round at the party, the contributor is recruited into someone else's project on stated terms, negotiable, enforced by a man standing there who wants his camera back. The licensing page is that arrangement with the man removed.

One thing the chapter's own apparatus cannot do belongs here too. Where a third stands between two parties who are forbidden each other, you can ask whether it would survive the removal of the prohibition, and a body of formal work has done exactly that.[^23] The deferred third has no such counterfactual, because its neighbours were never forbidden each other. They simply never met.

## 6. A community of memory, and no loyalty in it

Nobody in *Slacker* is devoted to anything, and the film is not shy about it: a man will take a job "when I hear the true call," and will not say what the true call is. Royce's conditions for a community of memory, as it happens, say nothing about devotion. They require selves capable of extending their own lives beyond the present moment, communication among contemporaries, and the same past events accepted by each member as part of his own life and self. A platform meets all three, and it meets them better than a city does, because it keeps the record.

What Royce has that a platform does not is loyalty, which in his account is what makes a shared past *mine* rather than merely true of me. This is a lack and not a swap, and the film's people show the same lack: they share a day and a city, they satisfy the memory conditions, and I claim nothing more for them than that.

Two differences follow, and they are testable. The first is custody. *Slacker*'s day is recountable by the people in it, and the film says so through the man surrounded by running televisions in segment 24, whose complaint about a real death he once witnessed is that it left him nothing to consult: "I have no reference to it now. I can't refer back to it. I can't press rewind. I can't put it on pause." A driver cannot replay an allocation. The offer expires, the queue moves, the fare behind it stays sealed, and the one party who could describe the pairing from the other side is the party he is barred from asking.

The second is whether the arrangement survives a member's indifference. The film's version of an arranged encounter is a guest list, made out loud in front of witnesses, checkable at a door, and it fails in public: "Fuck. I'm not on the list." "Figures." The whole group is left on the sidewalk to renegotiate the evening aloud. A queue position happens to one driver at a time, inside an app, on inputs only partly stated, and it does not require him to care about anything. He remains in it and remains rateable whether or not he assents, which is precisely what a Roycean membership cannot do.

The honest qualification is that loyalty reappears, and it reappears outside. The survey quoted above exists because 810 drivers organized to produce it. That is devotion doing Royce's work, and it forms against the third rather than through it.

## 7. The guest list and the stamp

Two doors apart, on one night, the film stages both sides of a distinction Charles Reich drew in 1964 about a different subject. Reich's subject was government largess, and his observation was that a class of valuables had migrated out of ownership and into grants, leaving the holder facing a counterparty who writes the terms, changes them, adjudicates disputes through its own tribunal, and owes no procedure.[^24] Courts moved first, he noted, where livelihood was at stake, on interests "intimately bound up with the individual's freedom to earn a living."[^25] And in a sentence that names this chapter's fourth capacity sixty years early, he wrote that "the denial of any form of privilege or benefit on the basis of undisclosed reasons should no longer be tolerated."[^26]

The guest list is a grant. It is allocated by a grantor on the grantor's terms, held in the grantor's record, denied on a ground never stated, with no tribunal but the doorman. "I made him put me on the guest list, and I'm on the guest list," says a woman who is not on it. The stamp is property. Outside another door a man hears the cover is five bucks and makes his offer: "But, uh, I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in or something." He demonstrates the transfer, "Here, watch. It's easy. Watch. You just do this," whatever exactly *this* is [DISC:S5], and at the very next door, against a five-dollar cover, the claim is made again by the group he made it to. A rule that can be said can be copied, and copied by strangers who met its owner two minutes ago.

Reich carries the structure of the relation and the doctrinal trigger, and no further. He wrote about government, the extension to private parties is mine, and he pointed at it himself when he listed franchises, equities, and "status in private organizations" as the next frontier and left them.[^27] The programme he opened ran through *Goldberg v. Kelly* and was cut back by *Board of Regents v. Roth*, which located property interests in independent sources rather than in the Constitution. Reich made the question askable. He did not settle it. What the film's coordinating objects give their holders is nearer what Edella Schlager and Elinor Ostrom call a claimant, holding rights of management without exclusion or alienation, than what they call an authorized user, holding access and withdrawal and no say in the rules; their subject is natural-resource regimes and the transfer is analogical.[^28]

The same distinction has a wholesale-finance history, and it dates close to this film. While *Slacker* was in its first run the interdealer currency market was building the venues that would carry it: Reuters Dealing 2000-2 opened in 1992 and EBS launched in September 1993, and in November 1991 the New York Federal Reserve's foreign exchange committee still described all three then-planned systems as under development.[^29] Those venues are third parties in the strict sense, not counterparties. The Bank for International Settlements draws the line itself, classing as indirect any trade involving "a third party in the matching process," an electronic broking platform among its examples, and the venues' own rulebooks say the same from inside: "LSEG is not a party to … Transactions conducted on the Platform."[^30] Liquidity then tipped, as network effects predict, onto one venue per currency pair.[^31]

Two things follow, and the second is the useful one. The first is a limit: these venues did not join parties who would otherwise never have met, since the same dealers went on trading directly alongside them, so they are not instances of the deferred third and I do not claim them as such. The second is that the fourth capacity turns out to depend on nothing structural at all. One firm runs the same matching business under three regulators. Its CFTC-supervised venue owes a removed participant written reasons within fourteen days and a route to reconsideration; its FCA-supervised venue owes none and may act "in the sole discretion" of the operator; its unregulated dealing service offers no reasons, no review, and no appeal.[^32] The capacity to withhold a reason is not a property of standing between people. It is a property of the rules around the standing, which is exactly the difference between a doorman who explains himself and a page that will not.

## 8. Aware and unaware

The film does not anticipate any of this. Nothing in 1990 Austin knew what a dispatch queue was, and a chapter claiming otherwise would be reading the film backwards through the thing it wants to explain.

The claim is about the object and not about the author. The structure is old: named in 1908 and deferred, described from the members' side in 1913, and recorded in 1990 in unusual purity because this particular city supplied encounter without employment and ran its coordination through instruments that visibly changed hands. Call the film pre-qualitative material, meaning material recorded before the category existed, usable as evidence about the category and not as evidence of foresight. The platform is a later instance of the same structure with four capacities attached, and the film is the control case: the same coordinating position with the capacities absent, which is how anyone learns what the capacities do.

Aware, then, in that the film names its own coordination problem in its own words, through parallel lives from the back of a taxi, a man who cannot press rewind on what he saw, an instrument that arrives blind and works anyway, a doorman who states his reason out loud. Unaware in that it has no concept of a third that prices, evaluates, removes, and withholds its reason as a system, and stages each capacity instead as one person's decision at one door, which is why they all stay arguable inside the film.

This bears on a competence the lab that produced this chapter calls algorithmacy: the integrated competence through which an actor coordinates with another party through an algorithmic third party that interprets both sides and commits determinations neither controls, exercised inferentially, translationally, and across time.[^33] The people in *Slacker* need none of it, and their not needing it is the finding. Their thirds are legible. A cover is named aloud, a stamp is demonstrated on a wrist, a card is drawn again if you ask, a list is spelled at the man holding it. What algorithmacy names is what the same person needs when the third stops being sayable. Competence with a third that can be spelled, demonstrated, and drawn again is ordinary social competence, and marking that boundary is what this film is good for.

At the last door the rule arrives out loud, past two o'clock, everybody out, you gotta be somewhere and you can't be here, and the room empties into the street where the film's last talkers are still going. One gate refused them.
