## What the remaining 408 words would cost

Ranked by what I would cut first. Nothing below is redundancy; each is a unit the research pass
specifically added or defended.

1. **§7, the Tzioumakis paragraph (~85 words).** It refutes an argument the chapter no longer makes —
   that the studios closed their specialty divisions and the streamers took over. Cutting it and moving
   the refutation to a note is the cheapest 85 words available. **Risk:** a media-industries referee
   asks why the obvious story goes unaddressed, and the note may not be enough.
2. **§2, the *PNAS* paragraph (~80 words).** Rosenfeld, Thomas and Hausen calling friends and family an
   intermediation in one sentence and "the essential network foci" in the next. It is the section's best
   illustration and not a link in its argument. **Risk:** the novelty claim loses its most vivid
   demonstration and rests entirely on the three-empty-sets warrant.
3. **§5, the Cameron paragraph (~95 words).** Workers reporting that they like the work and find choice
   in it. **Risk:** high. The research pass said to meet this objection head-on rather than absorb it,
   and cutting it makes the section look like it ducked. I would not cut this without instruction.
4. **§6, Münsterberg (~35 words).** The 1916 answer to the section's opening question. **Risk:** the
   section then opens on Benjamin, and loses the point that the training claim is as old as film theory.
5. **§4, the Moretti clause (~35 words).** The graph tradition dropping presentation order on purpose.
   **Risk:** low. The claim survives in the note.
6. **§1, the Sundance paragraph (~75 words).** The counterweight to the premise. **Risk:** high. This is
   the paragraph that keeps §1 honest, and the research pass added it because the old premise was
   refutable in one sentence.

Items 1, 2, 5 and assorted trimming come to about 220. Reaching 5,000 means taking item 3, 4 or 6 as
well.
