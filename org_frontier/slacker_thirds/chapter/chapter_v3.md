# The Setting and the Selector: *Slacker* and Two Ways to Coordinate Strangers

Roger Hunt, Bentley University

## 1. The coherence puzzle

The first face in *Slacker* belongs to the director. Richard Linklater gets off a bus, climbs into a
taxi, and talks the whole ride about a dream in which nothing happened, and about how every road you
don't take splits off and gets lived out by some other version of you. The driver says nothing. Then
Linklater pays, walks a block, passes a woman lying in the road where a car has hit her, and the film
leaves him for good. The end credits list him as "Should have stayed at bus station."[^1]

What the film does next it does about three dozen times. It follows someone until a stranger crosses
the frame, then leaves with the stranger. There is no protagonist. Of the ninety-eight credited roles,
almost none meet twice.[^2] Linklater shot it on 16mm in the summer of 1989 for about $23,000, opened
it at Austin's Dobie Theatre in July 1990 where it ran eleven weeks, and when Orion Classics released
it nationally the following July it took in something over a million dollars on a top of sixteen
screens.[^3]

By every rule of screenwriting I know, a hundred minutes of that should be unfollowable. So I want to
know what people were following.

The reviews support something narrower than the claim usually made for this film. In the notices I have
been able to retrieve — Ebert, Hinson, Howe, Turan, *Variety*, Canby, Jonathan Rosenbaum — no critic,
friendly or hostile, reports losing track of who was on screen or why the film had moved on. The
complaints are boredom, tedium, monotony, pointlessness. The most useful of them is Kenneth Turan's,
because he disliked the film and described its mechanism exactly anyway: you "follow each particular
slacker … for a few seconds or a few minutes until another member of the species wanders somnolently
into the frame."[^4] Hal Hinson, who liked it, put the same observation the other way up: "Linklater
moves from one exchange to the next with miraculous ease… Linklater's control seems all but invisible
here. But this kind of stylistic lucidity can only be the result of determined calculation and
planning."[^5]

That is narrower than "audiences were never lost," and it has to be, because at the Sundance premiere,
John Pierson reports, "the audience broke into thirds: one-third loved it, one-third walked out, and
one-third fell asleep."[^6] People walked out. Walking out on a film you find pointless is a different
act from losing the thread, though, and the complaint Pierson kept from the Dobie run says which one it
was: "The movie does not mean anything."[^7]

Whatever was carrying viewers through was built on purpose. In 1991 Linklater described explaining the
plan to his cameraman and his dolly grip: "They were like 'What?!' And I was like, 'No, no, no. We'll
hold {the viewer's} hand. We'll have these transitional characters. … It won't make any sense on paper,
but will be kind of easy to watch in its own kind of weird way.'"[^8]

Three published readings have already located that hand-holding in the camera. Alison Macor writes that
Linklater replaced *La Ronde*'s narrator with "the fluid camera and a kind of 'baton-passing'," under a
chapter section headed "Chaos by Design."[^9] Jeroen Boom describes a meandering camera "weaving
together the lives of eccentric characters."[^10] Enrique Ramirez has the camera marking "its dérive"
through Austin — a drift, which is movement without a chooser.[^11] The camera's linking work is not a
discovery and I am not claiming it as one. My question is what kind of coordinator a camera is.

## 2. Two ways to coordinate strangers

A third party can coordinate two people who never deal with each other directly. Georg Simmel saw a
century ago what the step from two to three does: add a third position and the social form changes in
kind, because now there can be mediation, coalition, a politics. He catalogued three ways to hold it:
the non-partisan who "produces the concord of two colliding parties," the *tertius gaudens* who profits
from their quarrel, and the third who provokes it.[^12]

On the first page of that chapter he names a fourth kind and puts it aside. "It is sociologically very
significant," he writes, "that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a phenomenon
which lies outside of them" — an alliance of states against a shared enemy, the invisible church that
unites the faithful through their equal relation to one God. He calls that a group-forming mediation,
says it belongs to a later discussion, and gives his reason: here the third stands at such a distance
that no properly sociological interaction takes in all three elements alike.[^13] He saw that not every
third is a broker of pairs, said he would return to it, and wrote the chapter about the other three.

Sociology after him built out the broker. Ronald Burt showed that two people who would each gain from
what the other knows, and who are not connected, are worth money to whoever *stands between*
them.[^14] David Obstfeld described a friendlier version, the third who introduces the two instead of
playing them off.[^15] The field argues about whether the two have to be disconnected to begin with.
What it does not argue about is that they are two identified parties: brokerage happens "when one actor
(the broker) is connected to two other actors (alters)."[^16] The formal apparatus is built on an
ordered triple of named actors, and every measure that came out of it is a property of a point in a
network — which a setting does not have.[^17]

There is another way to hold the position, and Scott Feld named half of it in 1981. A focus, in his
term, is an entity around which joint activities are organised, so that ties form among the people it
gathers as a side effect.[^18] A city is not itself one of these; it is made of them — the coffee shop,
the Drag, the campus. The other half is the thing Lyn Lofland described, the code by which strangers in
a city read each other's appearance and location and know who may be approached where.[^18a] Put the
two together and call the result a setting. **A setting puts people within reach of one another and
supplies the conventions that let one approach another. It arranges no particular meeting.**
Against it, **a selector chooses: which two parties come together, or which one an audience meets
next.** Austin in 1990 is a setting. Linklater's camera is a selector.

Three questions tell them apart. Does it act on everyone at once, or on two particular people? Could
the encounter have happened without it? Does it know who these particular people are, and change what
it does accordingly? Austin acts on the whole street, is one route among many, and reads types at most.
The camera picks one person out of the crowd, is the only way that person reaches you, and knows
nothing about anybody. Uber picks the pair, is the only route to the match, and reads both parties.

These are diagnostics and not a decision procedure, and they presuppose the two definitions above.
They sort thirds that operate where people are already reachable. A third that works to destroy
reachability never enters the field they sort, and Simmel's own case shows why the limit is needed. His
simplest *divide et impera* — the strategy of dividing in order to rule — is a superior who forbids by
general prohibition associations that "are merely possible and very often do not exist even as wishes
of the elements separated by the prohibition."[^19] That superior acts on everyone and knows nothing
about particulars, so on two of the three questions he answers as a setting would, while subtracting
encounters rather than supplying them. He is not a setting. The definitions say so; the questions
cannot, because he is outside what they sort.

One more thing the questions do not do is demote. A selector that answers no to the third question is
still a selector — the camera picks a person out of the crowd and knows nothing whatever about anybody.
That no is not a failure of the test. It is the test telling you which kind of selector you have.

What I am claiming is narrower than it first looks, and it has prior art I should name. Sociologists
have reviewed the setting and the broker together before: Rivera, Soderstrom and Uzzi sort the causes of
tie formation into assortative, relational and proximity mechanisms, with Feld and Burt in the same
reference list.[^20] What they sort by is what makes a tie form. What nobody has asked of both is the
question these three put to them — what does the third know about the two parties it brings together?
The disciplinary separation is real even so, and checkable: three canonical statements, none of which
cites the other's tradition.[^20a]

Two qualifications, both real. Mario Small and Leah Gose call routine organisations brokers outright —
though the conditions they list for successful brokerage are all properties of institutional norms, and
not one requires the third to know which two people are involved.[^21] And Finkel's team draws
something close to my distinction inside a single platform, between a site's access — exposure to
partners you would otherwise not encounter — and its matching, an algorithm selecting partners for
you.[^22] They get there and stop; the distinction stays inside dating.

The cost of leaving it there shows up in *PNAS*. Michael Rosenfeld, Reuben Thomas and Sonia Hausen
report that online dating has displaced friends and family as intermediaries, and name the pattern
disintermediation, "the removal or subordination of the human intermediary between two parties."[^23]
Their own opening paragraph calls friends and family an intermediation in one sentence and "the
essential network foci" in the next, citing Feld.[^24] Feld's foci are not people. And every figure in
the paper measures a human third, which leaves the arrival of a non-human one untouched.

## 3. Austin as setting

A man leaves a coffee shop and a stranger falls into step beside him, delivering an unbroken monologue
about the moon landings that he cannot easily shake. A woman works the sidewalk selling what she swears
is Madonna's pap smear, and gets a hearing. What makes these scenes play as oddly plausible is the
city, and Austin stages neither of them.

The mechanism is not politeness. Erving Goffman's civil inattention — the glance and withdrawal by
which strangers in public leave each other alone — explains why most people on that sidewalk do not
speak.[^25] What licenses the monologue is the opposite rule, and Emanuel Schegloff describes it: a
summons gets an answer, and the sequence it starts is nonterminal, offering no natural exit.[^26] The
moon-landing man exploits that. Schegloff closes the escape route at the other end, too. On a telephone
you can let it ring and be taken for out; on a sidewalk you cannot, because the other person can see
that you heard, so silence stops reading as absence and starts reading as a snub. Both sides of an
opening cost something. That the cost falls harder on the person who wants out is my claim, not his.

The material base was ordinary. Austin overbuilt through the mid-eighties and was still absorbing the
surplus at the end of the decade: housing stock rose 48.0 percent between 1980 and 1990 while
population rose 34.6.[^27] In December 1989 the Austin metropolitan area issued no multifamily building
permits at all, against 632 in Houston and 260 in Dallas.[^28] Austin was not cheap: median contract
rent on census day 1990 was $346, above Houston and San Antonio and below only Dallas among the state's
big cities. What it had was slack. The rental vacancy rate was 11.6 percent, roughly one unit in
nine standing empty,[^29] in a city with 50,245 students at the university in the middle of it.[^30]
Empty rooms and unstructured time are the same fact from two sides, and unstructured time is what this
film is about.

Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga set out what a place like that does for the people in it. The driver is
market size rather than density as such: a larger market improves both the chance of finding a good
match — a job, a band, a room — and the quality of the one you find.[^31] Better odds, and no proposals.
No number attaches to it. Big markets also let firms share suppliers and let workers learn from each
other, and in the data the three effects look alike, so nobody can say how much of the gain is matching.
Giordano Mion and Paolo Naticchioni add a complication: assortative matching, the tendency of like to
pair with like, is negatively related to local market size.[^32]

A setting that supplies conventions about who may approach whom is also supplying conventions about who
may not, and the American law of the street is where those were enforced. Until 1972 a vagrancy
ordinance of the kind struck down in *Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville* reached "poor people,
nonconformists, dissenters, idlers." The Court struck it down for vagueness on two grounds — that it
gave no fair notice, and that it "encourages arbitrary and erratic arrests" — and it is the second that
matters here, because it locates the decision in an officer. Justice Douglas defended wandering and
strolling as "amenities of life as we have known them," conceding in the same breath that they "are not
mentioned in the Constitution or in the Bill of Rights."[^33] A code that reads types and not persons
is no neutral improver of odds. It licenses the white twenty-something pitching Madonna's pap smear on
the Drag, and it has always been available against somebody else. Reuben A. Buford May finds the same
discretion at the nightclub door, in Austin among other cities, fifty years on.[^34]

One thing the city did not do is join the scenes. Brendan Gaughen retraced the film's locations and
found a jump of about three miles — the Old Anarchist's house to Thirty-fifth and Mopac — played across
a cut as though it were a walk. His survey is non-exhaustive and outdoor-only, and he says so, so I
want one instance rather than a pattern.[^35] One instance is enough. Austin put these people into the
same day. It did not decide which of them the film would follow.

## 4. The camera as selector

After the taxi, Linklater's talker walks away and the film hands you a ready-made
plot: a woman lies in the road, run down by a car, and the driver is her son. Matricide, arrest, grief.
A conventional film lives there for ninety minutes; this one gives it about two, then leaves the son
mid-arrest for a bystander who happened to watch. Writing in the *Austin Chronicle* in July 1990, before
the film had left Texas, Chris Walters described the move as it happened: "The camera eye slowly pulls
back until it sees another nameless guy … and follows him upstairs into another story already in
progress." He called the resulting structure a daisy chain, sixteen years before the term entered film
studies.[^36]

Two abandonments in the opening minutes — the director, then a tragedy — teach the rule the rest of the
film runs on. Nobody is owed a return.

What connects one scene to the next is almost nothing. The characters share
no history, no reunions, no coincidences that pay off later. No character introduces another. Pierson's
craft note is right that there is "not a single jump cut, and the passage from one scene to the next is
always in a flow" — he does not mean there are no cuts, since the arrest itself is cut together, but
that no cut breaks the movement.[^37] Almost every transfer is the film following one body until
another crosses it. Strip out every durable tie between characters, which here is easy because there
are none, and nothing in the story world is left to hold scene to scene. What holds them is the film's
decision about whom to follow next.

That decision is constrained. The camera can only leave with someone the city has already put within
reach of the lens. A dispatch system operates on the whole city's queue and waits for nobody to walk
past.

Film scholars have fought over whether a camera can be said to choose anything. David Bordwell's
objection goes further than the one usually attributed to him: he denies not just
the personified narrator but any sender at all, on the ground that film narration "presupposes a
perceiver, but not any sender, of a message." What he grants in the same account is everything I need.
Narration, on his own description, suppresses information, restricts what we know, generates curiosity,
sets a tone.[^38] Seymour Chatman answered him with a narrator already stripped of personality, "the
composite of a large and complex variety of communicating devices."[^39] So the argument is not about
whether a camera has intentions. It is about whether an impersonal system counts as an agent.

Where I stand is not the middle. Mario Slugan reports "little debate on this matter among film scholars
in general," among whom the narrator view predominates and Bordwell is the prominent exception, while
the dispute runs hot among philosophers of film.[^40] So I am siding with a minority inside film
studies and taking the fallback the philosophers offer, that a story "is actually told by the author in
his or her creation of the work."[^41] When I write "the camera," I mean whatever makes those choices.

When Bordwell reaches *Slacker* he cannot fit it into his own category: "Another borderline case, I
think, is *Slacker*. … This isn't a network so much as a wiggly, knotted string."[^42] His word is
string, and the important property of a string is that it has an order. The film runs two shapes at
once. Among the characters it is a bare chain, each one tied only to whoever shares their sidewalk for
a few minutes — the "daisy chain plot" that Charles Ramírez Berg names with *Slacker* as an
example.[^43] In presentation it is an ordered walk: scene one, then scene two, in that sequence,
chosen. No taxonomy in this literature sorts films by how scenes connect in presentation order, and the
graph tradition dropped order deliberately. Franco Moretti says so in as many words: his edges "have no
'direction'," because he "just couldn't find a non-clumsy way to visualize weight and direction," and in
the diagram that results "Time turned into space."[^44]

The camera does not coordinate the characters. The moon-landing man and his captive coordinate each
other; the camera coordinates *me*, serially, with one of them after another. That is a real difference
from Uber, and it is the difference the rest of this turns on, because selectors do two different jobs.
Some choose what an
audience sees next — the camera, and later the landing page of a streaming service. Some choose who
does business with whom — the taxi dispatcher, and later Uber. *Slacker*'s camera is the first kind in
its oldest form: it arranges what reaches you and knows nothing whatever about you.

The strongest account against me is Maria Poulaki's, and it is stranger than it looks. She does not
explain coherence without any coordinating agent — she gives the camera agency directly, writing that
the movement from one story to the next runs on "the self-reflexive agency of the camera."[^45] But her
camera describes rather than chooses, and the sentence is about *Gomorrah*, so I can grant her the
drift and keep the choosing. Her account gives me two further things. What links *Gomorrah*,
on her reading, is "primarily the setting … and the character of Maria" — a setting and a selector,
side by side, inside her own analysis. And she treats character interactions as "primarily
informational," which means the obvious rebuttal, that nobody in *Slacker* meets twice, does not touch
her. Her own floor is the answer: "A catalogue is not a system but a list of elements; a system needs
relations between the elements in order to form itself."[^46] The relations here are the order of the
visits, and somebody chose it.

## 5. The new selectors

Uber does not put drivers and riders within reach of each other and leave them to it. It chooses which
driver gets which ride, and nothing in the design lets the two settle anything between themselves. That
much has been true since researchers first studied the app.[^47] What a decade of subsequent work adds
is a precise account of what a selector can do that a camera cannot, and it comes to three things.

The first is that it prices each match. Uber announced dynamic pricing in London in February 2023 and
completed the rollout across the UK that summer, and an audit built from drivers pooling their own
subject-access data — 1.5 million trips, 258 drivers — found that "the price the passenger pays and the
fee the driver receives vary independently of each other." The driver cannot see the passenger's fare
in the app, and Uber bars him from asking the passenger directly. Across that sample the median take
rate per driver moved from 25 to 29 percent while the mean held at 25, the rise concentrated on
higher-fare trips.[^48] A price computed twice, per match, with the driver barred from learning half of
it.

Surge pricing is the wrong example here. In the economics,
surge is a price attached to a place and a time whose designed effect is to move drivers across a map:
Besbes, Castro and Lobel model a platform that "selects prices for different locations" and find an
optimum that induces overcongestion in some places and prices others out.[^49] That acts on everyone in
a zone, which is what a setting does. All I can say is that surge has not been shown to know who the
two parties are — a model that abstracts away from identity establishes nothing about identity.

The second thing is that it reads the parties. Veena Dubal states flatly what the record supports:
"people who are doing the same work, with the same skill, for the same company, at the same time may
receive different hourly pay."[^50] She goes further, and marks it as inference when she does — the
wage manipulators "appear to be personalized based on what Uber's machine learning systems know about
the habits, practices, and income targets of individual workers." Uber denies it, and Dubal's own
footnote says "We have no way to judge the accuracy of this statement."[^51] I will assert the
differential pay and leave the personalisation where she leaves it. No published engineering account
describes ride-hail dispatch as reading rider or driver identity in order to choose the pair; Uber's
and Lyft's own descriptions run on collective wait time and marketplace value. So the evidence for
reading sits on the pricing side, which is not the same as the pricing power over again. To pay two
people differently you have first to tell them apart. What has no evidence behind it is reading in
order to match.

The third thing is that it can remove a party, and that power has recently been litigated from both
directions. In April 2023 the Amsterdam Court of Appeal held that Uber's deactivation of three drivers
had been taken by automated means: notes written in Kraków by staff whose qualifications Uber never
disclosed, about drivers who were never heard, "in no way show that this act was much more than a
purely symbolic act." For a fourth driver it found the opposite, because Uber stated, uncontested, that
a personal conversation had preceded the decision.[^52] The remedy was disclosure, not reinstatement.
From the other side, in March 2026 a Ninth Circuit panel refused to enjoin Seattle's deactivation
ordinance, holding, over a partial dissent, that "when conduct is nonexpressive, as is the case here,
it is not subject to First Amendment scrutiny."[^53] That is a ruling on a preliminary injunction and
not a final judgment. It is still a court saying that removing a worker from a platform is conduct a
city may regulate.

A camera drops people for good — *Slacker* does it in the first three minutes, twice — and it costs the
dropped party nothing outside the frame. It cannot price your passage and it cannot take you off the
street. The difference has to be drawn in the right place, though, because Lindsey Cameron's seven-year study of
ride-hailing found that "many workers report liking and finding choice while working under algorithmic
management," with the choices made frequent and narrow, and the resulting consent apt to "mask the
more-structurally problematic elements of the work."[^54] Her subject is the management regime — when
to work, which nudges to take, how to game the inputs — rather than the act of matching. So the
contrast is not between a cut and a match. It is between a cut and a regime that prices and removes.

## 6. Cinema as training

Where does a 1991 audience get the skill to follow a hundred minutes of somebody else's choices, and
enjoy it? Hugo Münsterberg thought no skill was needed. In the first book of film psychology he argued
that the photoplay works because it reproduces the mind's own operations — the close-up "has objectified
in our world of perception our mental act of attention."[^55] Nothing on that account has to be
acquired. The measurements have gone against him.

Walter Benjamin put the strong version twenty years later. The function of film, he wrote, "is to train
human beings in the apperceptions and reactions needed to deal with a vast apparatus whose role in
their lives is expanding almost daily."[^56] His bet was hopeful; the training was to produce a public
equal to the machinery. Miriam Hansen keeps that half in view, calling cinema "the single most
inclusive cultural horizon in which the traumatic effects of modernity were reflected, rejected or
disavowed, transmuted or negotiated" — though she bounds herself to 1920s-to-1950s mainstream
Hollywood, and *negotiated* is the last of four verbs in a list that opens with rejected.[^57]

Münsterberg, Benjamin and Hansen between them measured nothing. Sermin Ildirar and Louise Ewing ran the
Kuleshov montages — a neutral face cut against a gravestone, a bowl of soup, a child, the 1920s
demonstration that viewers read an expression into a face that never changes — for people who had never
watched a film. The first-time viewers understood each shot perfectly well and did not connect them: they
"perceived them to be wholly separate from each other and did not relate them spatio-temporally."
Experienced viewers did connect them, though not unanimously: on the gravestone sequence all twenty
made the spatio-temporal link and eleven read sadness into the face, on the soup sequence the figure
was 30 percent, and on the child sequence nobody showed the effect at all.[^58] Eleven of twenty
against none is still the cleanest evidence that reading a cut is learned rather than given.

The same team then complicated their own result. Adding diegetic sound to the clips let first-time
viewers manage transitions that had defeated them in the silent studies, including cuts violating the
180-degree rule, while they still failed on unfamiliar environments.[^59] So the silent studies
establish something narrower than "reading cuts is learned": bridging shots *without an audio scaffold*
is learned.

The best account of what experienced viewers have comes from the same paper, and it names an agent.
They "understand that views are arranged in the service of telling a story, and that in cases where the
story is not initially evident, some effort at problem-solving may be rewarded," and that
problem-solving "likely involves assumptions about something akin to directorial intent."[^60] The
authors propose this rather than show it. Proposed or not, it is the posture exactly: attributing
intent to a hidden arranger, and working to meet it.

On that learned habit filmmakers built a craft aimed at the eye. Tim Smith and John Henderson found
that about a quarter of the cuts joining one view of a scene to the next go unnoticed
altogether.[^61] Lester Loschky and colleagues, tracking gaze through a film clip, found that "the
filmmakers influenced when and where viewers attended, irrespective of their viewing condition."[^62]
Taking a stranger's run of choices for a world went invisible as convention.

*Slacker* takes away the alibi. In an ordinary film the cut serves the plot, so a viewer credits the
plot. Remove the plot and the choosing is the only thing left to notice — Hinson's "miraculous ease" is
a skill meeting a craft.

Whether that skill transfers to reading an algorithm is unknown in a specific sense: nobody has run the
study. Five independent searches turned up no work relating fluency with film editing to comprehension
of a recommender or a dispatch queue, and two field-defining reviews of algorithmic literacy — one
covering 169 studies — contain no occurrence of film, cinema, montage or shot.[^63] The closest
available test found general media literacy and algorithm awareness only inconsistently related in the
same students.[^64] The default expectation here is against transfer, and Tim Smith and colleagues
state it: the benefits of screen experience "may be specific to the domain of movie narrative
comprehension and not transfer," and the trials that would settle it "have not been conducted."[^65]

What workers build instead is not simply the second skill. Heiner Heiland compared what food-delivery
riders believed about two dispatch algorithms against what the algorithms did. On speed, the parameter
they cared most about, the shared theory was wrong — former managers confirmed it and an A/B test bore
it out — and the wrongness disciplined them into riding faster. On proximity to restaurants they were
right, and exploited it. His verdict on the half that was wrong: "these obvious misunderstandings work
in the interest of the platforms and are not corrected by them."[^66] Convergence is uneven, and nobody
has a reason to correct the flattering half.

## 7. Enclosure

Every link in the chain that carried *Slacker* to an audience was a person who could be telephoned.
Pierson, a producer's representative whose job was introducing films to buyers, took it to them. The
Dobie booked it on its own judgment and held it eleven weeks. Orion Classics bought it and released it
nationally on a top of sixteen screens — not a national audience reached but sixteen placements argued
for one at a time, which is the scale to hold in mind before comparing any of this with a recommender.

Address was possible, and refusal was attributable. Both halves matter, because the arguing did not
always work. Linklater and Pierson took the video release to Susan Blodgett, who ran Orion's home video
division, and lost: the company shipped 7,000 cassettes, its foreign-language quantity, against a
tie-in book that sold 20,000.[^67] The film played three months past Orion's Chapter 11 filing that December, so the second
window was starved by a division applying a default rather than killed by the bankruptcy.[^68] One gate
refused them, and it had a name. The earlier gate had responded — a paragraph in *Film Comment* reached
a buyer, who telephoned relatives in Austin to ask whether the Dobie sellouts were real.

What replaced that chain narrows what reaches a viewer twice over, and the two stages are separate.
Cathrin Bengesser, Matthew Hilborn and Jeanette Steemers measured both: 15,584 British titles in
European video-on-demand circulation become 1,165 in Netflix's European catalogues, and inside a
national catalogue the British share of what reaches the landing page is under ten percent.[^69]
Licensing decides what can be found. Prominence decides what is found. Their landing-page figures are
about editorial promotion: they cleared cookies daily and worked from fresh accounts, though they are
careful to add that "it is by no means certain that this can be excluded totally." A page that chooses
what you see next, largely without knowing who you are, is the nearest thing streaming has to
Linklater's camera.

The studios did close their specialty divisions, and Yannis Tzioumakis documents it. What his numbers
refuse is the second half of the easy story, that the streamers then took over. The lowest tier's fall
from 10.22 to 2.11 percent of theatrical box office is, in his phrase, only "seemingly suggesting" a
collapse, because he reclassified the largest earners upward himself; and the tier above it
refilled.[^70] Theatrical distribution changed shape more than it shrank — and *Slacker*'s own position
in 1990, with no other bidder and one modest offer taken on a template, looks more like the present
than like the boom in between.

What changed is what a party is told. Amazon publishes its own account of how titles are licensed for
Prime Video, and two of the three powers are on the page. It reads: "we continuously review performance
metrics and viewing behavior." It removes: titles may be taken down "at any time at the sole discretion
of Prime Video." Pricing the page says nothing about. What it adds instead is a fourth thing the three
questions do not reach. Amazon promises a supplier the verdict — visibility in the portal if a title
isn't selected — and withholds the reasons: "Unless otherwise indicated," a removed title "may not be
resubmitted or appealed," and "we cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or
was not selected for licensing."[^71] That is the modern counterpart of the Orion vice-president who
said no. She said no, and Linklater knew whom to ask.

The questions the film leaves open have answers of unequal quality. When does a setting become a
selector? When its choices stop being one route across a field the parties could cross by themselves,
and start being the only route. What does navigating a selector take? Something a century of cinema did
not supply. What that century taught is a competence at receiving an arrangement — following a cut,
crediting a hidden arranger with intent, not noticing the choosing — and none of it is a competence at
answering back. Heiland's riders put it better than I can. The ones who read the proximity rule
correctly used what they had learned to work harder. Accurate reading produced self-intensification in
that case and obedient error in the other, and contestation in neither. What would make selectors
contestable rather than proprietary I cannot answer, and I am less sure than I was that the century of
training I have been describing is a resource for it rather than part of why it has not happened.

*Slacker*'s politics were mistaken for apathy, and the film headed the charge off itself on one of the
oblique-strategies cards a passerby hands out in it. Its people withdrew from plot, career and
convergence and stayed coordinated anyway — put within reach by a cheap and legible city, and shown to
us one at a time by a camera that took nothing from them except a day, and gave the day back watchable.
Both kinds of coordination do their work in the open here, in a film anyone can watch, which is the
rarest thing about it.
