# Reach and Handoff: *Slacker*'s Thirds

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

## 1. What joins them

A man fresh off an overnight bus rides into Austin telling his taxi driver about the lives he didn't choose. Every alternative that crossed his mind back at the station, he explains, split off at the moment he declined it and continues somewhere as its own reality — a whole parallel life in which a beautiful woman offered him a ride and he moved into her great apartment. The driver never answers him. In the film's next segment a woman lies struck in the street, and the strangers who gather manage the emergency entirely out loud: one calls for an ambulance, one reaches her son by telephone, and one presses his business card on a witness — "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it."[^1] The offer produces nothing; he leaves before the police arrive, and a bystander assembles the story the scene has been withholding: "Looks like some guy, uh, ran over his mother." Nothing audible joins the taxi to the accident. The film simply cuts, and two strangers who will never learn of each other stand a scene apart.

Two segments later a join works, and you can hear it working. In a café a man is dictating aphorisms — he is Dostoyevsky, his companion is Anna, they are writing *The Gambler* — when a passerby interrupts the dictation to ask after a friend: "You seen Gary around?" Nobody has seen Gary for months. The passerby moves on, and the next segment's second line belongs to a different man entirely, a stranger who says he overheard that a friend has gone missing. No seam in the film is cleaner to the ear, and no one made it on purpose — the asker asked one man, and another man answered. What follows the answerer runs about a hundred and thirty lines of the transcript: a suppressed moon program, colonies on Mars since 1962, a rain-forest drug that erases long-term memory and leaves "perfect employees."

Emanuel Schegloff gives that answer its mechanism. Working from tape recordings of calls to a police complaint desk in a middle-sized Midwestern city — and extending the argument himself to strangers in each other's presence — he showed that a summons binds whoever answers it: the answerer "commits himself to staying with the encounter," and a member of society "may not 'naively choose' not to answer a summons," because silence licenses inferences, cold-shouldering among them.[^2] The moon-landing man works the rule from the exploitation side — an opening exploited twice, a handoff made never. His listener secured, he talks until he decides to stop, and then stops a second passerby cold — "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing" — and grants the man his exit himself: "you're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense."

Reach is my name for what the environment is doing underneath all this: it holds people where they can summon one another without naming which two. Density alone won't deliver that, which is what makes the claim falsifiable. Exposure segregation — how rarely people of different incomes actually cross paths — runs 67 percent higher in the ten largest American metropolitan areas than in the smallest,[^3] and at street level the concentration of passersby explains 26 percent of observed social mixing while adjacent amenities, residential diversity, and income levels account for 44.[^4] A big legible city can fail at this. What reach buys beyond "dense downtown" is Schegloff's rule, a mechanism operating at the scale of a single exchange.

And the Austin doing the reaching is the edited one. Brendan Gaughen, walking the locations decades later, found exactly one covert jump — a cut that treats about three miles, the Old Anarchist's place down to a bridge at 35th and Mopac, as one burglar's walk — and he flags his survey as non-exhaustive and outdoor-only.[^5] Austin viewers complained about the geography anyway in 1990, and Linklater's answer conceded the map while keeping the grammar: "If you didn't know Austin, the film makes sense. … They're still walking right to left. Syntactically, it works."[^6] The city of 1990 enters this chapter as a condition the fiction drew on, never as evidence that anything was coordinated.

## 2. The joining migrates

Across its running order the film changes its mind about who does the joining. Of its first fifteen transitions, four or five carry an audible link. Of the middle fifteen — 16→17 through 30→31 — ten or eleven do. Of the last four, none. The early bridges: the missing-friend question at 4→5 and, at 6→7, a report that a housemate named Paul has vanished, closed with an invitation — "Why don't you come look? It's really weird." Then the middle of the film stacks them: a lift into town asked for and granted at 18→19, an address given to a reluctant driver at 23→24, a guest list and a van at 27→28, a stamp at 29→30, a camera handed to a stranger at 30→31 — and then, from 31→32 to the end, nothing audible crossing a seam at all.

Those figures come from a census. The film runs thirty-five segments, thirty-four of them with dialogue, and thirty-four transitions between them. Two coders, each working from the same transcript without sight of the other's work, classified every transition on two questions: does anything audible cross the seam — a line spoken across it, a word or a topic answered on the far side — and does an act aimed by one character at another produce the next encounter? The codings agree on 82 percent of the first question (κ = 0.75) and 91 percent of the second (κ = 0.84).[^7] Fifteen or sixteen transitions carry an audible link; four or five are equivocal. The instrument is a transcript of the film's subtitle track — dialogue only, no speaker names — and subtitles compress and paraphrase, so an afternoon with the disc may move these numbers.

Six transitions, identical in both codings, are deliberate — five of them in the film's middle third. Deliberate means an act aimed at another person that intends the next encounter — an offer, an invitation, an address, an object handed over with a purpose; a character who announces his own next move and goes there alone doesn't qualify. The six, in order: the invitation to come look at Paul's emptied room; the lift into town ("How about a lift back into town?" — "Yeah"); the address pressed on a driver who wants no part of the trip ("The address, man. Take me there. Drop me off"); the guest list and the van offered to two women who accept ("Do I have a car? I have a van"); the stamp offered at one door, demonstrated, and claimed again at the very next one; and the camera handed off at a party — "Shoot whatever you want. Pass it around … But I want my camera back, all right?"

The census's own finding is an asymmetry. No transition in either coding pairs a silent seam with a deliberate act: every handoff anyone in this film intends is also audible where it happens. The reverse is everywhere. Among the seams the codings leave bare, five hold a checkable offer, invitation, or destination the film declines to follow: the ride and the card at the accident, both refused; two exit lines from the pap-smear pitch — a collector named Chico on the east side, Stephanie's Friday guest list — neither pursued; the Video Backpacker's standing "Sure. I'll be here"; a bar errand ("I'll buy this round if you, uh, score me a pack of smokes, man") that dispatches a man the film never follows; and a recommendation to catch *Blow-Up* at 4:00, declined in the dialogue itself. The habit runs wider than the bare seams, too: a couple who miss the start of a movie agree out loud to meet at the next showing, two hours on — "I'll meet you there" — and the film walks off to a bookstore instead, leaving the plan neither kept nor broken on screen. The film hears its offers; it just doesn't take them. And a correction belongs on the record here: I first drafted that list of five with nine seams on it, and rechecking every row against both coders' files left five, which is the number that prints.

Then dawn, and the pattern falsifies itself from inside. Segments 33 and 34 are address with no addressee — a recorded voice meditating on mourning and freedom, a hail into the street ("Wake up, America"), a man ranting a weapons give-away program at nobody — and the four transitions that carry them carry nothing audible at all: the cut has the film back.

Five equivocal seams stay equivocal, each one the disc could settle. At 5→6, the line at the seam falls on the far side of my segmentation's boundary while its own notes treat it as the earlier scene's closing beat, and a transcript without speaker names can't assign it [DISC:T2]. At 12→13, the reply at the seam answers a line the transcript never carries [DISC:T3]. At 13→14, "They're back" names no referent anywhere in the dialogue [DISC:T4]. At 22→23, an object changes hands unnamed — if it's the rare Marquis de Sade the burglars admire two lines later, the seam is a bridge; if not, it's bare — and this single case is what makes the figure a band instead of a number [DISC:T6]. At 34→35 the transcript simply ends: the final sequence has no dialogue, so this instrument can show neither a link nor its absence [DISC:T11]. Three pairs of segments also share overlapping line ranges, and several boundaries fall inside continuous exchanges, so a few codes describe where I drew a seam rather than where the film cut; they stay in the count, flagged, because deleting any of them would manufacture the point estimate the band exists to refuse. Two of the film's neatest repetitions — a cigarette request doubled across 19→20, the stamp claimed at two consecutive doors — rest on exact subtitle wording, and I argue from neither [DISC:T5].

## 3. Two questions, four cells

Back on the sidewalk in segment 8, a man recognizes Stephanie, just back from a hospital stay in Dallas, and gets an offer inside two minutes of talk: her band plays Friday, and "If you wanna come, I can put you on the list." A third arrives mid-story — an old man came down the freeway from San Antonio at a hundred miles an hour, waving a gun and laughing, chased by squad cars, dead by his own hand on the median — and pivots straight from it to a lab slide he calls a Madonna pap smear: "Do you think maybe, uh, you'd be interested in buying something like this?" Nobody buys, and he takes the refusal in character: "It's a material world and I'm a material girl." Two questions come off that sidewalk. Does a third party hold people where they can summon each other, without naming which two? That's reach, and 1990 Austin as the film constructs it answers yes. Does a third party decide which specific party comes next, for a specific participant, in sequence? That's handoff, and the sidewalk answers no — everything in the scene is addressed to whoever happens to be standing there, and no one assigns anyone. The two questions are independent, so the cases fall into four cells. The club door late in the film answers no to both: it subtracts encounters and arranges none. The film's own cut answers no and yes — it holds nobody in talking range, and it hands its viewer a specific next scene, every time. Uber's dispatch answers the same way, over people who then have to live with the pairing. So does Amazon's submissions page, over films. Four cells, not two kinds of third.

Handoff also comes in degrees, which is why a cleaner test — does representation select, or doesn't it — would miss the only thing here worth measuring. This film's own degree swings across its runtime: four or five audible links in the first fifteen seams, ten or eleven in the next fifteen, none in the last four. And a residual stays open once the sorting is done. The cut, the dispatch queue, and the submissions page share a cell, and the sorting by itself says nothing about what else each of them can do to the parties it sequences. Sorting a third tells you where it sits. It doesn't tell you what it holds.

What the characters arrange, the film sometimes honours. The invitation and the van in segment 27 produce segment 28's ride — one of the six deliberate handoffs, kept exactly as spoken. Then the door refuses the very name the arrangement was made in: "The name's Steve. S-T-E-V-E." — "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you." An arrangement and its outcome are two different claims, and this film keeps them apart with something close to rigor: the ride happens because someone said it would, and the entry fails although someone said it wouldn't.

The distinction has ancestry, and some of the ancestry pushes back. Simmel, whose triad opened this line of thought, set the question aside in the middle of drawing it: elements unified "by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them" — his examples are an alliance against a common enemy and the invisible church — are, he writes, a mediating function "to be discussed in a later context."[^8] He promised a return, and the promise marks the gap this chapter works in. The German *Theorien des Dritten* debate names a related pair, which I report at second hand: *konkreter* versus *abstrakter Dritter*, the concrete third who stands between identified parties and the abstract third who never appears.[^9] The platform literature mostly runs the other way. Meyer and colleagues — I cite their published abstract, no more — divide matching mechanisms from attention mechanisms and find both "coexist on most platforms";[^10] O'Reilly, Strauss, and Mazzucato — whom I also know only at second hand — fold everything into one function: each dominant platform is "an attention gatekeeper of one kind or another."[^11] That unification is the strongest complication on record, and the grid concedes it by construction. The axes are independent, so a single platform can occupy more than one cell at once. What the grid refuses is only the inference from one cell to another — from where a third sits to what it can do.

## 4. What a third can do to you

"What's up, dude? I'm on the guest list." The door's answer prices him instead: "Three dollars." He spells his own name at the doorman — "The name's Steve. S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four" — and gets it handed back: "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you." He argues the one fact he has, that the band on stage is his roommate's; the door doesn't move; he stays outside, telling a companion he might hang out, might go in. Four capacities are on display around that door across the film's last stretch, and I want them on the table by name: a third can price an exchange, evaluate a party, remove a party, and withhold the reason. The fourth is its own power and no version of the other three, because a contested party needs the reason first, before any argument about the price, the verdict, or the removal can begin. None of the four follows from choosing which two parties meet.

Work the door through the register. It prices: a cover is named out loud at five dollars, at the door in segment 29 and again at the head of segment 30. It evaluates: a name offered in good faith is checked against a list and refused. It removes: at two in the morning the room is cleared with the reason said aloud and no destination named — "It's past 2:00. Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere. You can't be here. And I wanna go home." And through all of it the door arranges nothing; it never chooses which two people will meet, and the census records no deliberate handoff anywhere near it. So a concession is unavoidable, and it belongs here in the body rather than in a reply to a referee: pricing a passage and expelling a party are powers that owe nothing to selection — the register cuts across the grid, which is why the two stay separate. Choosing which two parties come together entails no right to charge either of them, and a market square — the least selective third there is — can still charge rent and call the police.

The platforms hold the fourth capacity in writing. Amazon's licensing page for Prime Video — a living page, quoted as retrieved — reads: "Unless otherwise indicated, removed titles (or titles not selected for licensing) may not be resubmitted or appealed. All content is selected at the sole discretion of Prime Video, and we cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not selected for licensing." The same page promises visibility in a rights holder's portal when a title isn't selected, so the verdict arrives; the reasons don't.[^12] Evaluation is on the page too: once a title is live, "performance metrics and viewing behavior" — buy and rent transactions, unique customer streams, total hours — decide which content "is resonating," and content that fails the engagement standards "may be subject to removal." Price, verdict, removal, and a stated policy of unstated reasons, all four capacities in one document. On Uber's side the evidence is narrower than the folklore, and I'll keep it that way: in a survey of 810 California drivers recruited through a drivers' union's networks between April and July 2022 — a convenience sample, whose own authors caution that people wanting to document a deactivation were likelier to respond — two-thirds reported having been deactivated at some point, temporarily or permanently, and 30 percent of those were given no reason at all.[^13]

The film's cut is the boundary case, because a cut never explains itself either, and a viewer has no decision to appeal. The difference sits in what can be revisited. The Video Backpacker, surrounded by his running televisions in segment 24, complains that a real death he once witnessed left him nothing to consult — "I have no reference to it now. I can't refer back to it. I can't press rewind. I can't put it on pause." A recorded image can be looked at again, which is what an appeal would need. And the doorman, unlike the cut, explains — the hour, the list, his own wish to go home — and explaining is exactly what makes him arguable.

## 5. The queue and the cut

An Uber driver waiting for airport work joins a queue and watches a number. The app shows a dynamic estimate of the wait until the next offer, updated on three stated inputs — how many riders are requesting trips, how many drivers are ahead, recent flight arrivals and cancellations — and the company's own help page names position as "a priority factor for trip matches."[^14] Position is also the lever. Refusing a request outright, or cancelling one after accepting it, "could result in your spot in the queue resetting"; a rider's own cancellation doesn't cost the driver his spot. And the exchange the queue delivers is priced around him: since early 2023 in London, the fare a passenger pays and the fee a driver receives vary independently, the passenger's number never appears in the driver's app, and Uber bars him from asking for it — findings from an audit of 1.5 million trips donated by 258 UK drivers, a self-selected sample the researchers describe as exactly that.[^15]

Set the queue beside the cut and the handoff question can't tell them apart: each hands a specific party the specific next thing, in sequence, no negotiation. The capacities differ. The queue prices the exchange it arranges; the cut hands a viewer the next scene and prices nothing. The film's own version of an arranged next party is the guest list — "I made him put me on the guest list, and I'm on the guest list" — an arrangement with a name on it, made out loud in front of witnesses, checkable at the door, and lost in public when the check fails. Everything about it can be seen by everyone standing there, and when it fails, it fails in public too — "Fuck. I'm not on the list." "Figures." — with the whole group left on the sidewalk to renegotiate the evening out loud. The queue's arrangements happen to one driver at a time, inside an app, on inputs only partly stated, and its failures arrive the same way.

Enclosure is a strong word, and I rest it on a single dated pair. When Roderik Smits did his fieldwork on film-distribution gatekeeping in 2017, Amazon's route stood open: rights holders could submit films directly, and the process was "unfiltered, with no gatekeeping restrictions to making such films available."[^16] The same company's page, retrieved 1 August 2026, reads: "Access to self-service submissions is by invitation only. We are not currently accepting new content submissions for rent or buy from content providers that do not already have an account."[^17] One channel tightened, in writing, inside a decade — and it's the channel a film shot for $23,000, as *Slacker* famously was, would have used.[^18]

The counter-evidence deserves its own sentences. In domestic theatrical distribution the middle tier — the specialty distributors sitting between the major studios and the smallest outfits — refilled: the "late indies" of 2009–2019 averaged 6.46 percent of the market against the 6.91 percent the vanished studio specialty divisions had held in 1998–2008, even as the surviving divisions themselves fell to 3.11.[^19] And Waldfogel — on his published abstract, at second hand, since I haven't opened the body — documents "substantial growth in the number of movies brought to market" in the digital era.[^20] So no general gate slammed on independent film. The claim narrows to one named channel on one dated page, and it stays there.

What the cut allows that the queue doesn't is re-inspection, and the film states the point itself. The Video Backpacker's whole case for the recorded image over the actual event is that the event can't be consulted afterward — no rewind, no pause, no slow motion for "all the little details." A film, whatever else its cut withholds, can be watched again; the handoffs this chapter counts are countable because anyone with the disc can recount them. A driver can't replay an allocation. The offer expires, the queue moves, the fare behind it stays sealed, and the one party who could describe the pairing from the other side is the party he's barred from asking. That gap between the two — replayable against sealed — is a capacity difference, and nothing in the cell they share predicted it.

## 6. What cuts taught

The film's first rhyme belongs to nobody in it. Riding in from the bus, the taxi passenger muses that in dreams "there's always someone getting run over or something really weird"; twenty-nine lines into the next segment, a bystander at the accident works out that the driver ran over his own mother. Both codings record the echo, and both record its agency as none: no character makes the connection, notices it, or acts on it — the man who said the line has left the film for good before the line comes true. His other idle image lands the same way: "you're probably giving someone else a ride, you know?" he tells the driver, and one segment later a stranger is offering a ride at the roadside. The joining here is entirely the film's. The following of it is entirely the viewer's, and following it is a skill with a measurable edge.

The measurements exist, and they're narrower than the folklore about a trained century of viewers. Sermin Ildirar and Louise Ewing showed edited sequences to forty adults from mountain villages south of Isparta, Turkey, a district only recently connected to the electrical grid: twenty with no prior experience of film or television, twenty from nearby with some television — a younger group with more schooling, in a small, elderly, precisely described sample, and every one of those qualifiers matters. On the gravestone sequence, all twenty experienced viewers linked the shots in space and time, and eleven of them — 55 percent — read the man's face as sad: the classic Kuleshov reading. On the child sequence, none showed the effect at all. The twenty first-time viewers, able to describe every shot, "perceived them to be wholly separate from each other."[^21] In a companion study, sound helped: dialogue and salient noise carrying across a cut let first-time viewers connect shots, though not always, and one unfamiliar sequence integrated for none of them.[^22] Shot-joining is learned, cheaply and incompletely, and the audible link is the easiest join there is. A census of what can be heard at this film's seams is a census of its lowest rungs.

The film carries its own theory of all this, in a bar. Two people in segment 26 argue that Saturday-morning cartoons train children: Scooby-Doo runs on bribery — beat the bad guy, get a Scooby Snack, hold out for two — "and they're teaching kids fuckin' bribery"; the Smurfs teach the colony, where anyone who "tries to take off and do their own little individual trip" gets stomped by Mr. Evil. A third drinker offers a friend's theory that Smurfs are blue to get children used to seeing blue people, for Krishna's arrival. Six segments later a British photographer is needled with a recommendation — "you being from London and being a photographer and all, you might wanna watch Blow Up tonight at 4:00" — and declines the whole medium before defining it flatly: she's into photography, and "movies are photography, 24 times a second." The film treats all of it as talk — media theory at the same table as the Elvis sighting — and commits to none of it.

Does the trained competence generalize? The one study on record that asks reports that the evidence "does not support the hypothesis" that more exposure to faster-cut movies, television, or touchscreens brings a general IQ advantage, and offers — introduced in its own words as "One explanation could be" — that the benefit "may be specific to the domain of movie narrative comprehension."[^23] That's what was measured, and I'll stop where it stops.

The strongest positive case for a competent audience is this film's own, and it cuts a precise shape. In 1990, Austin viewers audited the handoffs against a city they knew and told Linklater to his face: "It doesn't make sense!" — they'd caught characters rounding a corner from Sound Exchange straight into Half Price Books.[^24] His defense, quoted already, was syntax over geography: not knowing Austin, you'd never notice; they're still walking right to left. Those viewers contested a handoff's content, line by line, corner by corner. Whether an audience trained on a century of cuts can contest the order itself — why this stranger next and not that one — is a different competence, and nothing measured shows it.

## 7. Instruments you hand on

A card changes hands on the street. The woman handing them out in segment 25 attaches no instruction and no reason — "No two cards are alike. Think about whatever you want and take a card" — and a passerby takes one and, asked which he got, reads out the line the film is best remembered by:

> "Withdrawing in disgust is not the same thing as apathy."[^25]

Another man at the bar takes his card — "It's not building a wall, but making a brick" — as confirmation of the "total recalibration" he's been announcing all day: "There is no structure. The underlying order is chaos," he declares, and rides the card straight into quantum physics and the butterfly in Galveston.[^26] The instrument arrives blind and works anyway, because it can be read aloud, asked about, drawn again — "Could I have another one? Is that allowed?" — and handed to the next table.

The stamp is the same kind of object with a cover charge attached. Outside one door a man hears the cover is five bucks and makes his offer: "But, uh, I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in or something." He demonstrates the transfer — "Here, watch. It's easy. Watch. You just do this" — whatever exactly *this* is [DISC:S5] — and at the very next door, against a five-dollar cover, the claim is made again by the group he made it to. A rule that can be said can be copied, and copied by strangers who met its owner two minutes ago.

The camera is the instrument at its most explicit. At the party in segment 30 a man names it aloud — "it's my PixelVision camera" — and gives it away with terms attached: shoot whatever you want, pass it around, give it back. Ten segments earlier the film held the mirror case: a camera already inside the film, interviewing a man on the street who refuses its terms — he'll take a job "when I hear the true call," he won't say what the true call is, and he closes the frame himself: "End of interview." An instrument you can hold can also be refused out loud.

That's where the chapter's open question lands, and it isn't the one I expected to end on. The obvious question — does the coordinating third explain itself? — turns out to sort almost nothing, because a cut never explains itself and neither does a deactivation, and only one of the two feels like an injustice. The usable question is about the instruments. The club's rule is sayable and its stamp is copyable; the guest list has a name on it that can be spelled at a doorman. A queue position can't be held, read, or passed to someone else, and the reason for a removal is, in writing, the thing not provided. What would make the new engineering thirds — the platforms that broker each match — contestable is what the film's coordinating objects already have: a form that can change hands. At the door in segment 31 the rule arrives out loud — past 2:00, everybody out, you gotta be somewhere and you can't be here — and the room empties into the street, where the film's last talkers are still going. One gate refused them.
