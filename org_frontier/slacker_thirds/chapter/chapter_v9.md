# 'You seen Gary around?': The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

#### Roger Hunt, Bentley University

This chapter sets out to read Richard Linklater's *Slacker* (1990) as a film about a third that holds strangers available to one another without arranging who meets whom. The story, such as it is, is one day in Austin, Texas, among roughly a hundred people: someone talks for a stretch and is gone, and the next face is whoever the camera finds, with no plot that collects them and no character who walks us from one conversation to the next. The twin aspects of that day form the basis of this chapter's investigations: the city that keeps people in one another's way, which I will call reach, and the camera that chooses which of them we see next, which I will call selection. I have chosen the film for that pairing. It stages in the open, with everyone still able to answer for themselves, an arrangement we now meet behind a screen, and where another critic might find nostalgia in a film so fond of its city, Linklater keeps a cold eye on what that city does to the people walking through it.

What the city is doing to them was already thinkable, in pieces, before anyone shot a frame of it, and I hope to show where it had been thought. Georg Simmel, in 1908, saw that isolated people can be unified by their common relation to something lying outside them, a third standing at such a distance that it never comes between an identified pair; he called the insight sociologically significant, and then postponed its treatment to a later context. Josiah Royce, five years afterwards, asked when strangers count as members of a community, and answered that a shared relation to an outside past counts only when each person accepts that past as part of his own life, which members may do while keeping aloof from one another or even while enemies. Charles Reich, in 1964, described access held not as property but as a grant, issued by a grantor who writes the terms and can revoke them. What Linklater shoots is a day in which all three problems remain visible on the sidewalk: a city that unifies without introducing, a shared day nobody claims as his own, and access still spoken aloud to a person who can be argued with.

Those three problems bring three bodies of thought with them, and I want to name them at the outset. First, and most obviously, the sociology of the third party, from Simmel's triad chapter through the German *Theorien des Dritten* that took up his figure and the network-brokerage research that did not. Second, Royce's conditions for membership among people who never meet, which turn out to be considerably more demanding than the slogan they are usually reduced to. Third, Reich's account of the grant, which supplies the legal form of a dependence the film keeps putting on screen. Against these I will set a later vocabulary: David Stark and Ivana Pais, writing in 2020, describe platform owners who co-opt providers and users, enrolling them in algorithmic management without anyone having delegated managerial authority to them.[^1] Nobody had that description in 1990, and their terms help us see what the film already shows — contribution without a contract, and a third that still has a face.

That face is where I want the argument to stay, because it is what the film keeps and the platform removes. A third with a face can be argued with, and the day in Austin is full of people arguing with one: spelling a name at a door, copying a stamp, saying a handoff out loud. Fluency of that kind, with determinations that arrive without reasons and without anyone to answer for them, is what I call algorithmacy, and the film is where the skill it names can still be watched working. Naming what its loss costs is the argument this chapter is finally making.

Staying with that face also means steering away from one line the material invites, and the omission is deliberate enough to be worth stating. It would be easy, and it would be wrong, to read *Slacker*'s scattered form as an effect of the network technologies that arrived after it, as though the film were somehow prescient about the internet. David Bordwell has argued directly against that inference: network narratives long predate network technology, and their form answers to storytelling problems rather than to the shape of contemporary infrastructure.[^2] I take his objection as binding. My claim is not that Linklater anticipated platforms, nor that anything in Austin's technical life in 1990 caused the film's construction. It is the narrower claim that a coordination form which later hardened into software was, for one afternoon in Texas, still being performed by people on a street, and can therefore be watched.

What can be watched, though, is limited by what I could get hold of, and a word is owed about that and about my own position. I come to this film from organization theory, not from film studies, so my interest falls on the arrangement the film makes visible more than on its place in American independent cinema. I had hoped to work from a published screenplay. Alas, no official script for *Slacker* has been published, and the dialogue quoted throughout this chapter comes from the film's subtitle track, which compresses and paraphrases and which I have not yet been able to confirm against the Criterion disc.[^3] Every quotation below should be treated as subtitle-derived until that check is made.

## Philosophical Origins in Simmel's Postponement and Royce's Canoe

Before turning to the film, and to establish that the form was named and abandoned long before anyone put it on screen, I present two brief case studies from outside cinema. Both are moments at which a writer saw the arrangement clearly, said so, and then declined or failed to develop it, which is precisely the condition that leaves it available for a filmmaker to stage. Simmel's postponement is what the taxi ride and the struck woman will turn out to enact between them; Royce's canoe is the standard that the question about Gary will turn out to fail.

Simmel's triad chapter is famous for three figures, and none of them is mine. The non-partisan and the mediator repair a relation between two identified parties; the *tertius gaudens*, the laughing third, profits by standing between them; *divide et impera*, divide and rule, sets them against one another in order to rule both.[^4] Each requires the third to know which pair it stands between. On the opening page of that same chapter, however, Simmel writes a sentence he then refuses to follow, and it is a different kind of third entirely. Kurt Wolff translates it as the observation that "it is sociologically very significant that isolated elements are unified by their common relation to a phenomenon which lies outside of them." The examples offered are an alliance against a common enemy and the invisible church, which joins all believers through their equal relation to the one God. Then comes the postponement: "The group-forming, mediating function of a third element will be discussed in a later context." The German is *Diese gesellschaftsbildende Vermittlung eines dritten Elementes ist indes in späterem Zusammenhang zu behandeln*.[^5]

His reason is distance. The third here stands at such a *Distanz* from the other two that no properly sociological interaction encompasses all three alike. What remains are *Zweierkonfigurationen*, pairs: either the relation among those who join, or the relation between them as a unit and the outside centre of interest. God and two believers do not constitute one three-way field, and neither do two allies and a far enemy. Simmel's chapter wants three elements close enough to form a group, so the distant case falls outside its concern and he sets it aside. I will call what he set aside the deferred third. Austin holds precisely that shape, and holds it for ninety minutes: the man in from the bus station and the people standing around a woman struck in the street occupy one city and no relation whatever to one another.

Whether he ever came back to it is a question I had assumed answered, and I was wrong. The phrase itself occurs exactly once in the 1908 *Soziologie*, at the postponement. The theme, however, returns some two hundred pages later, in the chapter on conflict, where the very *Distanz* that had been his ground for deferring becomes a positive thesis: a certain distance between the elements to be united and the point that unites them turns out to be an especially favourable constellation for the bond. Both of the original examples come back on that page — the league formed against a common enemy, and the God whose infinite remoteness is exactly what lets him gather the most heterogeneous peoples into one church. Simmel indexed the passage himself, under *Distanz des vereinigenden Momentes*, and the alliance half recurs a dozen pages earlier still.[^6] So the promise was not simply forgotten. What never returns is the third *as a third*: the theme comes back in the dyadic register the postponement had itself predicted, unification described from the side of the parties who are unified, with the distant unifying element analysed as a condition of their bond rather than as an element of a triad. That failure is the one the film inherits, and it is why the cut from the taxi to the hit-and-run can carry the whole idea without a word of explanation: what is on screen is two parties and a city, never three terms in one field.

That inheritance has since acquired names, supplied by readers working on the figure Simmel dropped. Gesa Lindemann theorises an absent third whose presence is the precondition of any durable dyadic order, not an addition to it; Jan Fuhse distinguishes the abstract or generalised third from the concrete third that network research is equipped to observe; Christoph Neuberger has applied *gesellschaftsbildende Vermittlung* directly to journalism and platforms as mediating thirds.[^7] The strongest correction this literature offers is also the most useful to me. Where the chapter's own instinct is to imagine a pair to which a third is subsequently added, Albrecht Koschorke's account, with Michel Serres behind it, runs the other way: the third comes first, and the dyad is what a relation looks like once a third has made it possible.[^8] On that reading Simmel's postponement is not a small omission at the edge of his subject. It is the omission of the ground.

Royce's case is the second, and it fails differently. In *The Problem of Christianity* he asks what makes a community out of people who need not have met, and answers with a definition, not a metaphor: a community of memory takes shape when each of its members "accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same past events that each of his fellow-members accepts," and a community of expectation by the same relation to shared future events.[^9] His illustration is two Maori, each of whom says that he came over in the canoe Tai-Nui, and the illustration is chosen precisely for its coldness — they form a community on that shared past even though, as Royce adds, "Each may keep aloof from the other. They may be enemies."[^10] The line is quoted often, and quoted alone it produces the slogan that Royce licensed membership without meeting.

He did not, quite, and the qualification matters for everything that follows. Royce lists three conditions and the scholarship reads them as cumulative, not as a menu: the self's power to extend itself ideally in time, a plurality of distinct selves capable of social communication and in general engaged in it, and at least one identical event included in the ideally extended lives of all.[^11] The second condition is a gate on the existence of any community whatever, and Royce frames membership as holding among contemporaries. More constraining still, acceptance is written into the definition itself — the member must accept the past event as part of his own life — so there is no stage at which the conditions are met and the taking-as-mine is still outstanding. Royce's membership waits for the member's yes. Aloofness he permits, and enmity, and the absence of any meeting; what he does not permit is a community of people who have not each, individually, claimed the same past. That is a demanding standard, and a day in a city is not obviously going to meet it. When a stranger walks into a café on the Drag and asks whether anyone has seen Gary, the room he is asking will turn out to fail every part of it.

## 'You seen Gary around?' – The Deferred Third of *Slacker*

The film opens by putting the deferred third on a street twice within four minutes. Linklater himself, credited only as the man who should have stayed at bus station, rides in from an overnight coach and talks at a silent driver. His subject is a dream in which nothing happened, and then a theory that every unchosen path continues somewhere as its own reality. At the station he had almost skipped the cab, and in that other life a woman picks him up and he moves in with her. The driver never answers and he never seems to notice. He gets out on "Man, shit. I should've stayed at the bus station."

Then the film is on a different street, where a woman lies struck and a jogger jogs in place and barks at no one to touch her. Somebody goes for help. A man who has pulled over asks whether he is needed, hears that he is not, and presses his card on the jogger anyway — "Take my card. Give me a call. … I'd like to hear from you. I mean it" — before driving off ahead of the police. The camera then leaves the body entirely and follows the driver responsible into a house, where officers take him away for running over his mother.

No character bridges those beats, and nothing in the story connects them; Austin contains them both, and the cut decides which of them we watch. In the taxi the man had said there is always someone getting run over, and the next thing we see is a son who has run over his mother, an echo that no one on screen is in a position to notice. That echo is the arrangement I want to name: a third that holds strangers available to one another and arranges not one of their meetings.

Down at the Blue Bayou on South Congress, meanwhile, Reich's problem arrives in a form anyone can recognise. A man credited as S-T-E-V-E With A Van has spent the afternoon telling a friend about the night ahead — his roommate's band, his van, a guest list he had made the roommate write — and at the curb he claims the club keeps a spot for him because he is "tight" with them. The doorman charges him three dollars. He spells his way onto a list he believes he owns, "S-T-E-V-E. Steve plus three or four," and is refused on it.

> "Sorry, Steve. Don't see you."
>
> "Fuck. I'm not on the list."
>
> "Figures."

Steve has been refused without a reason, and the refusal has a shape that Reich had described twenty-six years earlier. Wealth of the modern kind, he argued, is increasingly held not as property but as largess: a grant, whose granting, regulation and revocation proceed "by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient."[^12] The Blue Bayou's door is that arrangement in miniature. It sets the price, keeps the record, decides the case in its own forum and owes nobody an explanation, and Reich's diagnosis of the form covers all four without strain. I lean on the diagnosis rather than on his remedy deliberately. Reich also demanded, in his closing pages, that denial on undisclosed grounds "should no longer be tolerated," and it is tempting to make that sentence the centre of the argument; but the reception has never treated it as the centre, and William Van Alstyne, the article's most careful early critic, twice characterises those closing pages as tentative suggestion and mild conjecture, well short of the article's load-bearing work.[^13] What Reich gave the law was the description of the dependence. The demand was a hope appended to it.

What the film adds to Reich is the face. Outside the same door, a man offers a hand stamp — "I got a stamp. I think I can, you know, get you in" — and copies it wrist to wrist, over an objection ("No, man. I don't want him licking my arm") that does not stop anyone, and the two of them walk in free.[^3] They can copy the stamp because a stamp is property, showable and transferable between strangers; the guest list was a grant, and only the door's own record could confirm it. Later at closing someone says the rule aloud and empties the room with it — "It's past 2:00. Everybody out. … You gotta be somewhere. You can't be here. And I wanna go home" — and even that peremptory sentence is addressed to people who could in principle argue back. The platforms that inherit this arrangement keep the charge, the list, the refusal and the withheld reason, and drop both the person who can state the rule and the stamp that strangers could copy among themselves.

Royce's problem arrives, appropriately, as a question nobody can answer. In a café later identified as Captain Quackenbush's on the Drag, a passerby breaks in on the room to ask after a man who is not in it.

> "You seen Gary around?"

Nobody has seen him for months, and the asker leaves without an answer. Outside, a man who has overheard latches onto him with suppressed moon transmissions, Mars since '62, a rain-forest drug that produces "perfect employees," and the suggestion that Gary may already have been recruited; the asker heads off toward a friend's place, and the talker waits behind to be sure they were not followed before stopping a second stranger with "Just a little security check. There've been some people missing" and then waving him along — "You're in a hurry. That makes perfect sense." Gary never appears. The sidewalk had held both men within reach of one another and the cut selected their meeting, and no one introduced them.

Test that pair against Royce's conditions and the result is instructive. They are contemporaries, and they are in communication, so the second condition is satisfied on the street in a way it is not satisfied for most of the people the film shows us. What fails is the third and the acceptance built into it: neither man takes Gary's absence into his own life as the same past event. The asker is looking for a friend, the talker converts the absence into evidence for a conspiracy, and the day they share remains a day neither of them claims. Sharing a city, it turns out, is not sharing a past. This is what membership looks like under a deferred third — or rather, this is what the absence of membership looks like, since on Royce's own terms these people are not members of anything, and the honest description is that Austin holds them and claims none of them.

Through the rest of the day that holding keeps working while the mechanism of the joins changes hands. Early on the cut does the joining, as it did between the taxi and the body: a missing-friend question pulls a conspiracy into the frame, and a friend breaking off a voting rant to say that Paul has vanished follows it with "Why don't you come look? It's really weird." By the middle of the day the people on screen have taken the joins over, and they do it with things they can hand across and sentences they say out loud — a lift ("How about a lift back into town?"), an address ("The address, man. Take me there"), Steve's van ("Do I have a car? I have a van"), the stamp already seen at the door, a PixelVision camera passed around at a party for "a project," handed over with its terms stated aloud: shoot whatever you want, pass it on, but I want it back. Austin made those meetings possible and the participants closed them themselves. By dawn the cut has resumed, with "Wake up, America" from a passing car, a taped weapons rant, and finally a wordless Super-8 thrown from the top of Mount Bonnell, and when the cut is doing the joining there is nothing handed over and nobody addressed.

The same day shows the labour problem in the same open form. Kids hawk Cokes, a man sells T-shirts, and outside Les Amis a hitchhiker fresh from his stepfather's funeral tells a camera crew that he will take a job only "when I hear the true call," and then refuses to say what the true call is. The city holds him within reach and hires him for nothing, and he withholds his reason exactly as the door withheld Steve's. Stark and Pais would call the later version of this co-optation without delegated authority, and the phrase fits, except that in 1990 there is always somebody standing there to be asked.[^1] Contribution without employment, with a person still available to answer for it, is the arrangement in its legible form.

Its illegible form is a page. Amazon's Prime Video licensing documentation states that removed titles "may not be resubmitted or appealed," that selection sits at the company's sole discretion, and that it "cannot provide additional details about why any specific title was or was not selected."[^14] Rideshare Drivers United and the Asian Law Caucus, surveying 810 California drivers, found two-thirds reporting deactivation and thirty per cent of those given no reason at all.[^15] Drivers contribute without being hired, as the people at that party did, and the counterparty that removes them has removed anyone who could be argued with; the four powers that the Blue Bayou's doorman exercised in person — price, evaluate, remove, withhold the reason — now attach by rule.[^16] The Video Backpacker gets at the loss from the other side: a video image is more powerful than an actual event, he explains, because he once watched a stabbing and now "can't press rewind," can't pause it, can't fix the hue. A driver cannot replay an allocation once the queue has moved on. Steve's list failed in public and the group renegotiated on the sidewalk; an app assigns a queue position whether or not the driver assents to it, and the inclusion waits for nobody's yes.

That last sentence needs a word other than membership, and Royce is the reason. Since he makes acceptance constitutive, calling the app's assignment a membership would spend his term twice and in opposite senses. Salomé Viljoen has named the mechanism precisely: a horizontal, population-based data relation includes a person in a grouping through no act of his own, on the basis of what he has in common with others rather than anything he has claimed.[^17] Enrollment of that kind is the platform's answer to Royce's question, and the answer is that it has stopped asking. Rideshare Drivers United exists because drivers organised to compel an answer from a third that will not give one.

## Reach, Selection, and the Refusal to Resolve

What makes the form visible rather than merely present? Two things do the work, and only the second of them is a decision of Linklater's. The first is the division of labour the film keeps strictly between its two thirds.

Scott Feld, writing in 1981 on the focused organisation of social ties, was asking how people come to know one another without a matchmaker, and his answer was the focus: a social or physical entity around which joint activities are organised — a workplace, a voluntary organisation, a hangout, a family — such that people who share the activity "will tend to become interpersonally tied and form a cluster."[^18] Austin looks at first like a focus of that kind, a place around which a day of walking and talking is organised. But the people on screen share sidewalks and form no cluster, and the difference is the whole point: the shared activity that produces Feld's ties is missing, and what remains is the bare holding I have been calling reach. Sarah Barns, writing on digital platforms as infrastructures of urban exchange, describes "basic match-making capabilities between mobile subjects," the picking of who actually encounters whom, which is nearer to selection, and to what the camera does when it chooses the next face.[^18] The city supplies the one and the cut supplies the other, and *Slacker* never lets either do the other's job.

Neither term is unoccupied ground, and two neighbours have to be acknowledged. Michael Warner's account of a public constitutes strangers as a collectivity precisely through their common relation to something outside them, with no interaction among them required and membership arriving by mere attention, which is close enough to my reading of the taxi-to-street cut that a reader could reasonably ask what the deferred third adds.[^19] What it adds, I think, is the selection half: Warner's public is addressed, and addressed publics do not have someone choosing which two of their members are put in front of each other next. Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga, from the other direction, already split match quality from match probability in their account of what cities do for the people in them, which is a genuine precedent for treating reach and matching as separable, not as one urban mechanism.[^20] The division of labour I am describing is theirs in economic form; what the film contributes is a case in which the two functions are performed by visibly different agents, one of them a city and the other a camera.

The second decision is the refusal to resolve. Linklater does away with the closure a hundred-character film would seem to demand, and the refusal is not coyness but fidelity: to produce Gary, or to let the hitchhiker name the true call, or to bring the man from the bus station back at dusk, would be to supply from outside precisely the introductions the arrangement does not make. The film's structure and its subject are the same thing. Its recurring question — asked of Gary, and of Paul, and implicitly of everyone who leaves the frame and does not return — is a question the city is not built to answer, and every time an answer fails to arrive the mechanism becomes a little more legible. A door still has to say the rule out loud. A camera still has to choose the next face without telling anyone why.

## Conclusion

The opening cut and the club door, and then the day walked underneath them, show one third doing one thing: holding strangers within reach and arranging none of their meetings. The taxi and the struck woman share a city and a cut and nothing else. Steve shares a sidewalk with a doorman who can charge him, check a list, turn him away and give no reason for it. Gary is asked after and never produced, and the hitchhiker will not say what the true call is. Linklater leaves those questions hanging, and the hanging is what keeps the form in view.

Simmel saw the arrangement in 1908, named it as sociologically significant, and postponed it; when the theme returned two hundred pages later it returned as a fact about pairs, and the third he had promised to treat was never restored to the analysis. Royce set out what it would take for strangers to be members of anything and made acceptance part of the definition, so that his community waits for each member's yes. Reich described the legal form of a dependence that the Blue Bayou's doorman was still exercising in person. What Linklater films is the interval in which all three descriptions are simultaneously true of one afternoon, and in which the people subject to them can still argue with somebody.

That is what the film preserves and what the platform removes, and naming the difference is the reason this reading seems to me worth making. The competence that the day in Austin demands of its participants — knowing how to argue with a door, how to copy a stamp, how to say a join out loud — is a competence the later arrangement makes useless without replacing. That competence is what I called algorithmacy at the outset.[^21] Simmel, Royce, Reich, and Stark and Pais describe structure, membership, grant-access and co-optive enrollment respectively, and among them they leave it unnamed. The people on screen still have it. They still know how to ask, of a city that will not tell them, whether anyone has seen Gary around.
