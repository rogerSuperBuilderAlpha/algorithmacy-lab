## 1. Network narrative theory

### The taxonomy that actually fits Slacker: Berg's "daisy chain"
- Charles Ramírez Berg, "A Taxonomy of Alternative Plots in Recent Films: Classifying the 'Tarantino Effect,'" *Film Criticism* 31, no. 1–2 (2006): 5–61 [SEARCH: Gale listing https://go.gale.com/ps/i.do?id=GALE%7CA153620392&issn=01635069&it=r&linkaccess=abs&p=LitRC&sid=googleScholar&sw=w&userGroupName=anon%7Ed1f93d3a&v=2.1 ; ResearchGate https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291249421_A_taxonomy_of_alternative_plots_in_recent_films_Classifying_the_Tarantino_Effect].
- **[FETCHED]** Screenplayology's summary of Berg's twelve plot types quotes the category verbatim: "*The Daisy Chain Plot:* The story jumps from one protagonist to the next, never to revisit a previous protagonist. Sometimes tied to a prop, sometimes random. E.G. *Slacker*." (https://www.screenplayology.com/content-sections/screenplay-form-content/3-2/)
- This is the sharpest available answer to "is Slacker even a network narrative?": Berg splits the non-reconvening chain off from ensemble, hub-and-spoke, and jumbled plots. Slacker is his type case. Under Bordwell's definition (plotlines that "affect one another to some degree"), Slacker sits at or past the boundary: nothing propagates between strands except the camera's relay.

### Hyperlink cinema
- Coinage: Alissa Quart, "Networked: Dysfunctional families, reproductive acts, and multitasking minds make for *Happy Endings*," *Film Comment* 41, no. 4 (July/Aug. 2005): 48–51 [SEARCH: ProQuest listing https://www.proquest.com/scholarly-journals/networked/docview/210247040/se-2]. Quart reads captions-as-footnotes and split screen as web-influenced form.
- Popularization: Roger Ebert, review of *Syriana* (Dec. 2005), describing films whose "characters or action reside in separate stories, but a connection or influence between those disparate stories is slowly revealed to the audience." Ebert's exact review text could not be fetched (rogerebert.com 403) — **direct quote as relayed by Wikipedia [FETCHED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperlink_cinema]; primary-source check advised.**
- Wikipedia's hyperlink-cinema article lists *Slacker* (1991) among notable examples [FETCHED, same URL]. Note the irony for the chapter: "hyperlink" already imports a web metaphor, but a hyperlink is user-clicked; Slacker's links are broker-chosen — a distinction the chapter can exploit.

### Fractal films
- Wendy Everett, "Fractal Films and the Architecture of Complexity," *Studies in European Cinema* 2, no. 3 (2005): 159–171 [FETCHED metadata: University of Bath research portal, https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/fractal-films-and-the-architecture-of-complexity/ ; also https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1386/seci.2.3.159/1]. Cities in these films are "both entirely random and yet, at the same time, structured by complexity, simultaneity, and violent encounters" (Bath abstract). Her examples: *Short Cuts*, *Magnolia*, *Amélie*, *Code Unknown*, *Free Radicals* — **Slacker not among cited examples in sources consulted.**

### Where Slacker sits (synthesis)
Bordwell's own taxonomy needs interaction among strands "to some degree"; Berg's daisy chain names the degenerate case where the only edge between characters is adjacency in the camera's itinerary. So Slacker is a limit case of the network narrative: the social graph among its ~100 characters is (nearly) edgeless, and the connected object is produced entirely at the level of narration. Bordwell's own section heading — "Narration Makes Networks" (p. 207) — concedes the point in general; Slacker is the film where narration makes *all* of the network. ## 2. Database narrative

### Marsha Kinder
- Concept: "database narrative" — narratives whose structure "exposes the dual processes of selection and combination that lie at the heart of all stories" [FETCHED from Kinder's own concepts page, quoting her chapter in *The New Media Book* (2002), p. 127: https://www.marshakinder.com/concepts/o3.html]. Her film examples there: *Groundhog Day*, *Pulp Fiction*, *The Matrix*, *Memento*, *Mulholland Drive*, *Run Lola Run*, *La Jetée*, *Smoking/No Smoking*, *The Gleaners and I*. **No mention of Slacker or Linklater on that page.**
- Canonical article: Marsha Kinder, "Hot Spots, Avatars, and Narrative Fields Forever: Buñuel's Legacy for New Digital Media and Interactive Database Narrative," *Film Quarterly* 55, no. 4 (2002): 2–15 [SEARCH: UC Press https://online.ucpress.edu/fq/article/55/4/2/41593/Hot-Spots-Avatars-and-Narrative-Fields-Forever]. Buñuel's "hot spots" (incongruous objects as navigation devices) are a suggestive analogue for Slacker's prop-and-passerby handoffs.

### Lev Manovich
- *The Language of New Media* (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2001) [SEARCH: MIT Press https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262632553/the-language-of-new-media/]. Full text fetched from Manovich's site (https://manovich.net/content/04-projects/172-language-of-new-media/language.pdf) and searched [FETCHED]:
  - Exact quote (section "Database and Narrative"): "database and narrative are natural enemies. Competing for the same territory of human culture, each claims an exclusive right to make meaning out of the world."
  - Immediately following, and highly usable for the chapter: "Just like a game player, a reader of a novel gradually reconstructs an algorithm (here I use it metaphorically) which the writer used to create the settings, the characters, and the events." Manovich already frames narrative reception as algorithm-reconstruction.
  - Vertov's *Man with a Movie Camera* is his prime example of "database imagination" merging database and narrative (see also the MIT Press excerpt "Vertov's Dataset": http://mitp-content-server.mit.edu:18180/books/content/sectbyfn?collid=books_pres_0&fn=9780262632553_sch_0001.pdf&id=4121).
  - **Slacker check: a full-text grep of the fetched PDF finds no occurrence of "Slacker" or "Linklater." Manovich does not mention the film.** [FETCHED]
- Kinder likewise shows no evidence of citing Slacker (her database-narrative canon is the forking/repetition corpus, not the relay corpus). **So the database-narrative literature has left Slacker untouched — open ground for the chapter, with the caveat that neither concept was built for it.**

## 3. The camera as narrating agent

- David Bordwell, *Narration in the Fiction Film* (Madison: University of Wisconsin Press, 1985) [SEARCH: Internet Archive https://archive.org/details/narrationinficti0000bord ; Routledge UK ed. https://www.routledge.com/Narration-in-the-Fiction-Film/Bordwell/p/book/9780415018777]. Position (as summarized in multiple fetched/searched secondary notes): narration is a process patterning cues for the spectator's story-construction; Bordwell rejects the "invisible observer" account and calls giving every film a personified narrator or implied author "an anthropomorphic fiction" — narration without a narrator. **Exact page for that phrase UNVERIFIED; commonly cited from ch. 4 — library check.**
- Edward Branigan, *Point of View in the Cinema: A Theory of Narration and Subjectivity in Classical Film* (Berlin/New York: Mouton, 1984) and *Narrative Comprehension and Film* (London/New York: Routledge, 1992) [SEARCH: https://archive.org/details/narrativecompreh0000bran ; UCSB memorial https://www.filmandmedia.ucsb.edu/in-memoriam-edward-branigan/]. Reported formulations: narration is "the overall regulation and distribution of knowledge which determines how and when a spectator acquires knowledge" and "narration refers not to the story itself, but to the knowing of the story" (via https://perspectivecriticism.com/2013/01/21/narration-in-film-edward-branigan-on-point-of-view-versus-focalization-laura-copier/ [SEARCH]). **Quote wording needs page-level check.**
- George M. Wilson, *Narration in Light: Studies in Cinematic Point of View* (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 1986), 250 pp., ISBN 0-8018-3137-7 [FETCHED metadata: https://archive.org/details/narrationinlight00wils]. Argues film needs a concept of point of view distinct from prose fiction's; close readings of five Hollywood films.
- George M. Wilson, *Seeing Fictions in Film: The Epistemology of Movies* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2011), 240 pp., ISBN 978-0-19-959489-4 [SEARCH: NDPR review https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/seeing-fictions-in-film-the-epistemology-of-movies/ ; PhilPapers https://philpapers.org/rec/WILSFI-2]. The "Imagined Seeing Thesis": viewers imagine seeing the fictional events, in the "Mediated Version," through movie-like images that function as a documentary record.
- **Use with care:** Bordwell's whole point is that "the camera chooses" is a spectator's heuristic, not an ontology — the chapter's "engineering third" is stronger if framed as *narration* (an impersonal patterning process) doing brokerage, or via Wilson's mediated imagined seeing, than as a literal camera-agent. Bordwell simultaneously supplies the needed bridge: his ch. 7 section title "Narration Makes Networks" (*Poetics of Cinema*, 207) is nearly the chapter's thesis in three words.

## 4. Cinema as perceptual training (the Benjamin locus)

- **Standard editions:** Walter Benjamin, "The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility: Second Version," in *Selected Writings, Volume 3: 1935–1938* (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 2002); also collected in *The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility, and Other Writings on Media* (Cambridge, MA: Belknap/Harvard University Press, 2008), ISBN 978-0-674-02445-8 [SEARCH: https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674024458 — page exists but content fetch failed; editor/translator names (Eiland/Jennings; Jephcott et al.) **UNVERIFIED here, confirm against the physical volume**]. The older Zohn translation ("The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction," third version) is in *Illuminations*, ed. Hannah Arendt (New York: Schocken) [FETCHED translation credit via marxists.org, below].
- **The training sentence (Second Version):** "The function of film is to train human beings in the apperceptions and reactions needed to deal with a vast apparatus whose role in their lives is expanding almost daily." Quoted with page citation **108** (i.e., *SW* 3:108) at https://circleuncoiled.wordpress.com/2013/05/11/walter-benjamin-the-work-of-art-in-the-age-of-its-technological-reproducibility/ [FETCHED]. This sentence is Second-Version-only; it does not appear in the *Illuminations* third version. **Confirm SW 3:108 in the library copy.**
- **Reception in distraction:** "Reception in distraction [Die Rezeption in der Zerstreuung] . . . finds in film its true training ground" — attested in Carolin Duttlinger, "Reception in Distraction" (Project MUSE, https://muse.jhu.edu/article/41340/summary) and the Bloomsbury *Walter Benjamin and Art* chapter (https://www.bloomsburycollections.com/book/walter-benjamin-and-art/ch1-reception-in-distraction) [SEARCH]. In the Zohn/third version, section XV: "The public is an examiner, but an absent-minded one." and "The film with its shock effect meets this mode of reception halfway." [FETCHED: https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/philosophy/works/ge/benjamin.htm, trans. Harry Zohn]. So the chapter's "cinema trained viewers for a century" claim has an exact Benjaminian locus: artwork essay, distraction section (sec. XV in the third version), plus the SW 3:108 training sentence.
- **Kuleshov effect [FETCHED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuleshov_effect]:** Kuleshov's 1910s–20s workshop intercut Ivan Mosjoukine's unchanged face with soup / a dead girl / a reclining woman; audiences read hunger, grief, desire. Pudovkin's 1929 account: audiences "raved about the acting" though "in all three cases the face was exactly the same." Hitchcock re-popularized it (Truffaut interviews, 1983 ed.; CBC *Telescope*, 1964). Primary texts: V. I. Pudovkin, *Film Technique* (1929, trans. Ivor Montagu); Lev Kuleshov, *Kuleshov on Film: Writings by Lev Kuleshov*, trans. Ron Levaco (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1974) — **Levaco edition details from search listings only; confirm.** For the chapter: the Kuleshov effect is spectators inferring a relation between elements that were never co-present — literally reading a broker's edit, a century of practice at trusting third-party composition.
- **Continuity editing as learned convention:** David Bordwell, Kristin Thompson, and Jeff Smith, *Film Art: An Introduction*, 12th ed. (New York: McGraw-Hill Education, 2020) [SEARCH: https://www.mheducation.com/highered/product/film-art-introduction-thompson-bordwell/M9781260056082.html] — the standard textbook treatment of the 180° system and shot-relation conventions (chapter on editing). Best-selling introduction since 1979 per publisher.

## 5. Algorithmic culture and cinema spectatorship (prior art)

- Ted Striphas, "Algorithmic Culture," *European Journal of Cultural Studies* 18, no. 4–5 (2015): 395–412 [SEARCH: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1367549415577392 ; open PDF https://www.dawsoncollege.qc.ca/ai/wp-content/uploads/sites/180/10-Ted-Striphas_Algorithmic-Culture.pdf]. Defines algorithmic culture as the delegation of "the work of culture — the sorting, classifying and hierarchizing of people, places, objects and ideas" to computational processes.
- Blake Hallinan and Ted Striphas, "Recommended for You: The Netflix Prize and the Production of Algorithmic Culture," *New Media & Society* 18, no. 1 (2016): 117–137, doi:10.1177/1461444814538646 [SEARCH: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444814538646]. **Directly connects movie culture and algorithmic curation** — algorithmic systems rendered "legible as forms of cultural decision making" via film recommendation.
- William Uricchio, "The Algorithmic Turn: Photosynth, Augmented Reality and the Changing Implications of the Image," *Visual Studies* 26, no. 1 (2011): 25–35 [SEARCH: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1472586X.2011.548486]. Images increasingly constituted by "algorithmically defined relations between the viewing subject and the world viewed" — the algorithm inserted into the subject–image relation itself.
- **Jonathan Beller, *The Cinematic Mode of Production: Attention Economy and the Society of the Spectacle* (Hanover, NH: Dartmouth College Press, 2006)** [SEARCH: https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/distributed/C/bo44893600.html ; review https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0196859907312288]. The closest prior art to the chapter's training claim: cinema construes looking as productive labor; cinema abstracts the assembly line — where workers performed sequenced physical operations on moving objects, "spectators perform sequenced visual operations on moving montage fragments"; the capitalization of perception underpins the current global (new-media) economy. Beller thus already runs the arc *montage → trained perception → digital economy*.
- Lev Manovich and Andreas Kratky, *Soft Cinema: Navigating the Database* (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2005), ISBN 978-0-262-13456-9 [SEARCH: https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262134569/soft-cinema/ ; project page https://manovich.net/index.php/projects/soft-cinema]. Software "edits movies in real time by choosing the elements from the database using the systems of rules defined by the authors" — algorithmic editing realized in practice; every viewing generates a different cut. Prior art from the production side: the edit itself as algorithm.
- Also relevant, from §2 [FETCHED]: Manovich's *Language of New Media* line that a narrative reader "gradually reconstructs an algorithm . . . which the writer used" — the reception side of the same equation, published 2001.
- Lead not yet verified: Shane Denson, *Discorrelated Images* (reported: Duke University Press, 2020) on post-cinematic, algorithmically generated/processed images — **UNVERIFIED, check before citing.**

## How this serves the argument

### For the "engineering third" claim
1. **Slacker really is a limit case, and the taxonomies say so.** Bordwell's definition requires plotlines that "affect one another to some degree" (blog, 2006) or are at least "contingently linked" (2008, 192). Slacker's characters share almost no edges; Berg's "daisy chain" (never revisit a protagonist; sometimes tied to a prop, sometimes random — *Slacker* as the named example) is precisely the category for a chain whose links are made only by the relay. The chapter can argue: strip every diegetic edge out of a network narrative and what remains is the broker — the narration — still producing a connected whole. The network in Slacker is a star graph whose hub is off-screen.
2. **Bordwell hands over the key phrase.** His ch. 7 section "Narration Makes Networks" (p. 207) states in general what the chapter claims Slacker radicalizes: connectivity is a narrational product, not a property of the story world. (Check p. 215, where the Google Books index places Slacker, when the library copy is in hand.)
3. **The narrator-theory literature disciplines the metaphor.** Bordwell (1985) warns that a personified camera is "an anthropomorphic fiction"; Wilson (1986, 2011) and Branigan (1984, 1992) provide the machinery for saying, rigorously, that viewers imagine a mediated seeing whose selections are made for them. The "engineering third" should be cast as impersonal narrational agency (Bordwell's process; Branigan's "regulation and distribution of knowledge") to preempt the obvious objection — which strengthens the platform analogy, since Uber's matcher and the feed ranker are exactly that: impersonal, unpersonifiable brokers whose choices are experienced as "what happens next."
4. **"Hyperlink cinema" is a foil, not a fit.** A hyperlink is user-actuated; Slacker's transitions are broker-actuated. The chapter can use Quart/Ebert's web metaphor to show that even the digital-native label chosen in 2005 mistook curation for navigation — the same error that makes feed ranking feel like browsing.

### For the "cinema as training" claim
5. **The Benjamin locus is exact.** SW 3:108 ("the function of film is to train human beings in the apperceptions and reactions needed to deal with a vast apparatus...") plus the distraction section ("the public is an examiner, but an absent-minded one"; film as the "true training ground" of reception in distraction). The chapter's claim is Benjamin-shaped and should cite the Second Version, not *Illuminations*, for the training sentence.
6. **Kuleshov gives the mechanism.** Viewers reliably infer relations between shots never co-present; continuity editing (Bordwell/Thompson/Smith) shows this inference became a learned, invisible convention. A century of practice at trusting third-party composition of adjacency is exactly the perceptual repertoire feed-reading requires.

### Prior art that limits the novelty claim
7. **Beller (2006) owns the strongest version of "cinema trained viewers for the economy."** He already runs montage → industrialized perception → new-media economy, with spectatorship as value-productive labor. The chapter must cite him and differentiate: Beller's story is about *attention extraction*; ours is about *brokered matching* — the structural skill of reading handoffs, not the economic capture of looking.
8. **Manovich (2001, 2005) owns "editing meets algorithm."** *Language of New Media* already frames narrative reception as reconstructing the author's algorithm and stages database vs. narrative ("natural enemies"); *Soft Cinema* literally implements algorithmic editing. Differentiate: Manovich's algorithm generates or organizes *content*; the engineering third performs *coordination among parties* (character-to-character, rider-to-driver) — a brokerage function, not a compositional one.
9. **Hallinan & Striphas (2016), Striphas (2015), Uricchio (2011)** connect film culture and images to algorithmic curation on the platform side, but none reads *editing itself* as proto-brokerage, and none touches Slacker. The specific claim — that the daisy-chain relay is cinema's rehearsal of platform matching — appears unclaimed in the literature surveyed. State novelty at that grain, not as "no one has linked cinema and algorithms."
10. **Gaps to close before publication:** exact wording of Bordwell on Slacker (*Poetics of Cinema*, p. 215); whether Azcona mentions Slacker; SW 3 editor/translator front matter; Ebert's Syriana review text; Denson if used. All flagged above.
