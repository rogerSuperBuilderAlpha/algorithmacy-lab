# Deep research: section 7, "Enclosure — the distribution chain then and now"

Run 2026-08-01 via the deep-research workflow, 5 search angles, 3-lens adversarial verification,
vote counts recorded per finding. Twelve claims went into verification; eight survived (refuted by at
most one of three lenses) and four were refuted by two or three. Every finding below carries the
verifier's corrected wording where a verifier supplied one, and says so. Where the three lenses
disagreed, the disagreement is stated in the confidence line rather than smoothed away. The run's two
principal results cut against the section as currently drafted: the phrase "not at the interface" is

## Finding 1 — Narrowing happens twice, at licensing and at prominence, and the section may not choose between them (medium, 2-1) [C1]

Bengesser, Hilborn and Steemers track British content through European VOD and measure two stages of
the funnel rather than one. Of 15,584 unique British first-produced film and TV titles in European VOD
circulation, Netflix's European catalogues hold 1,165 — 7 percent. Inside a national Netflix catalogue,
only about 200 titles can be reasonably found by a viewer who is actively looking, only about 50 reach
the landing page, and 70 are what viewers aged 16–34 recall as British shows they know. The section's
load-bearing formulation, that the enclosure operates at acquisition and licensing and *not* at the
recommendation interface, does not survive this. Neither does its inverse. COMPLICATES.

**Sources:** Bengesser, Cathrin, Matthew Hilborn, and Jeanette Steemers. 2025. "Comparative VOD
catalogue research: Circulation, presence and prominence of British content in Europe." *Convergence:
The International Journal of Research into New Media Technologies* 31 (5): 1511–1531 (published online
21 August 2024). DOI 10.1177/13548565241268057.
https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/463787496/bengesser-et-al-2024-comparative-vod-catalogue-research-circulation-presence-and-prominence-of-british-content-in-europe.pdf

**Evidence:** Abstract, pp. 1511–1512, verbatim: "In the case of British content availability on VoD,
it shows how a vast number of ca. 15,000 individual UK titles available in European catalogues boils
down to about 200 titles that can be easily found on Netflix, and 70 titles thereof that young
audiences (aged 16–34) recall as UK shows they know and remember." Conclusion, verbatim, including the
comma before "can be found," which is in the original: "On Netflix, British content plays an even
larger role in European offerings, surpassing domestic content in larger markets as well, but only 7%
of all British content in VoD circulation in Europe, can be found in Netflix's European catalogues. Of
this British content in Netflix's national catalogues only about 200 titles can be reasonably found by
audiences (across 10 categories and including searches for British content), while only about 50 titles
are promoted on Netflix's landing/home page." And: "British content with high prominence on Netflix
also tends to feature more highly among young audiences' recollections of British titles." Body, p.
1517, verbatim: "15,584 unique films and TV titles" and "1165 unique British first-produced film and TV
titles."

*Method:* European Audiovisual Observatory Lumiere VoD database, plus JustWatch.com, plus systematic
tracking of VoD landing pages in Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands and Italy, with audience surveys
from the AHRC "Screen Encounters with Britain" project (2022–2025; award AH/W000113/1 appears in the
Crossref funder record). Open-access CC-BY version of record retrieved from Aarhus University PURE and
text-extracted; running head reads "Convergence … 31(5)." The PDF renders "thereof" as "there of," a
kerning artifact of extraction, not a variant reading.

*Verifier corrections carried:* the third lens refuted the claim's ranking of the two stages, and the
refutation stands. Nothing in the article says the interface stage is worse, and the article's own
numbers make them comparable: licensing cuts 15,584 to 1,165 (92.5 percent), while Table 2's
per-country landing-page counts run Denmark 51/567 (9.0 percent), Germany 42/602 (7.0 percent),
Netherlands 48/935 (5.1 percent), Italy 46/746 (6.2 percent), with the text stating "the share of
British content featured is below 10% for all three markets." The funnel is also not nested: the 200
and 50 are per-country counts against per-country catalogues, not subsets of the 1,165. Two further
corrections the section must observe. First, the landing-page study deliberately stripped
personalisation — verbatim, "using 'pure', newly created accounts with limited personal information
(age) and no expression of genre preferences. Moreover, to limit personalisation as much as possible,
browser data and cookies were cleared every day" — so it measures editorial promotion and cannot be
cited as evidence about *recommendation*. The authors say as much: "more evidence is needed about the
algorithmic filtering and promotional processes happening between VoD catalogues as database back-ends
and VoD landing pages as interface front-ends." Second, the framing "the only study that measures both
stages" is an unverifiable absence claim and the authors present the piece as a methods article: "This
article's main concern are the methodological steps and challenges involved…" Drop that framing.

## Finding 2 — Linklater and Pierson argued with a named vice-president and lost (medium, 2-1) [C3]

On the home-video release, John Pierson and Richard Linklater took the case for *Slacker* directly to
Susan Blodgett, vice-president of Orion's Home Video division, and got nowhere. Orion shipped about
7,000 cassettes in summer 1992, the quantity it used for foreign-language titles, against a St.
Martin's Press tie-in book of the same film that sold over 20,000 copies. The section's contrast has to
be phrased as arguability, not answerability. COMPLICATES.

**Sources:** Macor, Alison. 2010. *Chainsaws, Slackers, and Spy Kids: Thirty Years of Filmmaking in
Austin, Texas*. Austin: University of Texas Press, 113–114; endnotes at 326 cite Linklater interview 31
Aug. 2004, Pierson interview 20 Sept. 2004, and Pierson, *Spike, Mike Reloaded*, 194–195. No DOI ;
Pierson, John. 2013 [1996]. "Slacker: Slacking Off." *The Criterion Collection, The Current*, 18
September 2013 (republication of the *Slacker* chapter of *Spike, Mike, Slackers & Dykes*, Faber,
1996). No DOI. https://web.archive.org/web/20260101160718/https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off
(the live criterion.com URL is bot-blocked)

**Evidence:** Macor, p. 113, verbatim: "Both Pierson and Linklater tried unsuccessfully to convince
Susan Blodgett, vice president of Orion's Home Video division, to consider the film's good press and
tremendous cultural impact as they planned the release of the *Slacker* videocassette. Linklater argued
that an untapped audience existed who was unfamiliar with the movie but aware of the word 'slacker' and
its cultural connotations, but his words fell on deaf ears." Same page, verbatim: "'We're doing
calculations. Thirty thousand stores, every store has to get at least one copy. We get 12%, that'll be
a couple of hundred thousand,' says Linklater. But Orion shipped only about seven thousand units, the
same figure they used for foreign-language titles, according to Pierson." p. 114, verbatim: "'Everybody
got behind it and it sold—the number I always heard—20,000 copies in its initial release. Orion shipped
7,000 videotapes. Three times more books than videos? That's not the America I'm familiar with,' says
Pierson." p. 114, verbatim: "The *Slacker* video and the book both hit stores in the summer of 1992,
approximately one year after the movie's national theatrical release." Pierson's own essay corroborates
the book figure independently, verbatim: "St. Martin's Press even had a tie-in book that sold over
20,000 copies."

*Method:* Macor's full OCR text was downloaded from the Internet Archive item and quotations were
located against drop folios, which in this book print the page number at the end of its page's text.
Pierson was retrieved from the Wayback capture by curl, since WebFetch refuses web.archive.org; the
byline block reads "By John Pierson / Essays — Sep 18, 2013."

*Verifier corrections carried:* three, and one of them narrows the finding materially. (a) The
archive.org item is a user upload in the "opensource" collection, an unauthorized scan of the UT Press
book. Cite the UT Press edition with page numbers and do not print that URL as a version of record.
(b) On drop-folio logic the video-shipping endnotes fall under the running head "NOTES TO PAGES
113–126" and sit on p. 326, not p. 325. (c) The third lens refuted the scope of the inference and the
refutation is accepted here: the refusal came from *one division* — Orion's Home Video division — after
Michael Barker and Tom Bernard had already left ("Before the end of the year, Barker and Bernard were
in talks with other companies") and while, in Pierson's words, "the entire company was poised to
collapse." The same book shows the *acquisition* gate responding to argument: Pierson's *Film Comment*
tip reached Barker, Barker phoned his Austin relatives to check the Dobie sellouts, and Orion Classics
bought the film. Write "one gate refused them," not "the chain did not move." Macor also offers a
competing explanation the original claim dropped — Pierson's "They saw no evidence that *Slacker*
should be treated any differently," which is template application under distress rather than deafness.
Note as well that Linklater's thirty-thousand-stores arithmetic is his own hope quoted from an
interview, not an industry estimate. The point survives in the sharper form: address was possible and
refusal was attributable, which is exactly what "we cannot provide additional details" denies.

## Finding 5 — The middle tier of American distribution refilled after 2008; the third-tier collapse is a reclassification artifact (medium, 2-1) [C7]

Tzioumakis must be used from his body text, not his abstract. Four studio specialty divisions closed
(Warner Independent, Picturehouse, Paramount Vantage, Miramax) plus MGM's failed United Artists
relaunch, 2008–2010, reducing an average of seven to eight such companies to three or four. The vacated
tier refilled: "late indies" averaged 6.46 percent market share in 2009–2019 against the divisions'
6.91 percent in 1998–2008, with the surviving divisions holding a further 3.11 percent on top. BOTH.

**Sources:** Tzioumakis, Yannis. 2023. "After the 'Great Studio Pullback of '08': Late Indiewood and
American Independent Film Theatrical Distribution in the Age of Streaming (2008–2019)." *Media
Industries* 10 (1): 1. DOI 10.3998/mij.1482. https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/mij/article/id/1482/
(the site runs Anubis bot protection; the version of record is served to a plain non-browser
user-agent)

**Evidence:** Body, "The Landscape Post-2008," verbatim: "The closure of four studio specialty film
divisions (Warner Independent, Picturehouse, Paramount Vantage, and Miramax) and the failed relaunch of
United Artists (MGM's specialty film division) within the space of three years (2008–2010) reduced the
number of these divisions greatly. While in 1998–2008 there was an average of seven to eight such
companies with a defining presence in the independent film sector, after 2010 their number ranged
between three and four." Abstract, verbatim: "Within the space of less than three years (2008–2010),
three of the 'Big Six' conglomerated Hollywood majors shuttered five of their specialty film
divisions." On the refill, verbatim: "Collectively, this group of companies on average managed a 6.46
percent market share in 2009–2019, a figure that is remarkably similar to the 6.91 percent of the
studio specialty film divisions in the 1998–2008 timeframe." Conclusion, verbatim: "However, the gap
created was quickly filled by 'late indies,' existing and mostly new companies, some of which quickly
established themselves in the theatrical distribution market. Despite not placing indiewood at the core
of their distribution activities and trading in a wide variety of films, they nonetheless did not
abandon indiewood either, with approximately a sixth of all their releases designated as such." On the
third tier, verbatim: "the total market share of the rest of the distributors (the third tier) (124, on
average, for the eleven-year period 2009–2019) is just 2.11 percent or a paltry 0.016 percent for each
company" and "This 2.11 percent compares with 10.22 percent for an average of 105 distributors in the
1998–2008 period, seemingly suggesting a massive shift in this category (many more distributors vie for
much less market share)." His own disarming qualification, verbatim: the 10.22 percent figure "includes
former studios such as MGM, companies such as Lionsgate and Artisan that were having major runaway
hits, companies that aspired to mini-major status such as Newmarket, and other entertainment
conglomerate-owned subsidiaries such as IFC Films" — and then: "In the post-2008 landscape, however,
they deserve to be seen as a separate group, as 'late indies,' that have joined the remaining specialty

*Method:* version of record retrieved with a plain curl user-agent past the Anubis check and converted
to text by two lenses independently; Crossref corroborates volume 10, issue 1, 2023-07-24, University
of Michigan Library. Tables 2 and 3 corroborate the averages: the 1998–2008 row ends "AVE 7.5 6.91%
77.31% 5.56% 10.22% 105"; the 2009–2019 row ends "AVE 4 3.11% 80.87% 7.45% 8.2 6.46% 2.11% 142."

*Verifier corrections carried:* the lenses split on how to describe the abstract, and the third lens
refuted the section's intended use of the third-tier figures. Both corrections are accepted. (a)
"Inconsistent" overstates the abstract problem. The body reads: "Between May 2008 and the end of 2010,
Time Warner, Viacom, and Disney moved to shutter their respective specialty film divisions (Warner
Independent and Picturehouse, Paramount Vantage, and Miramax), while former major MGM pulled the plug
on United Artists." The count of five and the window are identical; the abstract's slip is attributing
the fifth closure to a Big Six parent when MGM is a former major. Call it imprecision. (b) **Do not use
the 10.22-to-2.11 percent drop as evidence of contraction.** Tzioumakis says "seemingly suggesting" and
then explains that he promoted the largest 1998–2008 third-tier firms into the later period's second

places an Orion-Classics-scale distributor in the third tier. (c) Two numbers must stay apart in the
prose: 124 is the third-tier average distributor count, while Table 3's average *total* distributor
count is 142. (d) The surviving specialty divisions did not recover to 6.91 percent; their own share
fell to 3.11 percent, and the late indies supplied the 6.46. Write "the middle tier refilled," never

## Finding 6 — The worldwide-rights buyout is a documented pressure on presale finance that presale holders resisted (medium, 2-1) [C9]

Apple's record $25 million for *CODA* — the deal the section already cites — was for the world, on a
film whose financiers had presold territories. Distributors in Italy, the Nordics and Latin America
refused to hand back what they had bought. Territorial presale is how independent films get financed
before anyone has seen them, and a buyer that insists on the world puts that route under pressure.
SUPPORTS, with a hard limit on how far the sentence may go.

**Sources:** Lindahl, Chris. 2021. "Apple Paid $25 Million for Worldwide Rights to 'CODA.' Here's Why
That's a Problem." *IndieWire*, 6 March 2021. No DOI.
https://www.indiewire.com/features/general/apple-coda-sundance-25-million-international-rights-1234621432/
; Mayberry, Carly. 2021. "Apple Studios Buys 'CODA' for Record-Breaking $25 Million." *Forbes* (Former
Contributor column), 1 February 2021. No DOI.
https://www.forbes.com/sites/carlymayberry/2021/02/01/apple-studios-buys-coda-for-record-breaking-25-million/

**Evidence:** Lindahl, headline verbatim: "Apple Paid $25 Million for Worldwide Rights to 'CODA.'
Here's Why That's a Problem"; dek verbatim: "Apple bought 'CODA' at Sundance for the world, but the
film's financiers had already sold rights to international distributors." Body, verbatim: "How can
Apple hold all worldwide rights to a film that's already sold to territories all over the world?";
"Those international distributors participated in a bedrock strategy of independent film: Take the risk
up front for the potential reward of a hit title."; "Global streamers like Netflix and Apple TV+

"Apple Studios is now said to be buying out pre-sold international territories that helped finance
production."

*Method:* Lindahl retrieved directly with a browser user-agent by one lens (article:published_time
2021-03-06T15:00:18+00:00) and through the r.jina.ai text proxy by two others; Mayberry retrieved
directly, timestamped Feb 01, 2021, 05:00pm EST.

*Verifier corrections carried:* the third lens refuted the inference and the refutation binds. Lindahl
documents a conflict, not an extinction, and his closing paragraph — which the original claim omitted —

byline reads "Former Contributor," the same caveat that demotes Bloom in Finding 3, so lean on Lindahl
for anything Mayberry does not add; and the Palm Springs record figure is unresolved between Forbes
("more than $22 million") and TheWrap ("$17.5 million") — use neither. Two useful details the original
claim omitted and the section can use: the presales were "crucial in getting the $10 million film made
without a US distributor," and Nordisk Film pre-bought "nearly two years ago."

## How this lands in section 7

Two sentences have to come out, and both are load-bearing.

The first is "at acquisition and licensing rather than at the recommendation interface." Bengesser,
Hilborn and Steemers measured both stages, and the narrowing is comparable at each: 15,584 British
titles in European VOD circulation become 1,165 in Netflix's European catalogues, and inside a national
catalogue fewer than one title in ten reaches the landing page. The section may not choose a stage. It
may not choose the other one either — nothing in that article ranks them, and the funnel is not nested.
The repair is a two-stage sentence: licensing decides what *can* be found, prominence decides what *is*
found. That version is better for the chapter than the one it replaces, because the camera-as-selector
argument needs both. A landing page that chooses what you see next is the closest thing streaming has
to Linklater's camera, and the section had been arguing that the interesting action was somewhere else.
One discipline goes with it: the researchers cleared cookies daily and used fresh accounts precisely to
strip personalisation out, so their landing-page numbers are evidence about editorial promotion and
must never be cited as evidence about recommendation.

The second is any phrasing where "a chain you could argue with" shades into "and the argument worked."
Linklater and Pierson took the video release to Susan Blodgett by name, and Orion shipped 7,000
cassettes, its foreign-language quantity, against a tie-in book that sold 20,000. Arguability is not
answerability. But the correction has a floor as well as a ceiling: one division refused them, after
Barker and Bernard had left and while the company was collapsing, and the *acquisition* gate in the
same book did respond to argument — a paragraph in *Film Comment* reached a buyer, who telephoned his
relatives in Austin to check whether the Dobie sellouts were real. Write "one gate refused them." The
surviving contrast is narrow and durable: address was possible and refusal was attributable. Amazon's
policy page is the other half of that sentence, in Amazon's words — "we cannot provide additional
details about why any specific title was or was not selected for licensing."

The platform half loses a leg and gains a better foundation. Amazon publishes three of the four powers
the chapter attributes to platforms: it reads ("we continuously review performance metrics and viewing
behavior"), it removes ("at any time at the sole discretion of Prime Video"), and it refuses
explanation ("may not be resubmitted or appealed"). It does not publish pricing. Either find a rate
card or a licensing agreement, or drop pricing from the list; a four-item list with one unsourced item
is the failure mode this run exists to catch. The same page also dates the change, against Smits's 2017
finding that Amazon submission was "unfiltered, with no gatekeeping restrictions": self-service is now
by invitation only, and unsolicited SVOD submissions are not accepted at all.

The comparison the section wanted to draw between the 1991 gates and the platform cannot rest on
selector-versus-not-selector. Orion Classics applied the *My Beautiful Laundrette* template unchanged
and would not budge, which on the chapter's own third test is the behaviour of a setting. Canby's
review was a gate whose verdict could be waited out for three months. Keep the roll-call of named
people; drop the claim that each of them passes the three-question test; rest the contrast on the
powers Amazon publishes.

Three factual sentences get sharper. The sixteen-screen ceiling is the number worth printing: sixteen
bookings is not a national audience reached but sixteen argued-for placements, which supports the
section's people-you-could-argue-with claim while forbidding any like-for-like comparison of reach with
a recommender. The bankruptcy sentence must be recast — the film played three months past the 11–12
December 1991 filing and the video did ship in summer 1992, so the second window was starved by a
division applying a default, not killed by Chapter 11. And the Sony Pictures Classics lineage can be
reinstated, cited to Macor and Perren as an executive fact rather than a corporate one.

Two arguments the section should stop making because the sources it wanted disarm them. The
third-tier collapse from 10.22 to 2.11 percent is not evidence of contraction: Tzioumakis says
"seemingly suggesting" and then explains that he reclassified the largest earners upward himself. And
the middle tier refilled — late indies averaged 6.46 percent against the vanished divisions' 6.91,
with the surviving divisions holding 3.11 on top — so no sentence of the form "the studios closed the
specialty divisions and the streamers took over" survives. What survives, and is more interesting, is
that the acquisition market changed shape rather than shrinking: A24 outbid Netflix for the top
Sundance 2026 title, while only about a dozen of ninety-plus premieres arrived with any distribution at
all. *Slacker*'s own 1990 position — no other bidder, one lowball offer taken on a template — is closer
to that norm than to the 1996–2008 norm.

Finally, three absences are findings and belong in the prose, not in a footnote. Nobody has studied how
the major streamers decide what to acquire, and the field says why: "the anti-transparency policies of
the most popular streaming platforms… and scholars' lack of access to decision makers." No causal study
exists of whether film recommendation changes what gets watched; the causal work is about Spotify
playlists, and the catalogue-coverage difference blocks the transfer. And the producer's representative
has no scholarly literature at all, which means Pierson's memoir is not one account among several but
the account — a dependency the section should name, since its whole 1991 half rests on it.
