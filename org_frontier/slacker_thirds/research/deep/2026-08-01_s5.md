## Finding 1 — Two-sided pricing is the evidenced form of "prices each match" (high, 2-1) [C1]

Since Q1 2023, in London, the price the passenger pays and the fee the driver receives vary
independently of each other; drivers cannot see the passenger's fare in the app, and Uber bars them
from asking the passenger directly. A participatory audit of 1.5 million trips from 258 self-selected
UK drivers finds that Uber's median cut rose from 25% to 29%, exceeds 50% on some trips, and is
concentrated on the highest-fare trips, while the mean cut stayed at 25%; Uber's nominal surplus per
hour a driver spends on trip rose 38%, from £8.47 to £11.70. This, and not surge, is the current
evidenced form of the chapter's "prices each match" power, and it is two-sided pricing. SUPPORTS.

**Sources:** Reuben Binns, Jake Stein, Siddhartha Datta, Max Van Kleek, and Nigel Shadbolt, "Not Even
Nice Work If You Can Get It; A Longitudinal Study of Uber's Algorithmic Pay and Pricing," in
*Proceedings of the 2025 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT '25)*
(New York: ACM, 2025), 1484–1497. DOI 10.1145/3715275.3732099. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15278
(PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15278v1)

**Evidence:** §1, verbatim: "In Q1 of 2023, Uber introduced 'dynamic' pricing in London, which means
fares are no longer a simple function of time and distance. Instead the price the passenger pays and
the fee the driver receives vary independently of each other, and are calculated dynamically based on
location of pick up and drop off, time of day / week / year, probability of driver / passenger
cancellation, and other factors undisclosed by Uber. This means that Uber's take rate (the percentage
they keep) is no longer stable but varies trip-by-trip." §1, verbatim: "The amount that a passenger
pays for a trip is not available to drivers in the app, and Uber bars drivers from asking their
passengers for this information directly, so it is not possible for drivers to work out Uber's cut on
any given journey. In December 2024, Uber began disclosing a weekly average take rate to drivers in
the app, but this still does not include a trip-by-trip breakdown." §4.3, verbatim: "since dynamic
pricing, the customer fares were removed from drivers' weekly earnings report, meaning drivers could
no longer see how much of a cut Uber takes from any given trip (the 'take rate')"; "In contrast to the
standard 75/25% split prior to dynamic pricing, driver take rates are now frequently as low as 50-60%,
and in some cases even below 50%"; "Mean average take rates have remained at 75%, which supports
Uber's claim in [31] that their 25% cut remains stable. However, the distribution is skewed right,
with the median average take rate post-dynamic pricing at only 71%. Only 46% of drivers have managed
to maintain an average take rate of 75% or above"; "the higher the fare charged to the customer, the
higher Uber's take rate, and the less drivers earn per minute in absolute terms. Drivers are therefore
worse off when they select higher-value fares." §5.1, verbatim: "Uber's median take rate per driver
has increased from 25% to 29%, and on some trips the take rate is over 50%. Furthermore, the higher
take rates are concentrated among higher-fare trips, which explains how Uber can extract an additional
38% surplus from its driver's labour on average (equivalent to £3.23 per hour), a phenomenon which is
obscured in the average take rate." §4.2, verbatim: "In most months since 2023, drivers spend more
time waiting to be allocated their next job than they do on journeys with passengers."

Design and limits, the authors' own words (§5.2): "our sample of 258 drivers, while relatively large
for a research study, is still small relative to the whole population of 100,000 Uber drivers in the
UK. It is also drawn from drivers who are sufficiently motivated to sign up." A participatory audit
run with Worker Info Exchange on GDPR subject-access data; UK only; self-selected; 96% men.

*Conflict resolved before verification:* the paper uses "take rate" in both directions and the numbers
are complementary. §4.3's 71% median is the DRIVER's share; §5.1's 29% is UBER's cut (100 − 71). Write
"Uber's median cut rose from 25% to 29%" and cite §5.1. Do not mix the two conventions in one sentence.

*Verifier corrections carried:* (a) The third lens narrowed the scope — the paper says dynamic pricing
was introduced *in London*, so the section must not state it as an unqualified fact about Uber
everywhere. (b) The symmetry claim is unsupported in one direction: the source establishes only that
the driver cannot see the passenger's fare. It says nothing about what the passenger is shown. Drop
"keeps each side from seeing the other's." (c) The 38% is defined as revenue "for each hour they are
on a trip" — Uber's own working-time definition, not per hour worked — and the £8.47→£11.70 comparison
is nominal while the paper inflation-adjusts its pay figures. Say "per hour on trip," and pair it with
§4.2 on standby time. (d) Two further things the chapter should carry: §4.3 reports the driver's share
can "exceed 100%, i.e. the passenger's fare does not even cover the driver's pay, meaning Uber makes a
loss on these journeys," which strengthens the two-sided reading; and the mean cut stayed at 25%, so
the median is doing all the work.

*Residual notes:* an internal inconsistency in the source — §1 says Uber began disclosing weekly
average take rates in December 2024, the §4.3 lead-in says January 2025. DBLP renders the second author
as "Jake M. L. Stein" where arXiv gives "Jake Stein"; check the ACM byline before the footnote is set.

## Finding 2 — The Stark & Pais quotation in the chapter is half fabricated (high, 3-0) [C2]

The chapter's quotation from Stark and Pais splices two passages, one of which does not exist.
"Rankings but not ranks" is verbatim and sits on p. 49, restated on p. 54. "Accounts but no
accountability" appears nowhere in the article. The nearest real sentence is on p. 61 and is shaped
differently. Three lenses agreed; two of them downloaded the publisher galley and searched it, and the
string "accounts but" returns zero hits anywhere in the article. CORRECTS.

**Sources:** David Stark and Ivana Pais, "Algorithmic Management in the Platform Economy," *Sociologica*
14, no. 3 (2020): 47–72. DOI 10.6092/issn.1971-8853/12221.
https://sociologica.unibo.it/article/view/12221 (galley:
https://sociologica.unibo.it/article/download/12221/12281/43213)

**Evidence:** p. 49, verbatim: "In contrast to Scientific Management at the turn of the twentieth
century, in the algorithmic management of the twenty-first century there are rules but these are not
bureaucratic, there are rankings but not ranks, and there is monitoring but it is not disciplinary."
p. 54, the same formula restated, verbatim: "First, on platforms you will find plenty of rules but
these are not bureaucratic. Second, (and this more elaborated) you will also find lots of rankings but
there are hardly any ranks. Third, there is monitoring aplenty, but often it is not disciplinary."
p. 61, the accountability passage in full, verbatim: "The counter-parties are not accountable to
project operators; although they evaluate each other through all kinds of devices and must take these

plentiful, but no one is accountable because there is no accountability in algorithmic accounting."

Better fit for the selector/governor distinction than the quote the chapter now uses, p. 49, verbatim:
"Whereas the devices and practices of Taylorism were part of a system of hierarchical supervision, the
devices and practices of algorithmic management take place within a different economy of attention and
a new regime of visibility. Triangular rather than vertical, and not as a panopticon, the lines of
vision in algorithmic management are not lines of supervision." And, same page: "platform owners co-opt
the behavior of providers and users, enrolling them in the practices of algorithmic management without
managerial authority having been delegated to them."

*Verifier notes:* page assignments confirmed three ways by folio arithmetic and page-footer reading
(PDF p. 1 = printed p. 47; every page carries the DOI footer). The DOI is not registered with Crossref

cite for the exact wording above must stay 49. No corrections were required.

## Finding 3 — "Only the algorithm sees the queue" needs narrowing, but the correction was itself narrowed (medium, 2-1, lenses split) [C3]

At airports Uber discloses a derived quantity: "a dynamic estimated wait time until your next offer,"
computed partly from "How many drivers are ahead of you." The queue itself, and the driver's place in
it, are not shown. What a decline or a logout costs is the spot, not the sight of it. The chapter's
sentence "only the algorithm sees the queue" survives in the ordinary street case and survives at
airports too, provided the section does not claim the airport display is invisible — it discloses a
number computed from the queue. COMPLICATES.

**Evidence:** "Driving at the airport," verbatim, set as the source sets it: "Once you join the airport

## Finding 4 — Surge has not been shown to know who the two parties are (medium, 2-1, the third lens objects hard) [C5]

In the leading theoretical and empirical treatments, surge is modelled and estimated as a price
attached to a place and a time, whose designed effect is to move drivers across a map. Besbes, Castro
and Lobel's optimum induces overcongestion in some locations and indirectly prices others out. Castillo
finds the welfare gains accrue to riders while driver surplus falls. Whether the multiplier is also
individuated is contested and unmeasured. The section should not claim that surge FAILS the chapter's
third test; it should claim that surge has not been shown to PASS it, and put per-trip two-sided
pricing (Finding 1) in the "prices each match" slot instead. COMPLICATES.

**Sources:** Omar Besbes, Francisco Castro, and Ilan Lobel, "Surge Pricing and Its Spatial Supply
Response," *Management Science* 67, no. 3 (2021): 1350–1367. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2020.3622.
https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2020.3622 ; Alice Lu, Peter I. Frazier, and Oren Kislev, "Surge Pricing
Moves Uber's Driver-Partners," in *Proceedings of the 2018 ACM Conference on Economics and Computation
(EC '18)* (New York: ACM, 2018), 3. DOI 10.1145/3219166.3219192.
https://doi.org/10.1145/3219166.3219192 ; Juan Camilo Castillo, "Who Benefits From Surge Pricing?"
*Econometrica* 93, no. 5 (2025): 1811–1854. DOI 10.3982/ECTA19106. https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA19106

**Evidence:** Besbes, Castro and Lobel, abstract, verbatim: "We consider the pricing problem faced by a
revenue-maximizing platform matching price-sensitive customers to flexible supply units within a
geographic area… We propose a two-dimensional framework in which a platform selects prices for
different locations and drivers respond by choosing where to relocate, in equilibrium, based on prices,
travel costs, and driver congestion levels… In the optimal solution, the platform applies different
treatments to different locations. In some locations, prices are set so that supply and demand are
perfectly matched; overcongestion is induced in other locations, and some less profitable locations are
indirectly priced out." Lu, Frazier and Kislev, abstract, verbatim: "Ability to see the surge heatmap
explains 10%-60% of Uber drivers' self-positioning decisions, attracts drivers toward areas with higher
surge prices, and increases drivers' revenue on surged trips by up to 70%." Castillo, abstract,
verbatim: "I find that, relative to a uniform pricing counterfactual in which Uber sets the overall
price level, surge pricing increases total welfare by 2.15% of gross revenue. Welfare effects differ
substantially across sides of the market: rider surplus increases by 3.57% of gross revenue, whereas
driver surplus and the platform's current profits decrease by 0.98% and 0.50% of gross revenue,
respectively. Riders at all income levels benefit. Among drivers, those who work long hours are hurt
the most, especially women."

*The disagreement, unsmoothed:* two lenses confirmed all three sources bibliographically and verbatim,
and accepted the original claim that surge behaves like a setting on the chapter's own criteria. The
third lens refused the inference, on three grounds worth carrying. (1) Besbes et al. write "We PROPOSE
a two-dimensional framework" — that is their stylised model, not a description of what Uber does, and
price-per-location is a modelling assumption rather than a finding. (2) A model that abstracts away
from who the two parties are cannot establish that the real mechanism does not know who they are.
Absence in the abstraction is not absence in the world. (3) The claim set contradicts itself: Finding 5
below quotes Dubal at 1949 asserting that "the surge multiplier presented to Diego may differ from the
multiplier presented to Marta, even if both workers are working in the same area at the same time." C5
and C6 cannot both stand as originally written. The corrected wording above is the third lens's and this
file adopts it; the chapter gains the sharper distinction anyway (surge is a setting the platform
builds; the fare split is a selector's act) without asserting more than the economics establishes.

*Verifier corrections carried:* the caveat originally attached to the Lu, Frazier and Kislev quotation
can be struck — two lenses independently confirmed the sentence against the indexed publisher abstract
and OpenAlex confirms first_page = last_page = 3. Note that this is a one-page extended abstract; do not
cite it as if it were a full paper. Castillo's published abstract was matched against Crossref and
OpenAlex reconstructions rather than the Econometrica page (econometricsociety.org returns 403 to
automated fetch); confirm against the journal page before quoting.

## Finding 5 — Assert differential pay, not profile-based personalisation (high, 2-1) [C6]

What the evidence supports: workers doing the same work for the same company at the same time receive
different pay, and the platform's cut varies trip by trip and driver by driver. What it does not
support as fact: that the platform prices the individual on the basis of what it knows about that
individual. Dubal, who coined "algorithmic wage discrimination," marks personalisation as an inference
("appear to be"), reasoning by analogy from consumer price discrimination; Uber denies it; Dubal says
she cannot adjudicate the denial. The section should attribute personalisation-by-profile to Dubal as
an inference she herself flags, note the denial, and stop there. BOTH.

**Sources:** Veena Dubal, "On Algorithmic Wage Discrimination," *Columbia Law Review* 123, no. 7 (2023):
1929–1992. https://www.columbialawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Dubal-On_Algorithmic_Wage_discrimination.pdf
; Akshat Pandey and Aylin Caliskan, "Disparate Impact of Artificial Intelligence Bias in Ridehailing
Economy's Price Discrimination Algorithms," in *Proceedings of the 2021 AAAI/ACM Conference on AI,
Ethics, and Society (AIES '21)* (New York: ACM, 2021), 822–833. DOI 10.1145/3461702.3462561.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04599

**Evidence:** Dubal at 1949, verbatim: "While Part II details some of these wage manipulators, the
relevant point here is that these are not the same for every driver, nor are they the same across time.
For example, the surge multiplier presented to Diego may differ from the multiplier presented to Marta,
even if both workers are working in the same area at the same time. The bonus offer that Ahmed receives
on any given week is not the same as the one Sanjeev receives. The reasons underlying these differences
are opaque—the logic hidden inside black-box algorithms. But based on what is known about price
discrimination in the consumer context, these wage manipulators appear to be personalized based on what
Uber's machine learning systems know about the habits, practices, and income targets of individual
workers." Dubal at 1935–36 n.19, verbatim: "Uber, for its part, has stated that 'suggestions that Uber
offers variable pricing based on user-profiling is completely unfounded and factually incorrect.' … We
have no way to judge the accuracy of this statement." What Dubal asserts flatly, at 1936, verbatim:
"But algorithmic wage discrimination also creates a labor market in which people who are doing the same
work, with the same skill, for the same company, at the same time may receive different hourly pay.
Digitally personalized wages are often determined through obscure, complex systems that make it nearly
impossible for workers to predict or understand their constantly changing, and frequently declining,

ever-changing formulas using granular data on location, individual behavior, demand, supply, or other

Pandey and Caliskan, abstract, verbatim, showing the unit of analysis is the census tract and not the

higher poverty levels, younger residents, and high education levels are significantly associated with

## Finding 6 — A court has ruled on what counts as human review of a removal (high, 2-1) [C8]

The Amsterdam Court of Appeal held that Uber's deactivations of three drivers were based solely on
automated processing. File notes made by Risk-team staff in Kraków "in no way show that this act was
much more than a purely symbolic act"; the drivers were never heard; Uber never disclosed the staff's
qualifications. That triggered the drivers' Article 15(1)(h) GDPR right to know how the decision was
reached. For a fourth driver the court found otherwise, because Uber stated, undisputed, that a
personal conversation had preceded his deactivation. The remedy was disclosure within a month on pain
of €4,000 a day, not reinstatement. SUPPORTS.

**Sources:** Gerechtshof Amsterdam, beschikking of 4 April 2023, case no. 200.295.742/01,

Court of Appeal," 4 April 2023.
https://www.workerinfoexchange.org/post/historic-digital-rights-win-for-wie-and-the-adcu-over-uber-and-ola-at-amsterdam-court-of-appeal

## Finding 7 — Removal became a contested legal object in the United States, on two different fronts (high for the Ninth Circuit, medium for the TLC, 2-1) [C9]

In March 2026 a federal appellate panel affirmed the denial of a preliminary injunction against
Seattle's App-Based Worker Deactivation Rights Ordinance, holding that the ordinance "regulates
nonexpressive conduct—the unwarranted deactivation of worker accounts," over Uber's and Instacart's
argument that it compelled speech. Separately and for a different power: the New York City Taxi and
Limousine Commission, in a proposed rule, asserts that platforms have "restricted platform access for
drivers who were already working a shift" in order to lift the utilization rate that sets minimum pay,
and proposes a 16-hour access guarantee. That is a lockout used as a pricing instrument, with
disciplinary deactivation expressly carved out. SUPPORTS.

**Sources:** *Uber Technologies, Inc. v. City of Seattle*, Nos. 25-228 & 25-231 (9th Cir. filed 4 March
2026) (Clifton, J.; Bennett, J., dissenting in part; Graber, J.), on appeal from No. 2:24-cv-02103-MJP
(W.D. Wash., Pechman, J.), argued 10 July 2025.
https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/03/04/25-228.pdf ; New York City Taxi and
Limousine Commission, "Notice of Public Hearing and Opportunity to Comment on Proposed Rules: Amendment
of Driver Pay Rules for High Volume For Hire Vehicle Service" (Ref. No. TLC-154), preliminarily
certified 26 December 2024.
https://rules.cityofnewyork.us/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/TLC-proposed-Amendment-of-Driver-Pay-Rules-for-HVFHS-Preliminarily-Certified-12.26.24-clean.pdf

**Evidence:** Ninth Circuit, court-staff summary, verbatim: "The panel affirmed the district court's
denial of a motion by Uber Technologies, Inc. and Maplebear Inc. for a preliminary injunction to enjoin
the City of Seattle from enforcing Seattle's App-Based Worker Deactivation Rights Ordinance (the
'Ordinance'), which applies to network companies that provide a platform for customers to hire
temporary workers and prohibits unwarranted deactivations of these app-based workers' accounts." And:
"The panel held that the Ordinance does not regulate speech subject to protection under the First
Amendment because the Ordinance regulates nonexpressive conduct—the unwarranted deactivation of worker
accounts. The fact that the Ordinance, in plaintiffs' words, necessarily 'compel[s] and dictate[s] the
content of a written communication' does not transform a law regulating nonexpressive activity into one
that infringes speech, and any burden on speech is incidental." Opinion body, pp. 12–13, verbatim:
"Here, the conduct that draws the remedy is the unwarranted deactivation of worker accounts. When
conduct is nonexpressive, as is the case here, it is not subject to First Amendment scrutiny." The two
requirements at issue, verbatim from the summary: "Section 8.40.050.A of the Ordinance requires (1)
that a network company 'inform' any app-based worker 'in writing' of the company's deactivation policy

## Finding 8 — The people a platform selects experience it as a source of choice (medium, 2-1) [C11]

Cameron's seven-year study of ride-hailing finds that many workers report liking the work and finding
choice in it, and that the algorithm manufactures consent through choices made frequent and narrow —
consent that "can mask the more-structurally problematic elements of the work." The choices in question
are about how to work and how to game the system, not about who one is matched with. The contrast the
chapter wants still holds — a cinema audience is given no choice at all by a cut and does not resent it
— but the section must draw it against the management regime, not against the act of selection.
COMPLICATES.

**Sources:** Lindsey D. Cameron, "The Making of the 'Good Bad' Job: How Algorithmic Management
Manufactures Consent Through Constant and Confined Choices," *Administrative Science Quarterly* 69, no.
2 (2024): 458–514. DOI 10.1177/00018392241236163.
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00018392241236163

**Evidence:** Abstract, p. 458, verbatim: "While most research has argued that the task standardization
and surveillance that accompany algorithmic management will give rise to the quintessential 'bad job'
(Kalleberg, Reskin, and Hudson, 2000; Kalleberg, 2011), I find that, surprisingly, many workers report
liking and finding choice while working under algorithmic management. … I begin by showing how
algorithms segment the work at multiple sites of human–algorithm interactions and how this
configuration of the work process allows for more-frequent and narrow choice." Same abstract, verbatim:
"However, this choice-based consent can mask the more-structurally problematic elements of the work,
contributing to the growing popularity of what I call the 'good bad' job." Method, per the abstract: "a
seven-year qualitative study of the largest sector in the gig economy, the ride-hailing industry."

*The disagreement, unsmoothed:* two lenses confirmed the citation and both quotations verbatim against
the publisher abstract as indexed (SAGE returns 403 to every automated route). The third lens accepted
the quotations and rejected the reading on two counts, both of which this file adopts. Quantifier:
"many workers" is not "the platform selector is experienced by those it selects," and a seven-year
interpretive study is not designed to yield a population claim. Referent: Cameron's subject is the
algorithmic MANAGEMENT regime — when and where to work, which nudges to follow, how to game the inputs —
not the selector's act of joining two parties; and her abstract finds consent produced by both
engagement tactics and DEVIANCE tactics, "in which individuals manipulate their input into the
algorithmic management system." Deviance is choice taken against the system, which evidences felt
compulsion rather than its absence.

*Verifier caveat:* the incidental method details circulating in the project's notes — the "RideHail"
pseudonym, participant observation as a driver, data collected in 2016 and 2023 — sit in the article
body, which nobody on this project has read. Do not print them without the SAGE full text.

## Absences (searched for, not found)

**Nobody has theorised platform matching as cinematic selection.** Two scholarly corpora were searched
for any work analogising algorithmic matching to film editing, montage, or a director's choice of whom
the viewer sees next: Consensus ("platform matching compared to film editing or cinematic montage as
selection of who encounters whom," 20 results) and Scholar Gateway full text ("Has anyone theorised
platform algorithmic matching by analogy with cinematic editing, montage, or the film director's
selection of which character the viewer sees next?", 10 results). The returns split cleanly. Film-theory
papers on montage never mention platforms (Furstenau 2020, JCMS; Roque do Vale 2022; Oja 2024,
Semiotica); platform and matching papers never mention cinema (Pettersen et al. 2023 on dating-platform
matchmaker models; Ivarsson et al. 2020 on digital matchmaking). The one hybrid, Noronha 2021,
"Montagem eletrônica" (AVANCA|CINEMA), runs the analogy the other way, treating platforms as editors of
video content rather than of encounters. The setting/selector distinction appears to be unoccupied
ground and the novelty claim is available. Two caveats: both corpora are journal-weighted and would
miss a monograph or a film-studies chapter, and a planned second angle on the specific film-theory
terms (suture + recommender system; Bordwell/Branigan narration + platform mediation) never ran because
its search budget was exhausted. State the novelty claim with the search terms attached.

**The "reads the parties" power is the section's weakest joint, and the weakness is in matching, not
pricing.** No published engineering account describes ride-hail dispatch as reading rider or driver
identity to choose the pair. Uber's marketplace page describes batching ("In the seconds after a rider
requests a ride, we evaluate nearby drivers and riders in one batch"; "closest doesn't always mean
quickest"); its Fulfillment Platform post describes capturing "a consumer's intent" and matching it
"with the right set of providers"; Lyft's peer-reviewed account (Azagirre et al., *INFORMS Journal on
Applied Analytics* 54, no. 1: 71–83, DOI 10.1287/inte.2023.0083) describes a reinforcement-learning
value function over marketplace state. Across every Uber and Lyft account retrieved, the published
matching objective is collective wait time or marketplace value, not per-person targeting. If the
section says platforms read the parties and change what the MATCHING does accordingly, the load-bearing
evidence sits on the pricing side (Finding 1; the Amsterdam profiling order; Gomes and Pavan on
customized pricing as theory), not the matching side. Say so, or move the claim.

**Nobody has restudied the fifteen-second decision under the current interface.** Rosenblat and Stark
(2016) and Lee et al. (2015) studied the destination-concealed card. Binns et al. (2025) audits pay and
allocation outcomes, not the moment of the offer. No replication of the 2015–16 interface studies
against the 2023–26 card was found — nothing asks what a driver actually does with fifteen seconds, a
cross street and a number. The chapter cannot say the fifteen-second decision has been studied under
current conditions. This is a clean, nameable gap.

**Deactivation as an event is unmeasured.** Searches on deactivation, due process and account removal
returned regulation (Australia's Fair Work Digital Labour Platform Deactivation Code, in force 25
February 2025; EU Directive Arts. 10–11; Seattle Ordinance 126878) and NGO documentation, but no
peer-reviewed study whose object is the deactivation decision itself, with base rates, appeal outcomes
or reinstatement rates. In the literature deactivation appears as a threat that shapes behaviour
(Cameron 2024; Rahman 2021), not as a measured event. No platform publishes counts: Uber's deactivation
pages, its US/Canada Community Guidelines (last modified 8/11/2025) and its Seattle Deactivations
Policy (31 December 2024) state grounds, notice and a challenge route but give no number of
deactivations, appeals or reversals. The only mechanism located that compels those counts is the
Seattle Office of Labor Standards reporting schema published 10 June 2025, whose Report 5 requires
weekly "num_deactivations," "num_reinstatements," "num_deactivations_no_challenge" and
"num_deactivations_challenged_by_worker," filed quarterly over SFTP
(https://www.seattle.gov/documents/Departments/LaborStandards/ReportingGuideForNetworkCompanies_DeactivationRightsOrdinance_6.10_ADA.pdf).
No published output from that series was found. The claim that the removal power is under-studied
relative to the pricing and reading powers holds.

**California has no statutory deactivation due process, and several secondary sources say otherwise.**
An angle converted the official ballot-pamphlet text of Proposition 22
(https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2020/general/pdf/topl-prop22.pdf) to text and searched it: the string
"deactivat" occurs exactly once in the roughly 8,160-word enacted text, at Bus. & Prof. Code § 7456(a),
in the antidiscrimination provision. The whole of the operative protection is § 7452: "(b) A network
company shall not terminate a contract with an app-based driver unless based upon a ground specified in
the contract. (c) Network companies shall provide an appeals process for app-based drivers whose
contracts are terminated by the network company." No notice period, no written-reasons requirement, no
standard for the appeal. And § 7464(b)(4) preempts every California city from legislating on "App-based
driver rights with respect to a network company's termination of an app-based driver's contract."
Law-firm and news sources asserting that Prop 22 requires written deactivation policies, notice of
reasons and an opportunity to appeal are wrong; cite the ballot text. (Rideshare Drivers United sued in
San Francisco Superior Court in April 2026 alleging Uber never built the appeals system; the CalMatters
report of that suit came through a page-summarizer and needs re-checking before use.)

**No reciprocal channel is documented on the rider's side.** A search of Uber's rider-facing help
material found nothing disclosing to the rider what the driver is shown, what the driver is paid, or
that the two prices are computed independently. Binns et al. establish that the driver cannot learn the
rider's fare and is barred from asking; no source was found establishing that the converse channel
exists either. The chapter's line that nothing in the design lets a rider and a driver settle anything
between themselves is well supported on the driver's side and unrefuted but undocumented on the
rider's. Say which direction the evidence runs.

**There is no scholarly name for the fused power of selecting the pair and pricing the transaction.**
Economics splits it into "matching design" and "price customization" (Gomes and Pavan, *RAND Journal of
Economics* 55, no. 2: 230–265, DOI 10.1111/1756-2171.12464: "the profit-maximizing matching plans under
customized pricing"); operations research calls them "the two key levers" (Yan, Zhu, Korolko and
Woodard, all then at Uber, *Naval Research Logistics* 67, no. 8: 705–724, DOI 10.1002/nav.21872);
sociology and law name the pricing power ("algorithmic wage discrimination") but not the fusion.
Ajunwa's *tertius bifrons* is the nearest coined term but is defined for automated HIRING platforms
only — "automated hiring platforms, which are customizable at the behest of the employer – but never
that of the applicant – belong to this new category of brokers that I term the tertius bifrons" — and
the chapter should not silently extend it to ride-hail or delivery matching. Naming the combined power
looks like an available contribution rather than a term to borrow.

control, including in response to unexpected events. We found that, when managers used the tools to

uncompleted project outcomes." The claim spliced them into one joined by "but." And the generalisation

two studies of one freelancing market — least of all when the companion study describes that very
platform contractually forbidding the parties to settle payment between themselves. If a limit is
wanted, say the narrower true thing: Rahman and Valentine find that coercive use of the platform's own
control tools backfired and projects went uncompleted, whereas "collaborative repair" produced completed
ones. That is a finding about how control succeeds, not about parties escaping the selector. (Öborn,

## How this lands in section 5

Three things change, and one of them is a rebuild.

The rebuild is the pricing power. Section 5 currently illustrates "prices each match" with surge, and
surge is the wrong example. In the economics literature surge is a price attached to a place and a time
whose designed effect is to move drivers across a map; Besbes, Castro and Lobel's optimum induces
overcongestion in some locations and prices others out, which acts on everyone in a zone and does not
turn on who these two people are. Put Binns et al. in that slot instead. Since Q1 2023 in London the
passenger's price and the driver's fee "vary independently of each other," the driver cannot see the
fare and Uber bars him from asking, and across 1.5 million trips Uber's median cut rose from 25% to 29%
while the mean held at 25%. That is a per-match price, computed twice, disclosed to neither party in
full. It carries two of the section's three powers at once, and it upgrades the sentence about parties
not settling anything between themselves from a gap in the design to a prohibition with a penalty
behind it. Surge does not leave the section; it changes job, becoming the example of a price that
behaves like a setting, which sharpens the setting/selector distinction rather than blunting it. Do not
write that surge fails the third test. Write that it has not been shown to pass it.

Two sentences get corrected rather than strengthened. The Stark and Pais quotation is half fabricated:
"rankings but not ranks" is real and sits on p. 49, "accounts but no accountability" is not in the
article at all. Quote the two passages separately with their own page cites, or better, replace the
second with the p. 49 sentence the section actually needs — "Triangular rather than vertical, and not
as a panopticon, the lines of vision in algorithmic management are not lines of supervision." And "only
the algorithm sees the queue" needs a qualifier: at airports Uber shows a dynamic estimated wait time
computed partly from how many drivers are ahead, though not the queue or the driver's place in it.

The removal power is the section's strongest ground and it gains a spine. The Amsterdam Court of Appeal
found that file notes written in Kraków by staff whose qualifications Uber never disclosed, about
drivers who were never heard, are "not much more than a purely symbolic act" — and found the opposite
for the one driver Uber had spoken to first. Three months of American litigation says the same thing
from the other side: a federal appellate panel held that deactivating a worker's account is nonexpressive
conduct a city may regulate, over the platforms' argument that their deactivation policy was speech. The
chapter's closing line about a distribution chain "made of people who could be argued with" now has its
exact modern counterpart, and the counterpart is litigated rather than asserted. Two limits must travel
with it: the Dutch remedy was disclosure, not reinstatement, and the American case is preliminary-injunction
posture. Cite New York separately and for a different power — mid-shift lockouts used to lift the
utilisation rate that sets minimum pay, with disciplinary deactivation expressly carved out.

Three claims must come down in altitude. Do not assert that platforms price the individual on the basis
of a profile: assert differential pay for the same work at the same time, which is evidenced, and give
personalisation to Dubal as the inference she marks it as, with Uber's denial and her refusal to
adjudicate it. Do not say the EU directive is silent on pricing: it names "the pricing of individual
assignments" in the definition of the systems it governs, and what it declines to do is fix the price,
not see it. And meet Cameron's objection head-on rather than absorbing it — many ride-hail workers
report liking the work and finding choice in it, and consent is manufactured by choices made frequent
and narrow. The contrast the section wants survives, but it runs between the cut and the management
regime, not between the cut and the match. Draw it there.

Finally, the novelty claim is available and should be stated with its search terms attached. Two
scholarly corpora returned nothing that analogises platform matching to cinematic selection: film
montage papers never mention platforms, matching papers never mention cinema, and the one hybrid runs
the analogy backwards. The section's weakest joint is elsewhere — no published engineering account
describes ride-hail dispatch as reading the parties in order to choose the pair. The evidence for
reading the parties sits on the pricing side. Say so in the section, or move the claim there.
