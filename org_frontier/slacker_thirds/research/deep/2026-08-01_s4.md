## Finding 4 — Bordwell 1985 denies not the personified narrator but any sender at all, and grants narration every function a selector performs (high on the wording and pages by convergence, no page image obtained; 3-0) [C4]

The project note undersells Bordwell, and the shortfall lands on a load-bearing sentence.
`chapter_v2.md` says he "warned against picturing the camera as a little storyteller with intentions"
and then rests the argument on "the part both sides grant." At p. 62 Bordwell denies any sender
whatever, not merely a personified one — and in the same passage he grants that narration does
everything the chapter's selector does: it suppresses information, restricts knowledge, generates
curiosity, creates a tone. That list is the chapter's best single piece of evidence that narration is
what selects. The no-sender sentence is the price: Bordwell would refuse even "the camera chooses."
BOTH.

## Finding 5 — The rival-accounts map is wrong in two directions; Chatman's narrator is already depersonalised, and the split is film scholars versus philosophers of film (high on the sources, one hinge word struck; 2-1) [C5]

Two repairs to `chapter_v2.md`'s "Film scholars have fought over this" paragraph. First, Chatman's
cinematic narrator is explicitly not a person — a composite of communicating devices — so the dispute
between him and Bordwell is about whether an impersonal system counts as an *agent*, not about whether
the selecting is systematic. Second, the field is not two camps of equal size. Slugan's own survey has
the narrator view predominating among film scholars, with Bordwell the most prominent exception; the
lively disagreement sits among philosophers of film, where Currie, Kania and Slugan argue against
fictional narrators. Third, what those skeptics grant is narration by the actual filmmakers, not by any
in-film agent — which is the fallback the chapter needs and cannot get from Bordwell. BOTH.

The claim also proposed "present"/"presenter" as a neutral superordinate the chapter could stand on.
Lens 3 refused it, decisively: Chatman introduces "to present" precisely in order to conclude that
presenting entails an agent — "narration, narrative presentation, entails an agent even when the agent
bears no signs of human personality" — and Bordwell's p. 62 sentence denies any sender. A presenter is a
sender. Adopting "presenter" as the hinge smuggles Chatman's conclusion into a sentence meant to stand
between them. Struck. Lenses 1 and 2 confirmed the sources without testing the hinge word.

Lens 3 also refused "Bordwell as the standing exception" as an over-reading produced by truncating
Slugan's endnote, and that correction is applied below.

## Finding 6 — Poulaki is a stranger rival than the project note says, but her camera describes rather than selects (high on the quotations and pages, narrowed on the inference; 2-1) [C7]

informational linkage, which means the chapter's current rebuttal — nobody in *Slacker* meets twice —
aims at a weaker opponent than she is. Her system also has a floor the chapter can use: with no
relations between elements you have a heap, not a system. BOTH.

editing." Lenses 1 and 2 verified every quotation and page and did not test the inference; both called

## Finding 10 — Münsterberg 1916 is an ally of the training thesis, not the rival the claim proposed (high on the text, bearing REVERSED; 2-1, with the reversing lens right) [C11]

The claim offered Münsterberg's close-up passage as a century-old rival to the chapter's training
thesis: the close-up as the viewer's own act of attention externalised, so a 1991 audience following
*Slacker*'s handoffs would be watching its own attention played back rather than learning to follow a
stranger's choices. Lens 3 read the surrounding chapter and reversed it, and the reversal is right.

Ten pages earlier in the same chapter Münsterberg writes that in art voluntary attention is eliminated
and the audience follows a lead that comes from the work itself; he distinguishes voluntary from
involuntary attention and says the guiding influence in the latter "comes from without"; he dismisses a
spectator who exercises her own selection as one who breaks the spell; and the close-up sentence itself
credits the naming to the producers. His claim is that the technique reproduces the *form* of an
attentive act, not that the spectator controls what is shown. Chapter IV is one of the earliest
statements of the training thesis, not a rival to it. Lenses 1 and 2 verified every word and every page
anchor and endorsed the original bearing; the text they verified is what defeats it. SUPPORTS (bearing
reversed from COMPLICATES).

If the chapter wants a genuine rival to the training thesis, this run did not find one.

**Sources:** Münsterberg, Hugo. *The Photoplay: A Psychological Study*. New York: D. Appleton and
Company, 1916, ch. IV "Attention" (chapter opens p. 72), pp. 76–77 and 87–88. No DOI.
https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15383/pg15383-images.html

**Evidence:** pp. 87–88, verbatim: "The act of attention which goes on in our mind has remodeled the
surrounding itself. The detail which is being watched has suddenly become the whole content of the
performance, and everything which our mind wants to disregard has been suddenly banished from our sight
and has disappeared. The events without have become obedient to the demands of our consciousness. In the

## Absences (searched for, not found)

**No published account treats *Slacker*'s shot-to-shot succession as a SELECTION problem** — as a
question of what chooses which person the viewer sees next. Searched: Crossref bibliographic queries on
*Slacker* + Linklater + narration + camera; the full 33-record Crossref contents of *ReFocus: The Films
of Richard Linklater* (2022), the only scholarly collection on the director, whose one *Slacker* chapter
(Erickson, pp. 15–33) is about production, distribution and the "Keep Austin Weird" ethos; Open Library
full-text on "Slacker"; three Consensus corpus queries on film editing as selection of which character
the viewer follows, montage as choosing what the spectator sees, and relay/baton-passing structures.
Jeong 2021 is the single near-exception found, and he attaches the agency to uncut camera movement, in
*Birdman* and *The Phantom of Liberty*, not to *Slacker* and not to the cut (see Do NOT use, C6). State
the novelty at that grain — and note that the earliest claimant to any part of it is Walters in 1990
(Finding 7), not a scholar.

**No taxonomy classifies films by how SCENES connect** — by adjacency in presentation order — rather than
by how characters relate. Berg's twelve types classify by protagonist count, temporality, or
classical-rule violation (Finding 2); Bordwell's network narrative is defined by "x degrees of separation
among several individuals"; and the graph tradition is built to erase order. Moretti states the design
choice outright: "the edges have no \"direction\"… I just couldn't find a non-clumsy way to visualize
weight and direction" and "Time turned into space" (*Network Theory, Plot Analysis*, Stanford Literary
Lab Pamphlet 2, 2011, p. 3, https://litlab.stanford.edu/LiteraryLabPamphlet2.pdf). Any star-graph or
path-graph vocabulary the chapter imports from that tradition inherits its order-blindness, which is why
the network reviewer's correction is right.

**Nobody has connected film editing to social brokerage.** Editorial selection is richly theorised as a
social act with network structure, but only under the name gatekeeping, and only for news, music, art
markets and academic journals (Bonini and Gandini 2019, DOI 10.1177/2056305119880006; Foster, Borgatti
and Jones 2011, DOI 10.1016/j.poetic.2011.05.004). A Semantic Scholar corpus search joining editorial
selection to brokerage returned twenty records, none about film editing, the cut, or montage. The
gatekeeping literature is the closest neighbour and the better one to cite.

## Do NOT use (refuted or unconfirmed this pass)

**"Kathleen Boom" (C6, 0-3). There is no such person.** The author of "On Drifts and Swerves:
Linklater's Love for Lacunae" is **Jeroen Boom**. All three lenses caught it independently: Crossref for
DOI 10.3366/edinburgh/9781474493826.003.0005 returns "Jeroen Boom"; the publisher's record, reached by
resolving the DOI to
https://academic.oup.com/edinburgh-scholarship-online/book/56685/chapter/453372500, gives "Jeroen Boom";
and the EUP book table of contents lists chapter 4 under the same name. This is the failure mode the
third lens exists to catch — a citation that is right in every element but the author.

**The C6 prior-art claim as framed (0-3).** The claim said the chapter's central move "has already been
published twice, and one of the two names *Slacker*." It has not. Jeong's camera-agent passage is about
*Birdman*: the paragraph opens "I briefly note Iñárritu's Birdman (2014) in this regard" and continues
"The entire film indeed looks like a single-shot long take, which renders palpable the camera's own
agency unlike the cross-cutting made under the director's full control." The camera-agent then travels
to Buñuel, and it is *The Phantom of Liberty*, not *Slacker*, that Jeong calls "a remarkable precursor of
the 'daisy chain plot' (Berg 2006, 24–26)." Jeong's only *Slacker* sentence is bibliographic: *Waking
Life* "conveys several parallels to his other network narrative film Slacker (1991)." Two lenses also
corrected the locator: all three camera quotations are on p. 144, not "pp. 143–144."

WHAT SURVIVES FROM C6 AND MUST BE USED. Both sources are real, both are relevant, and both must be cited
— corrected. Jeong published a camera-agent reading, of other films, tied to *uncut* movement, and he
contrasts that agency with "the cross-cutting made under the director's full control," which is the
opposite of an editing-as-selection reading and is the best available thing for section 4 to argue with.
Boom describes *Slacker*'s camera doing the weaving in agentless verbs — "Its meandering camera drifts in
an almost continuous motion through the urban fabric of Austin, Texas, eagerly traversing from one site
to another, weaving together the lives of eccentric characters and the idiosyncratic worlds they
inhabit" (abstract, verbatim) — and never gives it agency. That is the opening the chapter occupies.
Nobody has published the camera-as-selector reading of *Slacker*. Also: verify the "only scholarly
collection on Linklater" superlative before printing it; one lens flagged it and none could test it.

**The Berg quotation "jumps from one protagonist to the next, never to revisit" (C1).** Never print it
over Berg's name. Strike it from `drafting/draft_v0.md` or re-attribute it to Gay/Screenplayology.

**"Berg 2006, 24–26."** Jeong's locator, unconfirmed at source. Do not repeat it in the chapter's own

**Gabler line numbers for the *Ulysses* passage ("U 17.2127–31").** Carried no source and no lens

## How this lands in section 4

The first thing to do is a deletion, and it is not in the chapter. `drafting/draft_v0.md` prints a
sentence in quotation marks over Berg's name that Berg never wrote; the wording is a teaching site's
paraphrase, and three lenses ran the full-text search against the complete article to confirm the
absence. Strike it or attribute it to Gay. The chapter files are clean and the Berg footnote at note 33
stands as written. The old draft's next sentence — "The characters have no edges to each other" — must
not migrate into the chapter under Berg's authority either; Berg's own definition of the variant he puts
*Slacker* in has characters who "come into contact with each other," and his sentence makes Linklater
the agent of the linking. The two-graphs move survives intact; what changes is which sentence Berg is
answering for. He gives the type name, the example, and story-world contact. The narration/story-world
split is the chapter's own.

The VERIFY on Berg closes, and it closes better than expected: "The Daisy Chain Plot" is his section
heading, and *Slacker* is named three times, including in the introduction, where he already ties film
to type — "the absent causality in Last Days [2005], and the daisy-chain structure of Slacker." Cite as
(Ramírez Berg 2006, 5–61) with the section named, and do not print the 24–26 range that circulates via
Jeong. Drop the idea, which was in the run's own claim set, that Berg had an ordering axis available and
declined to use it on *Slacker*. His nonlinear family is about scrambled chronology, and *Slacker* runs
forward through one day. The real point stands on its own: no taxonomy in this literature sorts films by
how scenes connect in presentation order, and the graph tradition erased order on purpose, in Moretti's
own words.

The paragraph beginning "Is it even fair to talk about a camera as if it acts?" needs rewriting, and the
rewrite makes it stronger. Bordwell at p. 62 does not merely warn against personification; he denies any
sender — "This presupposes a perceiver, but not any sender, of a message" — while granting narration
every function the chapter's selector performs: it suppresses information, restricts knowledge,
generates curiosity, creates a tone. Quote that list. It is the best evidence in the section that
narration is what selects. Then stop resting on "the part both sides grant," because the map is wrong.
Chatman's cinematic narrator is already depersonalised, "the composite of a large and complex variety of
communicating devices," so the fight is over whether an impersonal system counts as an agent, not over
whether the selecting is systematic. And the field is not two camps: on Slugan's own account there is
"little debate on this matter among film scholars in general," where the narrator view predominates and
Bordwell is the most prominent exception, while the debate is "very lively among those applying analytic
philosophy to film." The chapter is leaning on the film-studies minority. Say so, and take the fallback
from the people who actually offer it — Currie, Kania and Thomson-Jones, for whom a story "is actually
told by the author in his or her creation of the work." Do not take "presenter" as a neutral hinge; it
is Chatman's word for something that entails an agent.

The Poulaki paragraph currently answers a weaker opponent than Poulaki. She does not explain coherence
"without any broker at all": she writes that the movement from one story to the next runs on "the
self-reflexive agency of the camera." Two clauses of repair earn the section a real rival. Her camera's
agency is descriptive — Chatman's "describer," a drift unmotivated by narrative — not selective, and the
sentence is about *Gomorrah*, so the chapter can grant her the drift and keep the choosing. Her
*Gomorrah* link is "primarily the setting … and the character of Maria," which is the chapter's own
setting/selector pair appearing inside her analysis and worth naming as such. And her demotion of the
diegetic encounter — interactions are "primarily informational and secondarily
anthropomorphic/characterological" — means the rebuttal "nobody in *Slacker* meets twice" does not touch
her. Answer the informational version. Her own floor helps: "A catalogue is not a system but a list of
elements; a system needs relations between the elements in order to form itself." Cite the 2011
dissertation for every one of those words; the *Screen* article's full text was never obtained, and it
is not established that it mentions *Slacker* at all. One small gain: her ch. 7 relays Bordwell's
*Slacker* verdict at p. 215, which is a second attestation of the passage the chapter quotes at note 32.

The section gains a source it did not have and loses one it thought it had. Chris Walters, in the
*Austin Chronicle* of 27 July 1990 — before national release — describes the first handoff as "The camera
eye slowly pulls back until it sees another nameless guy … and follows him upstairs into another story
already in progress," and calls the whole structure "a daisy chain," sixteen years before Berg. Use it
early in the section as the contemporaneous description of the relay. Do not use it for camera choice
(his verbs are seeing and following) and above all do not use it in section 6 as evidence that 1990
audiences followed the device comfortably, because the same review says it remains to be seen how the
film will go over with the moviegoing public. The other loss is Münsterberg, who was offered as a rival
to the training thesis and turns out to be its earliest statement: in the same chapter as the close-up
passage he writes that "voluntary attention is eliminated from the sphere of art" and that "our attention
is constantly drawn in accordance with the intentions of the producers." He is an ally. Section 6 should
recruit him; section 4 should stop looking for its rival there. This run found no genuine rival to the
training thesis.

The last two paragraphs of the section are the ones in trouble, and the trouble is evidentiary rather
than argumentative. Nothing in section 4's scene material can be printed as quotation. The published
books disagree on *Slacker*'s signature line — Rosenbaum has "not the same thing as apathy," four other
books drop "thing" — and the "underlying order is chaos" line exists in at least three wordings across
Rosenbaum, Murphy and Stone. The PixelVision party returns zero hits anywhere in the Internet Archive
book corpus, and no published source states the gauge of the final image; Rob Stone's "a montage that
recalls A Hard Days Night" is the whole published description of the ending. So: paraphrase both
paragraphs until someone watches the disc with the subtitle track, or gets the 1992 St. Martin's book,
and drop the Super-8 assertion entirely rather than defend it. The *Ulysses* recitation is the one piece
of that material that is now safe — the passage is verbatim Joyce, "Ithaca," checkable at Gutenberg — and
it turns out not to be the chapter's own discovery: Rob Stone already quotes the passage in an Austin
and *Slacker* context. Cite him, and get his page.

On novelty, the grain is now fixed and the section can state it without hedging. Nobody has published
the camera-as-selector reading of *Slacker*. Jeong published a camera-agent reading of *Birdman* and
*The Phantom of Liberty*, tied to uncut movement and explicitly contrasted with "the cross-cutting made
under the director's full control" — which is worth arguing with, since the chapter locates the selecting
partly in the cut. Jeroen Boom (not Kathleen; the name in the run's own claim set was wrong) describes
*Slacker*'s camera weaving lives together in agentless verbs and never gives it agency. And the earliest
claimant to any of it is a local critic writing in July 1990.
