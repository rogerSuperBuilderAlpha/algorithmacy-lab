## Finding 1 — Schegloff supplies the binding half of the section's asymmetry and contradicts the cheap half (high on the text, contested on the inference; 2-1) [C6]

The section's mechanism sentence — "once an encounter is opened, it costs more to close than to endure" —
has a named source for its first half and an obstacle in the same paper for its second. Schegloff's 1968
account of conversational openings argues that a summons–answer sequence is *nonterminal*: answering
commits the answerer to staying in the encounter, which is exactly the trap the moon-landing monologue
exploits. Two lenses passed the claim as written, that Schegloff supplies both halves of the asymmetry.
The third lens refused, and its refusal is a textual argument from quotations all three lenses
independently verified, so the corrected version is adopted here. Schegloff's second passage does not say
that declining is cheap. It says the opposite: a member "may not 'naively choose'" not to answer, because
in co-presence the excusing inference of physical or interactional absence is blocked, so cold-shouldering
or insult gets inferred instead. That is a cost on declining, in precisely the co-present case *Slacker*
stages. Schegloff establishes a cost on both sides of an opening. If the chapter wants an asymmetry it
must argue for it *against* Schegloff, not from him. Two further limits the third lens is right to press:
Schegloff's co-present illustrations are acquainted parties (a mother calling her child; acquaintances
cold-shouldering each other), not strangers opening an encounter, and his corpus is telephone calls to a
police complaint desk. The sentence in the claim about civil inattention has no support in Schegloff —
he cites Goffman 1963 twice, for being "with each other" and for being "in play," and nowhere for civil
inattention or its scope. BOTH: it supports the binding of an opened encounter and complicates the
cheapness of declining one.

**Evidence:** p. 362, verbatim: "Nonterminality is an outcome of the obligation of the summoner to talk

## Finding 2 — the conventions were enforced at an officer's discretion, and the section's own claim about racial policing has a legal record (high on the sources, contested on the legal inference; 2-1) [C9]

The section already says that the same code licensing one body to speak polices others. That claim has a
documentary record, and the record is more precise than the draft. In 1972 the Supreme Court struck down
Jacksonville's vagrancy ordinance — the instrument that criminalized exactly what *Slacker*'s characters
do, loitering, strolling, wandering, accosting — as void for vagueness. Two lenses passed the claim as
written. The third refuted its legal characterization on two grounds and is correct on both, so its
version is adopted. Douglas did *not* create a constitutional right to loiter; the opinion says so in the
sentence the claim itself quotes, "They are not mentioned in the Constitution or in the Bill of Rights."
And the holding rests on two grounds, not one: failure to give fair notice, *and* the encouragement of
arbitrary and erratic arrests. The word in the opinion is "unfettered," not "unreviewable." What survives,
and it is what the section needs, is that until Papachristou the conventions licensing a stranger to
address a stranger in public were enforceable at an officer's discretion against "poor people,
nonconformists, dissenters, idlers" — and that the petitioners in the case had been arrested riding four
to a car, two white women and two Black men. The same discretionary structure operates in commercial
gatekeeping: May's audit-pair study, whose sites include Austin, documents facially neutral dress codes,
steering and fabricated guest lists at nightclub doors. COMPLICATES.

**Sources:** *Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156 (1972), opinion of the Court (Douglas,
J.), official U.S. Reports PDF,
https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep405/usrep405156/usrep405156.pdf (pp. 158, 159,
164, 170) ; Reuben A. Buford May, "Exploring the Use of Exclusionary Practices Against African American
Participation in Urban Nightlife," *Sociological Forum* 37, no. 1 (2022): 91–110,
https://doi.org/10.1111/socf.12780

## Finding 3 — exposure among strangers is not exposure across difference (high on the quotations, contested on the target; 2-1) [C8]

Three empirical literatures bear on what a dense city actually delivers, and the honest reading is
narrower than the run's headline. Nilforoshan and colleagues measure 1.6 billion real-world exposures
among 9.6 million people across 382 US metros and find exposure segregation 67 percent *higher* in the ten
largest metros than in metros under 100,000, because large cities offer a greater choice of differentiated
spaces targeted at particular socioeconomic groups. Fan and colleagues find at street scale that the
concentration of visitors explains only 26 percent of experienced social mixing, while adjacent amenities,
residential diversity and income level account for 44 percent. Two lenses passed the finding as framed
against the section's premise that a city "puts strangers within reach of each other." The third refused,
and its objection holds: these studies measure the *composition* of urban encounters, not their
availability. Nilforoshan et al.'s design presupposes billions of cross-person exposures and reports that
in large metros those exposures are less socioeconomically diverse — reach is what their data assumes. The
third lens also caught an omission that restores the section's setting logic almost exactly: the same
abstract reports that the segregation-increasing effect "is countered when a city's hubs (such as shopping
centres) are positioned to bridge diverse neighbourhoods," and concludes that "urban design can both
prevent and facilitate encounters among diverse individuals." That sentence is a gift to the section, not
a threat to it. COMPLICATES — but a different claim than the one the section makes.

**Sources:** Hamed Nilforoshan, Wenli Looi, Emma Pierson, Blanca Villanueva, Nic Fishman, Yiling Chen,
John Sholar, Beth Redbird, David Grusky, and Jure Leskovec, "Human mobility networks reveal increased
segregation in large cities," *Nature* 624, no. 7992 (2023): 586–592,
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06757-3 (open text at https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC10733138) ;
Zhuangyuan Fan, Tianyu Su, Maoran Sun, Ariel Noyman, Fan Zhang, Alex "Sandy" Pentland, and Esteban Moro,
"Diversity beyond density: Experienced social mixing of urban streets," *PNAS Nexus* 2, no. 4 (2023):
pgad077, https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgad077 ; Jan K. Brueckner and Ann G. Largey, "Social
interaction and urban sprawl," *Journal of Urban Economics* 64, no. 1 (2008): 18–34,
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jue.2007.08.002 (read as the October 2006 UC Irvine working paper,
https://www.economics.uci.edu/files/docs/workingpapers/2006-07/Brueckner-07.pdf, §5, printed folio 17)

**Evidence:** Nilforoshan et al., abstract verbatim: "A long-standing expectation is that large, dense and
cosmopolitan areas support socioeconomic mixing and exposure among diverse individuals. … Using mobile
phone mobility data to represent 1.6 billion real-world exposures among 9.6 million people in the United
States, we measure exposure segregation across 382 metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs) and 2,829
counties. We find that exposure segregation is 67% higher in the ten largest MSAs than in small MSAs with
fewer than 100,000 residents. This means that, contrary to expectations, residents of large cosmopolitan

increased socioeconomic segregation in large cities arises because they offer a greater choice of

Fan et al., abstract verbatim: "We conduct multiple experiments and show that the concentration of visitors
only explains 26% of street-level ESM. However, adjacent amenities, residential diversity, and income
level account for 44% of the ESM."

Brueckner & Largey, §5 (working paper, folio 17): "The results are unfavorable: whether the focus is
friendship-oriented social interaction or measures of group involvement, the empirical results show a
negative, rather than positive, effect of density on interaction."

## Finding 4 — the agglomeration gloss in the section is wrong in three ways, and Duranton and Puga say so themselves (high on the quotations, contested on the "fragile" reading; 2-1) [C4]

The section's one-line gloss — density improves everyone's odds of a good match without anyone proposing
one — misstates the source it cites. Duranton and Puga's matching argument splits into two distinct
sub-mechanisms: §3.1 concerns match *quality*, after Helsley and Strange 1990, and §3.2 concerns match
*probability*. The driver in both is workforce size *L* and the endogenous number of firms, not density;
lens 3 counted the word "density" seven times in the whole chapter. And the authors themselves warn that
sharing, matching and learning generate outcomes "observationally equivalent in most (but not all)
respects," which makes it very difficult to attribute any measured agglomeration benefit to matching in
particular. On one point the run overreached and lens 3 is right. Calling the probability half
"theoretically fragile" quotes a setup as a verdict: Duranton and Puga do write that most micro-foundations
"do not yield an aggregate matching function with increasing returns to scale," and then two paragraphs
later adopt the Coles/Coles-and-Smith foundation, derive a matching function that *does* exhibit increasing
returns, and build §3.2 on it. The same correction kills the empirical limb. Donovan et al.'s 0.015–0.039
range is a *productivity* elasticity, not an odds-of-a-good-match elasticity, and Duranton and Puga's own
observational-equivalence warning is exactly why that number cannot be assigned to the matching mechanism.
Mion and Naticchioni supply the version of the point the section actually wants — and cut against it at the
same time, since they find assortative matching negatively related to local market size. BOTH.

**Sources:** Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga, "Chapter 48 Micro-foundations of urban agglomeration
economies," in *Handbook of Regional and Urban Economics*, vol. 4, *Cities and Geography*, ed. J. Vernon
Henderson and Jacques-François Thisse (Amsterdam: North-Holland/Elsevier, 2004), 2063–2117,
https://doi.org/10.1016/S1574-0080(04)80005-1 (authors' draft of 11 June 2003 read in full at
https://diegopuga.org/papers/urbanagg.pdf; ScienceDirect returns 403, so printed Handbook page numbers for
these sentences are UNCONFIRMED) ; Stuart Donovan, Thomas de Graaff, Henri L. F. de Groot, and Carl C.
Koopmans, "Unraveling urban advantages—A meta-analysis of agglomeration economies," *Journal of Economic
Surveys* 38, no. 1 (2024): 168–200, https://doi.org/10.1111/joes.12543 ; Giordano Mion and Paolo
Naticchioni, "The spatial sorting and matching of skills and firms," *Canadian Journal of Economics* 42,
no. 1 (2009): 28–55, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5982.2008.01498.x

**Evidence:** Duranton & Puga §3.1 (draft line 1022, inside "Improving the quality of matches"): "The first
is a matching externality first highlighted by Helsley and Strange (1990), whereby an increase in the number
of agents trying to match improves the expected quality of each match." §3.2 (line 1363, inside "Improving
the chances of matching"): "There are various approaches to providing micro-foundations for an aggregate
matching function. However, most of them do not yield an aggregate matching function with increasing
returns to scale." Line 1380: "This aggregate matching function exhibits decreasing returns to scale."
§5 (line 2077): "These mechanisms generate final outcomes that are observationally equivalent in most (but
not all) respects. … this equivalence is also partly bad news because empirically identifying and
separating these mechanisms becomes very difficult." §5 (line 2087): "heterogeneity (of workers and firms)

by the derivation and by "This aggregate matching function exhibits increasing returns to scale in the

Donovan et al., publisher-deposited abstract: "We find that elasticities are likely to lie in the range
0.015–0.039 and, like earlier reviews, that the controls enabled by detailed data are associated with

## Absences (searched for, not found)

**The sustained uninvited monologue has no empirical interaction literature.** *Slacker*'s characteristic
speech act — a long uninvited address delivered to a stranger who did not solicit it — is undescribed.
Searches across Scholar Gateway and Consensus for studies of people delivering long uninvited speeches to
strangers in public returned only adjacent literatures, and every one of them studies a SHORT opening turn:
panhandling (Leeson, Hardy & Suarez 2025, DOI 10.1002/soej.12792; Llewellyn & Burrow 2008; Dromi 2012, DOI
10.1111/j.1573-7861.2012.01359.x), street vending (Meir-Dviri & Raz 1995; Brito 2013; Wee 2016), offensive
street remarks (Nielsen 2002, DOI 10.1111/1540-4560.00260), and street music (Lin et al. 2024). The chapter
can claim this absence and should: the film performs a speech act the interaction literature has not
described.

**No scholarship reads *Slacker* as a record of a city's coordinating conventions.** Consensus, queried on
"Richard Linklater Slacker 1991 film Austin Texas urban space public sociability strangers," returned twenty

Two sentences get stronger. The agglomeration paragraph — "Pack enough people close together and everyone's
odds of a good match improve … without anyone proposing a single one" — is currently footnoted to Duranton
and Puga, who do not say it. Their driver is market size, not density; their matching argument splits into
quality (§3.1, after Helsley and Strange) and probability (§3.2); and they warn that sharing, matching and
learning are observationally equivalent, which is why no measured agglomeration elasticity can be assigned
to matching. Rewrite the sentence around market size and two mechanisms, and keep the host's-trick line,
which survives intact. Do not import the 0.015–0.039 range as a magnitude for it. Mion and Naticchioni give
the sharper version — in larger markets, general attributes matter relatively less and specific ones more —
and also the complication, that assortative matching is negatively related to local market size.

The conventions paragraph gets a named source and a correction at the same time. "Once an encounter is
opened, it costs more to close than to endure" now has Schegloff's nonterminality behind it, which is
exactly what the moon-landing scene dramatizes. But the second half of the asymmetry has to be argued rather
than borrowed: Schegloff says a member cannot naively choose *not* to answer either, because in co-presence
the excusing inference is blocked and cold-shouldering gets inferred instead. Both sides of an opening carry
a cost. Say so, and take the asymmetry as the chapter's own claim against him. The sentence beginning "What
licenses the monologue is not the polite reflex Erving Goffman described" needs its Goffman note (18)
checked directly — Schegloff supplies no warrant for what civil inattention does or does not govern, and
this run could not open *Behavior in Public Places* at all.

One new paragraph is available and one is not. Papachristou gives the section a dated, quotable instrument:
until 1972 the conventions licensing a stranger to address a stranger in public were enforceable at an
officer's almost unfettered discretion against "poor people, nonconformists, dissenters, idlers," and
Douglas defended wandering and strolling as "amenities of life" in the same breath as conceding that they
"are not mentioned in the Constitution or in the Bill of Rights." That is the discretionary-enforcement
point the draft's "blindness to persons, not to types" sentence is reaching for, and May's Austin-inclusive
audit-pair study carries it into commercial gatekeeping. What is not available is the Koch & Fowler
paragraph. The 1928 plan is real, the quotation is real, and the temptation to write "Austin was engineered
to sort" is strong — but the plan's own text says the district was already all-Black in 1928, the 1935 HOLC
survey still describes a population scattered across five sections, and the film's east-side locations sit
outside the district as the plan defined it, which was south of the City Cemetery. Two of three lenses
refused it. Leave it out of this draft, or rebuild it as a claim about a concentration the 1935 survey
describes.

Finally, the "almost too good" pair in the slogan paragraph needs one word changed and one absence added.
The council did not declare Austin the Live Music Capital; it authorised the City Manager to adopt the
phrase as an official slogan for correspondence and publicity, and the declaration sits in a WHEREAS. The
interval is 55 days, eight weeks to the nearest week, and it should not be written as exact. And if the
chapter says Austin certified itself on a per-capita count of venues, it should add that the Convention and
Visitors Bureau, which owns the trademark now, no longer has the survey. The strongest new material in this
run, though, is an absence rather than a source: nobody has described the sustained uninvited monologue as
an interaction form. Every adjacent literature — panhandling, street vending, offensive street remarks,
street music — studies a short opening turn. The film performs a speech act the interaction literature has
not described, and the section can say so, provided it says what was searched.
