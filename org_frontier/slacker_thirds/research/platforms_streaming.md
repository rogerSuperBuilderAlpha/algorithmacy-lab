# Platform-studies base for the Slacker chapter (back half)

Research date: 2026-07-02. Every bibliographic claim below was checked against a fetched page; items that could not be fully confirmed are marked UNVERIFIED. No quotes or page numbers are supplied beyond what a fetched source showed.

## 1. Anchor citations — verified

### Stark & Pais (2020) — VERIFIED
- **David Stark and Ivana Pais, "Algorithmic Management in the Platform Economy," *Sociologica* 14, no. 3 (2020): 47–72. DOI: 10.6092/issn.1971-8853/12221.**
- Verified directly on the journal page: https://sociologica.unibo.it/article/view/12221 (authors' affiliations: Columbia University; Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore).
- Core claims confirmed on the article page:
  - Platforms are a **distinctive organizational form**: whereas actors in markets *contract*, in hierarchies *command*, and in networks *collaborate*, platforms **"co-opt assets, resources, and activities that are not part of the firm."** The platform neither hires (hierarchy) nor merely contracts (market): it enrolls what stays outside the firm's boundary.
  - Signature formulation (from the article page): platforms have **rules but are not bureaucratic; rankings but not ranks; accounts but no accountability**.
  - "Algorithmic management involves a peculiar kind of cybernetic control because at each fold of the feedback loop accountability can be deflected and denied." (quoted from the abstract as displayed on the journal page)
  - The abstract describes a **"triangular geometry"** among platform owners, providers, and users — a three-party structure, directly usable for the engineering-third framing.
- **Simmel check:** the journal landing page/abstract contains **no mention of Simmel, tertius, triads, or brokerage** — the "triangular geometry" is the authors' own three-party description, not an explicit Simmel citation. (Checked against the abstract page only; full-text PDF not searched. If the chapter asserts "Stark & Pais do not invoke Simmel," soften to "do not foreground Simmel" or verify against the PDF.)

### Stark & Vanden Broeck (2024) — CONFIRMED (previously verified in companion project)
- **David Stark and Pieter Vanden Broeck, "Principles of Algorithmic Management," *Organization Theory* 5, no. 2 (2024). DOI: 10.1177/26317877241257213.**
- Journal page: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/26317877241257213 ; open PDF at the author's site: https://davidcstark.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/principles-of-algorithmic-managment.final_.pdf
- The co-optation/enrollment summary is accurate: the paper positions **co-optation as a fourth coordination mechanism** alongside command (hierarchy), contract (market), and collaboration (network) — algorithmic management co-opts actors, assets, and activities without employing them, enrolling participants into the platform's logic. This paper (not Stark & Pais 2020) is where the Simmelian triadic apparatus appears, per the companion project's verification; search results here are consistent with that.

## 2. Uber as broker — verified

### Rosenblat & Stark (2016) — VERIFIED
- **Alex Rosenblat and Luke Stark, "Algorithmic Labor and Information Asymmetries: A Case Study of Uber's Drivers," *International Journal of Communication* 10 (2016).**
- Verified on the journal page: https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/view/4892 (published July 27, 2016; authors at Data & Society and NYU/Dartmouth). SSRN mirror: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2686227
- Pagination note: commonly cited as pp. 3758–3784, but the fetched abstract page did not display page numbers — treat the page range as UNVERIFIED until checked against the PDF.
- From the abstract (fetched): a nine-month empirical study; **"Uber does leverage significant indirect control over how drivers do their jobs"** and **"the information and power asymmetries produced by the Uber application are fundamental to its ability to structure control over its workers."**
- Concrete brokerage mechanics (confirmed via search results summarizing the paper, incl. https://www.informationweek.com/data-management/uber-drivers-under-algorithmic-management-study and the SSRN abstract):
  - **Blind passenger acceptance**: drivers are not shown the passenger's destination before accepting a ride; they get roughly **15 seconds** to accept or reject. They therefore cannot judge whether a ride will be profitable. The two matched parties never see the alternatives — the algorithm alone sees the queue.
  - Fares, surge multipliers, and acceptance-rate requirements are set and enforced by the platform; **neither driver nor rider negotiates terms with the other**. Low acceptance rates trigger warnings/deactivation risk.
  - This is exactly the engineering-third mechanic: the match is made by a party the matched parties can't see into, on terms neither of them set.

### Lee et al. (2015) — VERIFIED
- **Min Kyung Lee, Daniel Kusbit, Evan Metsky, and Laura Dabbish, "Working with Machines: The Impact of Algorithmic and Data-Driven Management on Human Workers," in *Proceedings of the 33rd Annual ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '15)* (ACM, 2015), 1603–1612. DOI: 10.1145/2702123.2702548.**
- ACM DL entry (403'd to the fetcher but citation verified via dblp): https://dblp.org/rec/conf/chi/LeeKMD15.html ; ACM: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2702123.2702548
- Qualitative study of Uber/Lyft drivers under three algorithmic functions: **work assignment** (the algorithm decides which driver gets which ride), informational support, and **evaluation via passenger ratings**. Drivers respond to opaque assignment by sense-making in online forums — i.e., the managed parties reverse-engineer the third because they cannot address it.

## 3. Streaming as engineering third over cinema itself — verified

### Hallinan & Striphas (2016) — VERIFIED
- **Blake Hallinan and Ted Striphas, "Recommended for You: The Netflix Prize and the Production of Algorithmic Culture," *New Media & Society* 18, no. 1 (2016): 117–137. DOI: 10.1177/1461444814538646.**
- Journal page: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444814538646
- Uses the Netflix Prize (2006–2009; $1M for a 10% improvement in recommendation accuracy) to define **algorithmic culture**: "the use of computational processes to sort, classify, and hierarchize people, places, objects, and ideas," plus the habits of thought and conduct that arise around them. Cultural decision-making — what counts as worth watching — is delegated to an engineered system.

### Striphas (2015) — VERIFIED
- **Ted Striphas, "Algorithmic Culture," *European Journal of Cultural Studies* 18, nos. 4–5 (2015): 395–412. DOI: 10.1177/1367549415577392.**
- Journal page: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1367549415577392
- Keywords-style genealogy (information, crowd, algorithm) of how humans came to **delegate the work of culture — sorting, classifying, hierarchizing — to computational processes**. The theoretical bridge from "aesthetic engineering" to "platform engineering."

### Lobato (2019) — VERIFIED
- **Ramon Lobato, *Netflix Nations: The Geography of Digital Distribution* (New York: NYU Press, 2019). 240 pp. ISBN 978-1-4798-0494-8 (pbk).**
- Publisher page: https://nyupress.org/9781479804948/netflix-nations/ ; open-access edition: https://opensquare.nyupress.org/books/9781479882281/
- Netflix as infrastructure of distribution: geoblocked, country-variable catalogs; the platform decides market-by-market what a national audience can see. Useful for the enclosure claim: distribution — once a negotiated chain of distributors, exhibitors, video stores — becomes a single brokered interface.

## 4. The indie infrastructure that made Slacker — verified

### Pierson's book and role — VERIFIED (2014 edition; 1995 first edition via secondary sources)
- **John Pierson, *Spike, Mike, Slackers & Dykes: A Guided Tour across a Decade of American Independent Cinema* (Austin: University of Texas Press, 2014). ISBN 9780292757684.** Publisher page fetched: https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292757684/
- First published 1995 (per Wikipedia entry, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spike,_Mike,_Slackers,_%26_Dykes — publisher of the 1995 edition not verified from a fetched page; UNVERIFIED detail, widely given as Hyperion/Miramax Books).
- UT Press description confirms: Pierson was a **producer's representative** who "hunted down, represented, and, in some cases, financed over twenty first-time independent features," including Spike Lee's *She's Gotta Have It*, Michael Moore's *Roger & Me*, and **Linklater's *Slacker*** — which gets its own chapter, **"Slacking Off."** That chapter is excerpted at Criterion: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/338-slacker-slacking-off (page 403'd to the fetcher; existence and title confirmed via search result). So the verified account is: the film that anthropologized Austin's talkers was itself brought to market by a human third — a rep who negotiated on the filmmaker's behalf with distributors, the opposite of an unaddressable algorithm.

### Slacker's production/distribution economics — VERIFIED (Wikipedia, with citations therein)
- Source fetched: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slacker_(film)
- Shot 1989 on a 16mm Arriflex; **budget $23,000**. Premiered at Austin's Dobie Theater July 27, 1990; **Orion Classics acquired it for nationwide distribution**, national release July 5, 1991; **domestic gross ~$1.2 million** (a ~50x multiple on budget through theatrical alone). Orion Home Video VHS June 1992 (~7,000 copies); Criterion Collection edition August 31, 2004.
- The path: local theater → festival circuit → human rep (Pierson) → studio-affiliated classics division (Orion Classics) → theatrical run → video window. Every link was a negotiable, addressable party. The contrast case for the chapter: today's $23k film's route to a national audience runs through festival-to-streamer acquisition or platform self-distribution, where the audience-matching is done by a recommender no one negotiates with.

## How this serves the argument

**Engineering-third claim.** Stark & Pais give the organizational-form warrant: the platform is a third that neither hires nor contracts but co-opts — present in every match, party to none, "accounts but no accountability." Rosenblat & Stark and Lee et al. supply the concrete mechanics: the algorithm alone sees the queue; drivers get a 15-second, destination-blind accept/reject; riders and drivers never negotiate — the match is engineered by a third that neither side can address. This is structurally the film's camera (which alone connects the chain of encounters, invisible to the characters) and structurally unlike the hosting city (a third that is a commons its parties inhabit and can renegotiate). Ajunwa's tertius bifrons is the mandatory prior-art citation for Simmel-onto-platforms; Stark & Vanden Broeck for the triadic/enrollment theory.

**Training claim.** Striphas 2015 and Hallinan & Striphas 2016 establish that "algorithmic culture" is culture's sorting work delegated to computation — and the Netflix Prize shows the delegation happening over movies first. Sirůček 2025 makes the apparatus-theory version of the chapter's claim explicit: the spectator-constituting machinery of cinema is the template for the data-subject-constituting machinery of platforms. The chapter can position Slacker's camera — an aesthetic engineering of who meets whom next — as the felt, watchable rehearsal of the mechanism audiences would later live inside.

**Enclosure claim.** The verified Slacker path ($23k → Dobie Theater → Pierson as human rep → Orion Classics → $1.2M theatrical → video window) was a chain of addressable, negotiating thirds; Pierson's book documents that world from inside. Tzioumakis documents its demolition (five specialty divisions shuttered 2008–2010; streaming pressure on theatrical), Lobato shows what replaced it (a geoblocked platform interface deciding national catalogs), and the CODA/$25M–Fair Play/$20M Sundance economics show the festival market now clearing through streamers. The recommender that then decides whether anyone is shown the film is the engineering third installed over cinema itself: the infrastructure that let Slacker find its audience has been enclosed by the kind of system the film's form anticipated.
