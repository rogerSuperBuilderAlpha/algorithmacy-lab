# Accepted abstract (verbatim)

Slacker (Linklater, 1991) follows about a hundred characters through a single day in Austin. There is no
protagonist. Most characters never meet. The film rejects the typical cinema narrative of return, resolution,
and convergence. Yet, audiences still find the film coherent. This chapter considers what holds it together.

The answer is a structural third. A third party can coordinate people who never directly interact. Though
neither the characters nor their encounters occupy this third position. Cities do this. Matchmakers do this.
Algorithms do this. The sociology of the third goes back to Simmel, who suggests that adding a third position
in a two-party relationship changes the social form both quantitatively and qualitatively (Simmel, 1908/1950).
The third mediates, brokers, or simply supplies the shared coordinates to organize the chaos.

Slacker contains two distinct kinds of third: "hosting" and "engineering." A third party "host" provides
shared reference points for coordination. Austin of 1990 "hosts" the film's strangers through the walkable
downtown, subcultural density, and conventions of civil inattention (Lofland, 1973). These conditions make
both the conspiracy theorist's monologue, and the Madonna Pap smear seller's pitch seem oddly plausible. In
contrast, an "engineering" third actively brokers each coordination. Linklater's camera does this. The camera
selects which face the viewer sees next, when a scene ends, and how the handoffs between strangers are paced.
Austin hosts while the camera engineers.

I want to use this framing to explore the new hosts and engineers of our world: algorithmic platforms (Stark
& Pais, 2020; Stark & Vanden Broeck, 2024). Uber does not facilitate coordination between drivers and riders
the way Austin hosts strangers. Uber brokers each match, selects which driver receives which ride, and
enforces the terms of the exchange. The parallel between Linklater's camera and Uber's algorithm is formal
and specific. Both select, rank, and filter among parties that never negotiate directly with each other.
However, their political economies differ. Cinema engineers for aesthetic ends. Platforms engineer for
extraction through coordination or co-optation.

Reading Slacker this way reframes the film's relationship to capitalism. Not only the characters and the
city, but also that audiences in 1991 could already follow coordination through an engineering third as an
aesthetic experience. I'll argue that cinema has trained viewers to read engineering thirds for a century.
Platforms have industrialized an operation that was already familiar before it became economically dominant.

Under what conditions does a hosting third become an engineering third? The indie cinema infrastructure that
made Slacker has now been encroached upon by streaming platforms. These platforms broker what viewers watch.
What interpretive skills does collective navigation of an engineering third require? How do these skills
compound across participants who share their readings of systems they cannot directly inspect (Möhlmann,
Alves de Lima Salge, & Marabelli, 2023)? What political forms could make "engineering thirds" transparent and
contestable instead of proprietary and extractive? This classic (cult, if one must!) opens these questions.
It shows a moment when a hosting third and an engineering third operate together.
