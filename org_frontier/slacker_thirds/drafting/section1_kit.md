# §1 drafting kit — The coherence puzzle (~500 words)

Raw materials and prompts. **Not prose.** You draft in your voice (rough is the point — the abstract's

## The section's moves (from the outline)

1. The refusal: no protagonist, no return, characters who never meet — and audiences follow it anyway.
2. The film announces its own structure in its first minutes (the taxi monologue).
3. What others say holds it together — and why none of it is quite the answer.
4. The question, sharpened: what *position* does the coordinating work?

## Verified raw material, by move

**Move 1 — the refusal and the facts:**
- "Roughly one hundred characters": Criterion's synopsis says the crew "threw out any idea of a traditional
  plot," creating "a tapestry of over a hundred characters"; Pierson: "about a hundred losers and
  schmoozers" in "three dozen different episodes"; Canby: "a hundred or so relentlessly eccentric people."
  (Never "97" — that's Pierson's "ninety-seven-minute talkfest," a runtime.)
- Production: 16mm, ~$23,000, shot summer 1989 in Austin; Dobie Theatre July 1990 (year-long run); Orion
  Classics national release July 5, 1991; ~$1.2M gross.
- Canby's image if you want a critic's voice for the refusal: "a 14-course meal composed entirely of
  desserts."

**Move 2 — the film announces itself:**
- Linklater plays the first character, credited as "Should have stayed at the bus station."
- The taxi monologue: a dream in which "instead of anything... going on, there's nothing going on"; each
  un-taken choice "fractions off" into its own reality. Rosenbaum reads it as announcing "the movie's
  contrarian discourse on the virtues of inaction."
- The structural echo you can draw: each handoff is one road taken while ninety-nine others fraction off —
  the monologue is the structure describing itself. (Speed 2007, "The Possibilities of Roads Not Taken,"
  shows scholarship already reads Linklater through this monologue — build on, don't claim discovery.)

**Move 3 — the existing answers (position against, with credit):**
1. *Hidden craft* — Rosenbaum: it "appears to have no structure, to be chaotic... when, in fact, it has a
   very subtle, extremely well-crafted structure that makes it a portrait of chaos." Pierson: "not a single
   jump cut... the passage from one scene to the next is always in a flow."
2. *The surrealist relay* — Ebert: the camera caroms "like a cue ball with a camera" (via Buñuel's Phantom
   of Liberty); Wilkins: "passing the baton"; Barker: La Ronde. These name the mechanism but treat it as
   inherited genre device, not active brokerage.
3. *The city itself* — Linklater: "a 100 percent Austin product." But Gaughen shows the film's spatial
   continuity is partly fictional (implausible distances between consecutive scenes) — the city can't be
   doing the joining. Save this card for §3/§4; it can be teased here.
4. *The day-in-the-life frame* — Canby: "morning, afternoon, night and morning again."
5. *Talk as connective tissue* — Hinson: characters "talk, nonstop, for the sheer pleasure of hearing
   themselves talk"; Stone makes dialogue "the determinant factor in the form."
6. *Spectatorship* — Linklater's own account: the first TV-remote generation, "creating our own narratives
   by watching five minutes of this and then one minute of that"; Slacker as "you're either channel surfing
   or you're going in and out of different movies." (This one is closest to §6's training claim — tee it up.)
- The honest novelty line: none of these decomposes the coherence work into a shared reference environment
  plus an active per-handoff broker. Macor's "The Least Auteur Film Ever Made" (coherence from collective
  authorship) is the strongest counter-position — name it.

**Move 4 — the sharpened question:**
- Linklater's own words are the bridge to §2: "We're not going to offer a story but at least we'll offer a
  comprehensive architecture that you can pass through and not feel completely disoriented." The question
  the chapter asks: what *is* that architecture, and what position does its work?

## Three prompts (answer these in your own words — that's the draft)

1. **Your first viewing.** Where in the film did you realize you weren't lost — that something was carrying
   you even though no one on screen was? What did that feel like it was?
2. **The insufficiency.** "Austin holds it together" and "the editing holds it together" are both true and
   both incomplete. In your words: what does each answer miss that the other has?
3. **The promise.** What does the taxi monologue promise the viewer in the first five minutes — and what
   would it mean to take that promise as the film's actual method rather than a character's rambling?
