# Deep research: the joins — city, camera, platform (Packet 6)

Run date: 2026-08-12. Four search angles — the urban/settings literature on what gathers strangers; the network-narrative and film-form literature; the platform-studies and platform-law literature; the genealogical literature on Simmel, Royce and Reich. Every claim that came back from those angles went to three-lens adversarial verification, each lens required to retrieve the source, state the source's own thesis in the author's terms, and then try to break the claim on quotation, locator, scope and provenance.

Forty-nine claims went to verification. Twenty survived. Twenty-nine were refuted — fourteen by two lenses, fifteen unanimously. Two of the twenty survivors (C48, C49) carry provenance class D, meaning the source was never opened, so they are demoted below to "Do NOT use" with the retrieval that would close them; eighteen findings remain. Gap rows covered: A-S8, A-S9, A-R4, A-R8, A-C6, A-C7, A-C8.

**The run's central question came back negative, and the reader should go to the Absences section before writing a word.** The question was whether anyone has already assembled the chapter's platform stack — access held as a revocable grant, plus membership among parties who never meet, plus coordination at a distance by a party that does not know which pair it joins. Nobody has. Each of the three elements has a published near-occupant; the three never occur together, and the non-knowledge clause, which is the load-bearing one, appears nowhere at all. That is a result, not a hole, but it means A-C6 and A-C7 are the chapter's to argue and not the chapter's to cite. Two further negatives bite harder. Bordwell explicitly rejects the inference on which the chapter's platform-shape and genealogy joins rest — that network film form follows from network technology — and the Simmel→Royce→Reich sequence has no documented transmission at either seam. Read the Absences first; several of them are the chapter's best material, and one of them is a wall.

## Finding 1 — Kalmijn and Flap work with a standing supply/demand distinction that is a reach/selection split in all but name, but they locate the selecting in the parties' own preferences rather than in a second coordinator (high, 3-0) [C6]

All three lenses retrieved the article — two through the Groningen repository scan, one through a Relais interlibrary scan of the same pagination — and all three confirmed the p. 1290 passage verbatim with the folio visible in the running head. The interpretive point held under every attack: "preferences determine how people choose partners out of the pool they face" puts the selecting agent inside the pair, and no second entity does any choosing anywhere in the article. Lens 2 called this the strongest near-miss in the whole set and warned that the phrase "a reach/selection distinction in all but name" is fair only if the chapter says plainly that Kalmijn and Flap's selector is a party, not an institution — which the claim does say. Lens 3 pressed two narrowings a referee would raise, and both should be absorbed rather than argued with. First, Kalmijn and Flap report the supply/demand split as a standing distinction in an existing literature, crediting Fischer, Flap, Huckfeldt and Marsden on one side and Buss and Barnes and South on the other, and noting that the two are "sometimes combined into the notion of filtering (e.g., Murstein 1986)"; "already split" must not be read as a priority claim for them. Second, their own thesis is that inside organised settings "preferences and opportunities are both operating in the same direction," so the two sides are not cleanly separable in the cases they study — which is a reason the chapter needs its two coordinators, not a reason it cannot have them. COMPLICATES. Bears on A-S9 and A-C6.

**Sources:** Matthijs Kalmijn and Henk Flap, "Assortative Meeting and Mating: Unintended Consequences of Organized Settings for Partner Choices," *Social Forces* 79, no. 4 (June 2001): 1289–1312 ; DOI 10.1353/sof.2001.0044 ; https://doi.org/10.1353/sof.2001.0044 (read in the Groningen repository scan, pure.rug.nl/ws/files/14689454/kalmijn-assortative.pdf).

**Evidence:** p. 1290: "Hypotheses about interaction opportunities are part of a general supply-side perspective on social relations. The supply-side perspective argues that the social contexts in which people participate, mold their networks by shaping the pool from which they draw their contacts (Fischer et al. 1977; Flap 1999; Huckfeldt 1983; Marsden 1990). Demand-side theories, in contrast, emphasize the preferences people have to meet and select a specific type of partner (e.g., Buss & Barnes 1986; South 1991). … Institutional arrangements delimit the pool from which people can choose, and preferences determine how people choose partners out of the pool they face." p. 1293: "When people meet outside organized settings, in contrast, the selection process is largely shaped by preferences." Abstract, p. 1289: "our findings generally confirm the notion that mating requires meeting: the pool of available interaction partners is shaped by various institutionally organized arrangements and these constrain the type of people with whom we form personal relationships."

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "Kalmijn and Flap work with an already-standing distinction between a supply side that delimits the pool and a demand side that chooses out of it — 'Institutional arrangements delimit the pool from which people can choose, and preferences determine how people choose partners out of the pool they face' (p. 1290) — a reach/selection distinction in all but name. But the selecting sits in the parties' own preferences, not in a second coordinating entity, so the chapter's division of labour between two coordinators is not pre-empted."

*Correction applied, from lens 1:* the abstract quotation begins mid-sentence (the original runs "While in some cases, settings have unexpected effects on marriage choice, our findings generally confirm…"), so it needs a leading ellipsis.

## Finding 2 — Kalmijn and Flap state the by-product condition the chapter needs: settings built for other purposes that throw off meeting opportunities incidentally (high, 3-0) [C7]

Unanimous, and unusually well corroborated: three independent OCR passes over the same scan put both sentences on p. 1291, with the page fixed twice over by the recto running head "Assortative Meeting and Mating / 1291" and by the verso footer of the following page, "1292 / Social Forces 79:4, June 2001," which also confirms volume, issue and date against the citation. The by-product condition is the article's own premise rather than a stray phrase, so the chapter is using the authors for what they argue. Lens 3 supplied the two narrowings that matter. "Settings that were built for something else" over-covers, because Kalmijn and Flap immediately divide their five settings into consciously designed organisations — schools, firms, voluntary associations — and undesigned but socially constructed ones — neighbourhoods and family networks. And what the by-product supplies is meeting and interaction opportunity for personal relationships; the word "coordination" is the chapter's, and the chapter has to argue meeting opportunity up into coordination rather than assume the lift. ## Finding 3 — Duranton and Puga separate match quality from match probability but drive both from the number of agents, so urban economics supplies no reach party distinct from a selection party (medium-high, 2-1) [C8]

The dissent here is the finding, and it is worth more to the chapter than the majority. All three lenses confirmed both quotations verbatim at the working paper's pp. 2 and 30. Two lenses accepted the claim as a negative about parties: whatever internal distinctions Duranton and Puga draw, both matching mechanisms take city size as their single driver, so no party furnishes reach while a different party furnishes selection. Lens 3 refuted it, and refuted it on the sentence the claim itself quotes. Duranton and Puga give §3.2 its own heading, its own literature (Mortensen–Pissarides, Petrongolo–Pissarides) and its own formal object (the aggregate matching function), and they call it "Another possible source." Improving the chance you match at all, as against improving whom you end up matched with, is very close to the chapter's reach/selection split, and it is drawn explicitly on p. 30; shared causation in city size does not collapse two mechanisms into one. The chapter should take the dissent's version: the precedent for the *distinction* exists in the matching literature and must be acknowledged; what does not exist is the *division of the two functions between two different parties*.

All three lenses also flagged the same citation defect, which must be fixed before print. The claim called the NBER working paper "identical" to the published Handbook chapter. The working paper's own cover says otherwise — "This is a draft of a chapter written for eventual publication in the Handbook of Regional and Urban Economics, Volume 4" — and its pagination (1–61) has nothing to do with the chapter's (2063–2117), so "p. 30" is a working-paper page and cannot be printed as a Handbook page. Lens 2 also notes Crossref dates the working paper September 2003, not August. COMPLICATES. Bears on A-S9.

**Sources:** Gilles Duranton and Diego Puga, "Chapter 48: Micro-foundations of urban agglomeration economies," in *Handbook of Regional and Urban Economics*, vol. 4, *Cities and Geography*, ed. J. Vernon Henderson and Jacques-François Thisse (Amsterdam: Elsevier, 2004), 2063–2117 ; DOI 10.1016/S1574-0080(04)80005-1 ; https://doi.org/10.1016/S1574-0080(04)80005-1. Quotations read in the draft working-paper version: NBER Working Paper No. 9931 (Cambridge, MA: NBER, 2003) ; DOI 10.3386/w9931 ; https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9931/w9931.pdf.

**Evidence:** NBER WP 9931, p. 2: "we distinguish three types of micro-foundations, based on sharing, matching, and learning mechanisms." p. 30, §3.2 ("Improving the chances of matching"): "In the previous section we have discussed models in which an increase in the number of agents trying to match improves the expected quality of each match. Another possible source of urban agglomeration economies based on a matching mechanism arises when an increase in the number of agents trying to match improves the chances of matching."

*Correction applied, from lens 3 (the dissent):* "Duranton and Puga fold both effects into a single family: 'we distinguish three types of micro-foundations, based on sharing, matching, and learning mechanisms' (NBER WP 9931, p. 2). Within that family, though, they do split the two functions the chapter wants split — §3.1 covers models in which more agents improve 'the expected quality of each match,' while §3.2 covers 'Another possible source... when an increase in the number of agents trying to match improves the chances of matching' (p. 30). The chapter's reach/selection distinction therefore has a close precedent in the matching literature; what is not there is the division of the two functions between two DIFFERENT parties, since in Duranton and Puga both are driven by city size alone."

*Correction applied, from lens 1:* cite the NBER paper as a draft — "which its own cover describes as 'a draft of a chapter written for eventual publication' in the Handbook; the published chapter's wording and pagination were not checked."

## Finding 4 — Warner is the closest neighbour to A-S8, not a prior occupant of it (medium-high, 2-1) [C9]

Every quotation and every page pin in this claim survived all three lenses, checked against the Project MUSE scan folio by folio: p. 50, p. 55, p. 56, p. 60. So did the Simmel negative, which is stronger than the claim stated — "Simmel" occurs exactly three times in the essay, all inside note 3 on p. 56, and all about "The Stranger." The tertius never appears at all, which is why lens 1 corrected "expressly not via Simmel's third" to a silent rather than an express exclusion.

The dissent is substantive and the chapter should take it. Lens 3 refuted the claim on the two features that do the work. Warner's first thesis is that a public is *self-organized*, and he says flatly that a public produces belonging only "if it is self-organized through discourse rather than through an external framework" (p. 52) — so the circulating discourse is not a power standing outside the parties; it is autotelic, and it calls into being the public it postulates. And Warner's fifth thesis makes reflexive circulation constitutive, with the link between prior and subsequent discourse "not mere consecutiveness in time, but a context of interaction" (p. 62). What Warner denies is co-presence and mutual acquaintance, not interaction. Read that way, the news is good for the chapter: Warner is a near-neighbour whose public fails the chapter's own two conditions — no power outside the parties, and interaction of a reflexive kind — which makes him a foil rather than a claimant. Lens 2 adds a second obligation. Warner already writes at p. 62 of "membership by mere attention," a membership predicate for strangers who never meet, and that is a live prior occupant of part of A-C6/A-C7 and should be cited there too, not only at A-S8. ## Finding 5 — Bordwell rejects the inference the chapter's platform-shape and genealogy joins depend on: network film form is not an effect of network technology (high, 3-0) [C16]

This is the hostile finding of the run and it was confirmed unanimously, from the publisher's own preview file, with the page independently fixed by the compositor's foot line "RT7797X_C001.indd 29" and by the following page's head, "30 Poetics of Cinema." The passage is verbatim; the only departure is the dropped footnote marker 50 after "Internet," which is correct practice. Bordwell's surrounding argument makes it worse for anyone who wants to soften the finding: the passage illustrates his methodological statement that historical poetics "is conservative, often trying to remind people that things that seem brand-new almost always proceed from longer-lived conventions," and it closes "The motto of historical poetics might be that of Shakespeare's Lear: Nothing comes of nothing."

Two narrowings protect the chapter without rescuing it. The passage is an illustration inside a methodological credo rather than a standalone argument, and it forward-references the sustained case, the "network narratives" essay later in the same volume — so the chapter that wants to argue with Bordwell must argue with chapter 7, not only with p. 29. And Bordwell does not say the recent salience of network narratives is uncaused; he relocates the cause, to "culture and pressures within the film industry." A chapter that reads a filmed city as a platform can still do so as a reading rather than as a genealogy; what it cannot do is claim that the form arose because the technology did. REFUTES. Bears on A-S9 and A-C8.

**Sources:** David Bordwell, "Poetics of Cinema," ch. 1 in *Poetics of Cinema* (New York: Routledge, 2008), 29 ; no DOI (book; e-ISBN 9781135867812) ; publisher preview PDF, https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781135867812_A23809676/preview-9781135867812_A23809676.pdf.

**Evidence:** p. 29: "If we call *Crash* (2005) and *Happy Endings* (2005) 'hyperlink narratives,' we're implying that their formal principles are pretty new, arising from recent technological changes like the Internet. *Correction applied, from lens 3:* "Bordwell rejects the inference the chapter's platform-shape and genealogy joins depend on: calling such films 'hyperlink narratives' implies 'their formal principles are pretty new, arising from recent technological changes like the Internet,' whereas 'those principles are revealed to be modifications … of norms that have been used in cinema for decades' (*Poetics of Cinema*, p. 29). He does not deny that the form became salient recently; he attributes that salience to 'culture and pressures within the film industry' rather than to network technology."

## Finding 10 — Gillespie treats contact with strangers as a moderation exposure, not as a form of unification — but he is scoping his study, not defining platformhood (medium-high, 2-1) [C28]

## How this lands in the chapter

The run gives the chapter three things: a set of foils, a set of restatements it must make, and one wall.

The foils are the strongest result. Warner, Khurana, Silvey, Cohen and Stark and Pais each stand one step away from a move the chapter makes, and each fails a different one of the chapter's conditions. Warner's public is self-organised and reflexively interactive; Khurana's every third is in live contact with both parties; Silvey's network community runs on Wittel and Tönnies and is visible only to viewers; Cohen's membership is sovereignty, not grant; Stark and Pais's membership is Möbius topology and co-optation of the user into the product. The chapter should name all five in the same passage. A near-neighbour named and beaten is worth more than an empty field claimed.

The restatements are unglamorous and unavoidable. The Duranton and Puga citation must go to the working paper, as a draft, with its own pagination. The Lazar quotations must be flagged as preprint, and the second one moved from §4 to §3.1. The Bain sentence must recover its modality and its elite condition and drop Halbwachs. Silvey must be cited as a range. Brescia's counts must be indicative. Erickson's chapter must either be read or the note rewritten to say it was not. And two citation errors that reached this dossier from earlier passes would have failed on sight in review — an MA thesis attributed to the wrong author, and a Grabher and van Tuijl article given the wrong title — which is a reason to keep the second-hand pins visible rather than tidied away.

The wall is Bordwell. The chapter's platform-shape and genealogy joins want to read network film form as an effect of network technology, and the leading authority on that form denies it in print, in a passage whose surrounding argument is a methodological credo against exactly that inference. Two routes out. The chapter can drop the genealogical claim and keep the reading — a filmed city read *as* a platform, offered as an analytic device rather than as a historical derivation, which costs nothing the argument needs. Or it can take on chapter 7 of *Poetics of Cinema* directly, and accept that Bordwell has already relocated the cause of network narrative's recent salience to culture and industry pressure, so the burden is to show his relocation is incomplete. The first route is cheap and honest. The second is a different chapter.

One last thing worth saying plainly, because it is what the negatives add up to. The composite the chapter proposes — grant, membership without meeting, coordination by a party that does not know which pair it joins — is not in the literature, in whole or in any pair. That is a real finding and the chapter should say so as a finding, with the search scope attached, rather than as an absence of competition. The non-knowledge clause in particular appears nowhere in five full platform texts searched for it. It is the chapter's own, it is load-bearing, and nothing in this run will carry it.
