# Deep research: P3–P4, Royce on community, memory, loyalty, and non-personal interpreters

Run 2026-08-12 against A-R1–A-R12 and B-R1–B-R4. This was a targeted source-retrieval
pass, not a completed three-lens adversarial verification run. No synthetic vote is invented below:
confidence instead records source convergence and read depth. Provenance follows the project ladder:
`A` retrieved source with verbatim evidence; `B` convergence-verified with the primary unopened;
`C` abstract-only, metadata-only, second-hand, or single-lens; `D` requires library access. Abstract-only
material is never upgraded above `C`.

The main result is corrective. Royce permits community without face-to-face meeting, acquaintance,
cooperation, affection, or agreement, but not community without communicable selves and not, in the
definition retrieved here, a community whose living members are non-contemporaries. His three stated
conditions are cumulative. Loyalty is not a fourth condition of the thin community of memory, but it is
what makes a complex community conscious of itself and what distinguishes a genuine community from
mere association or predatory community. Peircean semiotics supplies real company for non-human and
distributed semiosis, but it does not straightforwardly put a camera in Royce's interpreter seat:
the interpretant is not an interpreter, external artifacts augment human interpretation, organisms can
be receptive interpretive systems, and Nöth gives ordinary machines only quasi-semiosis.

## Finding 1 — Royce's three conditions are cumulative, and communication cannot be dropped (high; convergent primary and secondary full text) [A]

Royce labels three items as conditions “upon which the existence of a community depends”: ideal
temporal extension of the individual self; a plurality of distinct selves capable of social
communication and generally communicating; and at least one identical event included in the ideally
extended lives of all. The grammar is cumulative, not a menu of alternatives. The current sentence
“shared relation to an outside past or future is enough for membership” therefore overcompresses the
definition.

The 2026 Stanford Encyclopedia revision independently gives the same limit in categorical form:
“Not just any association or collection of individuals is a community. Community can only exist where
individual members are in communication with one another.” Jacquelyn Ann K. Kegley's retrieved paper
likewise includes “the presence of communication among selves in the community” among Royce's
conditions of genuine community. The secondary literature located in this pass does not license a
community of wholly non-communicating selves.

The phrase “and, in general, engaged in communication” should not be silently weakened into mere
logical communicability. Royce's “capable” clause states a capacity; “in general” qualifies actual
engagement. The safest formulation is: capacity for social communication is necessary, and actual
communication is Royce's normal case. A claim that actual communication is never required would need
a contemporary commentator who argues directly from “in general”; none was found.

**Sources:** Josiah Royce, *The Problem of Christianity*, vol. II (New York: Macmillan, 1913),
60–68; 1968 Chicago reset, 253–56 (primary passages and pagination verified in
`v4/factbase/royce_card.md`). Kelly A. Parker and Shawn E. Klein, “Josiah Royce,” *Stanford
Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 3 August 2004, substantively revised 15 January 2026,
§2.2.2, no DOI. Jacquelyn Ann K. Kegley, “Josiah Royce: Classical American Philosopher; Pragmatist,
Phenomenologist, Process Thinker and Advocate for Community,” conference manuscript hosted by Nordic
Pragmatism Network, n.d., PDF pp. 8–9, no DOI.

**Evidence:** Royce's three numbered conditions are reproduced verbatim in `royce_card.md`:
the self's power to extend its life ideally (II, 60–61 / 253); “a number of distinct selves capable
of social communication, and, in general, engaged in communication” (II, 67 / 255); and ideally
extended selves including events “which are, for all these selves, identical” (II, 68 / 256).
Parker and Klein, §2.2.2: “Community can only exist where individual members are in communication
with one another so that there is, to some extent and in some relevant respect, a congruence of
feeling, thought, and will among them.” Kegley, PDF p. 8: Royce's conditions include “the presence of
communication among selves in the community as well as attentive listening to the ideas and hopes of
others.”

**Relation:** REFUTES A-R3 as written; SUPPORTS B-R1 and the communication half of B-R4.

## Finding 2 — No meeting is required; contemporaneity and communication are separate constraints (high on Royce, medium on secondary reception) [A]

Three propositions must remain separate:

1. **Never meeting face to face:** compatible with Royce's definition. The Maori may remain aloof and
   may be enemies. Encounter, acquaintance, warmth, and cooperation are not necessary.
2. **Not communicating:** not licensed. The second condition explicitly retains communicability and
   generally actual communication.
3. **Not being contemporaries:** not licensed by the retrieved definition. Royce asks about “many
   different selves, all belonging to the present time” and defines the case through “many
   contemporary and distinct individual selves.” The dead can be commemorated objects of the shared
   past; the living commemorators are the members in the example.

Thus “membership without meeting” is defensible only when it does not become shorthand for membership
without communication or across non-contemporaneous living/dead membership. “Royce named membership
when those people never meet face to face” should become “Royce permits a community among
communicable contemporaries who need never meet face to face.”

The secondary sources retrieved emphasize communication but do not specifically discuss the Maori
case. B-R2 therefore remains open as a contemporary-reception row even though its primary basis is
secure.

**Sources:** Royce, *Problem of Christianity*, II, 44, 46–50, 67 / 1968, 246–48, 255.
Parker and Klein, “Josiah Royce,” §2.2.2. Kegley, “Josiah Royce,” PDF p. 8.

**Evidence:** Primary evidence, verified in `royce_card.md`: “Each may keep aloof from the other.
They may be enemies” (II, 46 / 247); “many contemporary and distinct individual selves” (II, 49 /
248); and the communication condition (II, 67 / 255). The contemporary secondary summaries retain
communication and supply no exception for never-communicating or non-contemporary members.

**Relation:** SUPPORTS a narrowed A-R1 and first half of A-R6; REFUTES any stronger no-contact
reading; leaves B-R2 `open` for a page-pinned secondary discussion of the Maori example.

## Finding 3 — Appropriation, not mere co-location or common exposure, performs the members'-side work (high) [A]

Royce's definition is not that the same event happened around several persons. Each member must accept
the same past or expected event “as part of his own individual life and self.” That is a members'-side
act of ideal temporal self-extension. A city, day, screening, log, or archive may supply the same
object to many people without satisfying that act.

This directly supports the chapter's question whether the film's strangers “take that day as theirs,”
but it blocks the conclusion that they are already members simply because the city and camera place
them in a common relation. Shared exposure is evidence for a candidate common event, not yet for
Roycean membership.

**Sources:** Royce, *Problem of Christianity*, II, 50–51 / 248. Parker and Klein, “Josiah Royce,”
§2.2.2.

**Evidence:** Royce: “each of its members accepts as part of his own individual life and self the same
past events” (II, 50 / 248). Parker and Klein: common past and future events are events “which all
members hold as identical parts of their own lives.”

**Relation:** SUPPORTS A-R2; REFRAMES A-R4 and A-R7.

## Finding 4 — Loyalty is not a fourth condition of a thin memory community, but it is constitutive of conscious and genuine community (high; convergent primary and secondary) [A]

The three conditions at II, 60–68 do not mention loyalty. A community of memory can therefore be
identified at that thin definitional level before asking whether its loyalty is good, bad, conscious,
or durable. Parker and Klein make the distinction useful for the chapter: common past and future
events are “the basis of” loyalty; some communities are defined by true loyalty and are “genuine
communities” or “communities of grace,” while others are organized by predatory loyalty.

Lecture X, however, prevents the chapter from treating loyalty as decorative. When complex cooperation
exceeds individual comprehension and appears mechanical, “such love—such loyalty” furnishes the basis
for consciousness of community. Royce expressly speaks of love's place “in the constitution of the
life of a real community.” Loyalty is therefore not necessary for the bare three-condition community
of memory, but it is constitutive of the community's self-conscious, genuine life under the modern
conditions the chapter describes.

The defensible platform claim is not “loyalty blocks every equation of platform with community.”
It is: a platform record cannot by itself establish members' appropriation, and even a platform-hosted
memory community is not thereby a Roycean conscious or genuine community. If the chapter wants only
the thin community of memory, loyalty cannot serve as an absolute exclusion test.

**Sources:** Royce, *Problem of Christianity*, II, 84–95 / 1968, 262–67; Royce, *Philosophy of
Loyalty* (New York: Macmillan, 1908), 16–17, 51–53, 118, 121. Parker and Klein, “Josiah Royce,”
§2.2.2.

**Evidence:** Primary passages and locators are verified in `royce_card.md`, especially II, 91–95 /
265–67. Parker and Klein: “These common past and future events ... are the basis of their loyalty to
the community”; “some communities are defined by true loyalty” and are genuine communities, while
others are defined by “vicious or predatory loyalty.”

**Relation:** REFRAMES A-R9 and A-R10; complicates any thesis that technology replaces loyalty while
leaving Royce's full logic of community intact.

## Finding 5 — Bellah preserves memory-plus-hope but changes the operative sociology (high on retrieved Bellah text; medium on *Habits* page reconstruction) [A/C]

Bellah's reception is the largest route by which “community of memory” entered contemporary social
science. In a 1986 address responding to readers of *Habits of the Heart*, Bellah explicitly says the
book combines community of memory with community of hope and identifies Royce as the source:
“Royce's point is that a genuine community of memory is a community of hope and vice versa.”
Bellah stresses painful and dangerous memories as the condition of non-naive hope.

The Bellah reception does not preserve Royce's three-condition test as an analytic checklist.
In *Habits of the Heart*, the phrase works through constitutive narrative, shared practices, social
interdependence, and participation in discussion and decision-making. It is therefore a reception
surface for narrative and public memory, not evidence that a permanent record alone constitutes
membership.

**Sources:** Robert N. Bellah, “Tradition and Community in the Humanities,” address to the Community
College Humanities Association national conference, 22 November 1986, printed in *Community Colleges
Humanities Review* 8 (1987), retrieved ERIC ED288580, printed pp. 5–6, no DOI. Robert N. Bellah,
Richard Madsen, William M. Sullivan, Ann Swidler, and Steven M. Tipton, *Habits of the Heart:
Individualism and Commitment in American Life* (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1985),
152–55 and 333; the book itself was not retrieved in this pass, so those book locators remain `C`.

**Evidence:** Bellah address, printed p. 6: “we combine the notion of ‘community of memory’ with that
of ‘community of hope’”; “we are drawing on another American philosopher, Josiah Royce”; and
“Royce's point is that a genuine community of memory is a community of hope and vice versa, and, to
put it even more strongly, without memory there is no hope.” A retrieved 1994 secondary quotation of
*Habits*, p. 333, defines community through social interdependence, joint discussion and decision,
and shared practices; because the book page was not directly opened, that quotation remains `C`.

**Relation:** SUPPORTS reception/memory framing for A-R8, but not the specific *Slacker* join;
COMPLICATES A-R11's archive-equals-community inference.

## Finding 6 — Film and city memory scholarship supplies adjacent couch, not a Roycean application (high on adjacent field; high-confidence absence for this pass) [A]

Film reception scholarship gives the chapter strong non-Roycean company. Kuhn, Biltereyst, and Meers
describe film and cinema as repositories and producers of individual and collective memory; distinguish
audience from spectator; and report that cinema memories are often narrated as “we” rather than “I,”
suggesting remembered membership in social, cultural, or familial groups. Their survey covers cities,
neighbourhoods, vanished places, and later recollection. This is directly useful for the chapter's
reception and place-memory applications.

It is not, however, an application of Royce. Searches combining Royce and “community of memory” with
film, cinema, audience, city, urban, single-day, and reception found no scholar applying Royce's
three conditions to a film public, a city as such, or an ephemeral one-day collectivity. No source
located treats mere appearance in one filmic day as members' appropriation of that day.

**Sources:** Annette Kuhn, Daniel Biltereyst, and Philippe Meers, “Memories of Cinemagoing and Film
Experience: An Introduction,” *Memory Studies* 10, no. 1 (2017): 3–16,
https://doi.org/10.1177/1750698016670783.

**Evidence:** pp. 3–4: cinema and memory scholarship studies films and cinemas as repositories for
“individual and collective memories”; cinema memory can participate in collective memory and cultural
identity. The article later reports a recurrent “we” in informants' accounts and says this suggests
association of past cinemagoing with sociability and membership in particular groups. The issue's case
studies include cities, city neighbourhoods, erased places, and remembered cinema cultures.

**Relation:** supplies adjacent couch for A-R4 and A-R8, but those specific Roycean joins remain
OWN-AS-AUTHOR.

## Finding 7 — A platform's record is neither appropriation nor communication, and permanence adds no Roycean condition (high) [A]

Royce's test concerns what members accept as part of their lives, not whether an intermediary stores a
durable record. A permanent platform log may make a candidate past available for commemoration, but it
does not demonstrate that users appropriate the event, communicate with one another, or form a
conscious/genuine community. A city may in some cases sustain thicker communication and shared
practice than a platform even when its records are less durable.

No Roycean source located supports “more securely than a city because it keeps the record permanently.”
Permanence is not one of the three conditions, and Bellah's reception emphasizes retelling, practices,
discussion, and hope rather than storage duration.

**Sources:** Royce and Parker/Klein as in Findings 1–4; Bellah as in Finding 5; Julie E. Cohen,
“Law for the Platform Economy,” *UC Davis Law Review* 51 (2017): 133–204, especially 154–58, no DOI
located.

**Evidence:** Royce's three conditions contain no archive-duration requirement. Cohen, pp. 154–55,
describes the platform relationship as an “access-for-data” bargain governed by non-negotiable
terms, not as communal membership.

**Relation:** REFRAMES A-R10 and REFUTES A-R11 as written.

## Finding 8 — “Enrollment,” “assignment,” “account status,” and “access relation” can replace equivocal platform “membership” (high) [A]

Cohen provides the cleanest replacement vocabulary. Platforms structure data collection around broad
presumptive consent and make “user enrollment seamless and near-automatic.” They govern an
“access-for-data” bargain through boilerplate agreements that are “contractual in form but mandatory
in operation.” This vocabulary describes platform-imposed or platform-configured status without
importing Royce's members'-side appropriation.

Recommended distinctions:

- **platform enrollment** — entry into the platform's sociotechnical data relation, including
  near-automatic enrollment;
- **account registration** — the user's account-creation act;
- **contractual assent** — clickwrap or other legally asserted assent, where actually present;
- **algorithmic assignment** or **dispatch assignment** — the app's unilateral allocation of a task;
- **user/account status** — the administratively recognized platform role;
- **access relation** or **access-for-data relation** — the continuing platform bargain;
- **Roycean membership** — reserve for communicable selves who appropriate an identical past or
  future event as part of their own lives.

For A-R12, “membership here does not wait for his yes” should become “dispatch assignment does not
wait for his assent” or “the platform enrolls/configures him in an access relation before any
Roycean act of appropriation.” Do not use “assent” if the factual mechanism is merely app notification;
assent names a legal or expressed yes, while assignment names the platform's act.

**Sources:** Cohen, “Law for the Platform Economy,” 139, 154–58. See also Cohen, *Between Truth and
Power: The Legal Constructions of Informational Capitalism* (Oxford University Press, 2019), ch. 2;
the retrieved author-posted chapter confirms the same “enrollment seamless” vocabulary, but the
article gives stable journal pagination.

**Evidence:** Cohen, p. 158: platforms use “broad presumptive consent” and configure infrastructures
to make “user enrollment seamless and near-automatic.” At pp. 154–55, the platform's access-for-data
contract is “a boilerplate terms-of-use agreement not open to negotiation”; such agreements are
“contractual in form but mandatory in operation.”

**Relation:** SUPPORTS a vocabulary repair for A-R12; also disciplines A-R10.

## Finding 9 — Royce's interpreter is a self addressing another self; Peirce's interpretant is not that interpreter (high) [A]

Royce's interpretation triad is interpreter, object interpreted, and person addressed. His
Community of Interpretation is explicitly a community of selves, and interpretation is a conversation.
The nearest impersonal case, geological traces, is only potential interpretation to a possible future
observer. A camera or archive can occupy the sign/object position in Royce's scheme; it cannot be
inserted into the interpreter position without an explicit extension.

Peirce does not remove the problem by replacing “interpreter” with “interpretant.” In Peircean
semiotics, the interpretant is the further sign or effect produced in semiosis, not the person or
machine who occupies Royce's interpreter office. Skagestad's full-text exposition says interpretation
creates a new sign in the interpreter's mind, while also showing that cognition depends on external
sign vehicles and tools. That makes a camera plausible as part of a distributed interpretive process,
not by itself a Roycean interpreting self.

Fabbrichesi's Royce–Peirce comparison reinforces the self/community side: the Community of
Interpretation is a public triadic sign relation, but her interpreter remains the self serving other
selves, not an apparatus.

**Sources:** Royce, *Problem of Christianity*, II, 140–50, 211–16 / 1968, 286–90, 315–17.
Peter Skagestad, “Peirce, Virtuality, and Semiotic,” paper presented at the Twentieth World Congress
of Philosophy, Boston, 1998, §§ on semiosis and logical machines, no DOI.
Rossella Fabbrichesi, “The Body of the Community: Peirce, Royce, and Nietzsche,” *European Journal of
Pragmatism and American Philosophy* 1, no. 1 (2009), no DOI located.

**Evidence:** Royce passages and locators are verified in `royce_card.md`. Skagestad: interpretation
is “the creation in the interpreter's mind of a new sign, which Peirce labeled the ‘interpretant’”;
human reasoning depends on external “hard” and “soft” machinery, but whether a future machine could
perform full theorematic reasoning was left open. Fabbrichesi, PDF pp. 1–4, treats public
interpretation as constitutive of community and identifies the interpreter's office through service
and self-surrender.

**Relation:** SUPPORTS B-R3; REFRAMES A-R5 and the second half of A-R6; SUPPORTS the “all terms are
selves” limit in B-R4.

## Finding 10 — The best non-human company divides into organisms, cognitive tools, and quasi-semiotic machines (high where full text; C for Nöth body) [A/C]

Three adjacent literatures must not be collapsed:

1. **Biosemiotics:** Hoffmeyer puts genuine interpretive agency in living receptive systems. Semiosis
   is constitutive of life; organisms interpret environmental cues; bees can stand in the place of
   persons as sites for interpretants. This genuinely seats non-human organisms in interpretive
   processes, but it draws the boundary at life rather than licensing a camera.
2. **Extended/artifactual cognition:** Skagestad makes pens, inkstands, notations, and machines
   constituents and instruments of human reasoning. The artifact is part of the cognitive process,
   but his conclusion is augmentation, not autonomous apparatus interpretation.
3. **Machine semiosis:** Nöth's “Semiotic Machines” is the most direct candidate. Its retrieved
   abstract says machines can participate in quasi-semiosis and distinguishes this from genuine
   semiosis requiring self-control, autopoiesis, self-reference, and creativity. Because only the
   abstract was retrieved, this finding remains `C` for any claim about his detailed argument.

T. L. Short's chapter abstract says mature Peircean semiotics admits interpretants outside thought,
especially animal behavior. It is abstract-only (`C`) and supports nonmental interpretants, not an
autonomous camera interpreter. Colapietro's retrieved full text moves away from mentalism and toward
social, somatic, situated semiosis, but the passages located discuss interpretation of artifacts and
human practices; they do not put an artifact in the interpreter seat.

The chapter therefore has contemporary company for a distributed interpretive process and for
non-human living interpreters. It does not yet have `A`-class authority for the camera itself as an
autonomous interpreter. The strongest honest formulation is: “the camera participates in, constrains,
and externalizes interpretation; calling it the interpreter is my Peirce-adjacent extension beyond
Royce.”

**Sources:** Jesper Hoffmeyer, “The Natural History of Intentionality: A Biosemiotic Approach,” in
Theresa Schilhab, Frederik Stjernfelt, and Terrence Deacon, eds., *The Symbolic Species Evolved*
(Dordrecht: Springer, 2012), 97–116, https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-007-2336-8_6.
Skagestad, “Peirce, Virtuality, and Semiotic.”
Winfried Nöth, “Semiotic Machines,” *Cybernetics and Human Knowing* 9, no. 1 (2002): 5–21,
body not retrieved; abstract only, `C`.
T. L. Short, “Semeiosis and the Mental,” in *Peirce's Theory of Signs* (Cambridge: Cambridge
University Press, 2007), 289–316, https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511498350.012, abstract only, `C`.
Vincent Colapietro, “Toward a Truly Pragmatic Theory of Signs: Reading Peirce's Semeiotic in Light of
Dewey's Gloss,” in John Muller and Joseph Brent, eds., *Peirce, Semiotics, and Psychoanalysis*
(Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 2004), author-hosted full text; no DOI located.

**Evidence:** Hoffmeyer, pp. 97–116 manuscript page markers corresponding to PDF pp. 10–13:
biosemiotics “takes sign processes or semiosis to be constitutive for life”; a sign process “requires
an interpretative agency”; signs elicit “an interpretative process in an organism”; and all organisms
must read cues. Skagestad's conclusion calls machines instruments essential to human reasoning while
leaving full machine reasoning open. Nöth's abstract: machines “can certainly participate in
processes of quasi-semiosis.” Short's abstract: “there are interpretants outside thought as well, in
animal behavior especially.” Colapietro's full text describes a move away from mentalistic signs
toward situated, social, somatic, semiotic human agency.

**Relation:** gives qualified contemporary couch for A-R5, A-R6, B-R3, and B-R4; rejects a simple
camera-is-interpreter attribution to either Royce or settled Peircean machine semiotics.

## Finding 11 — Digital Royce scholarship exists, but it argues for richer communication rather than automatic platform community (C)

Kegley's “Royce on Self and Relationships: Speaking to the Digital and Texting Self of Today” is a
direct contemporary digital application. Its retrieved abstract argues that Royce's account of self,
relationships, and community diagnoses anonymous online self-presentation, weakened ethical
commitment, isolation, and the erosion of face-to-face communication. This is relevant opposition to
the platform-memory thesis, not support for automatic digital membership.

Because only the abstract was retrieved, all substantive use remains `C`. No full-text source located
applies Royce's three conditions to platform logs, app assignment, or algorithmic enrollment.

**Source:** Jacquelyn Ann K. Kegley, “Royce on Self and Relationships: Speaking to the Digital and
Texting Self of Today,” *Journal of Speculative Philosophy* 32, no. 2 (2018): 285–303,
https://doi.org/10.5325/jspecphil.32.2.0285. Abstract only, `C`.

**Evidence:** The abstract says Royce's theory of community addresses isolation and loneliness
promoted by the Internet and the “starving of our capacity to communicate face-to-face” fostered by
phone technology.

**Relation:** COMPLICATES A-R10 and A-R11; does not close them as a direct application.

## How this lands in §§3 and 7

The chapter can keep its strongest insight by making the boundary explicit: Royce gives community
without encounter, not community without communication. Replace the slogan with “communicable
contemporaries can constitute a community around a past they each take as their own even if they
never meet.” Then make the film application conditional: the camera supplies a record and the city
supplies a candidate common past; later appropriation and communication would constitute the
community of memory.

The loyalty argument should be narrowed, not discarded. A platform log cannot satisfy the
members'-side appropriation condition, and true loyalty remains necessary for conscious/genuine
Roycean community. But a thin community of memory is defined by the three conditions, not by loyalty
as a fourth condition. “Platform can meet memory conditions and still fail loyalty” is an authorial
hypothesis that must show communication and appropriation before it reaches the loyalty question.

Finally, retain the honesty flag on the camera. Peirce, Skagestad, Hoffmeyer, Nöth, Short, and
Colapietro make the extension intelligible, but they do not erase its cost. The precise claim with
contemporary company is that the camera is an external sign-producing component in a distributed
interpretive process. Calling it the interpreter itself remains beyond Royce and stronger than the
full-text Peircean evidence retrieved here.
