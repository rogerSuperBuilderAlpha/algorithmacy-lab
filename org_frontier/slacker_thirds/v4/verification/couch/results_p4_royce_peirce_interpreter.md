# Deep research: can a medium interpret? Royce, Peirce, non-personal interpreters (Packet 4)

**Run date:** 12 August 2026. **Angles:** four — (1) Peirce studies on the interpretant/interpreter distinction and the warrant for a non-person in the interpreting seat; (2) machine semiosis, biosemiotics, cybersemiotics, and semiotic engineering; (3) Royce scholarship on the restriction of the three terms to selves, plus Hayles on nonconscious cognition; (4) film and media theory on the apparatus as a term rather than an instrument, plus the Luhmannian alternative. **Verification:** three-lens adversarial, every lens required to state the source's own thesis before ruling.

Fifty-six claims went to verification. Thirty survived; twenty-six were refuted — fifteen by two lenses, eleven by all three. Of the survivors, eleven carry a unanimous 3-0 vote and nineteen carry a 2-1 with a live dissent, and in this run the dissent is usually the finding: the dissenting lens almost never says the quotation is wrong, it says the quantifier around the quotation is. A further nine items came back unreachable and were never voted on; they sit under "Do NOT use" with the retrieval that would close each.

Gap rows: the claim records tag **A-R5** explicitly and repeatedly, on both its branches. No claim in this packet carries an **A-R6**, **B-R3**, or **B-R4** tag; the four angles ran against the working questions Q1–Q4. I have not guessed at what those three rows say. See "Disposition of gap rows," where each is marked open with the specific retrieval that would close it.

**The run's central question came back negative. Read the Absences section before writing a word.** No source in any of the four literatures — Peirce studies, machine semiosis, biosemiotics, film theory, media theory — seats a camera, or any manufactured device, in the interpreter's place between two parties. The one Peircean who names cameras in exactly this debate puts them in the sign-*producing* seat. The one film theorist who comes near it counts his three terms as director, film, and public. What the run does supply is a lineage that depersonalises the interpreter without vacating it (Peirce's Quasi-mind), a scholarly consensus that Royce's restriction to selves is load-bearing rather than incidental, and a precise statement of what the extension costs. The extension is the author's. The dossier's job is to make owning it cheap.

## Finding 1 — Peirce marks the requirement that a sign act upon a person as a concession, not a doctrine (high confidence on Peirce, low on the gloss, 2-1) [C2]

Peirce's sentence to Lady Welby is the anchor of the whole packet, and it is solid: two lenses reached it verbatim through independent full texts (Legg 2008, Koshkin 2020), both giving the Hardwick 1977: 80-81 locator, and one lens reached it inside an accepted manuscript of Cobley's chapter. What splits the vote is everything wrapped around the sentence. The dissenting lens refuted the claim on two grounds. First, "disowned" overstates: Peirce keeps "upon a person" inside the definition he sends Welby and explains it as a concession — he does not repudiate it. Second, and more consequentially for this chapter, the broader conception Peirce despairs of communicating does not empty the seat, it depersonalises it: the place is held by a Quasi-interpreter that is still mind-like (see Finding 2). A chapter reading the sop as a licence for a non-mind-like interpreter reads past the evidence.

The Cobley half failed at two lenses out of three. Bloomsbury Collections serves a login wall, Unpaywall reports the chapter closed, and the Middlesex repository returned 405. One lens did retrieve the accepted manuscript and confirmed the sentence about Fisch and anthropocentrism verbatim, in the section named — but from an unpaginated author version, so no published page locator exists for it. Provenance for the Peirce half: class A/B. For the Cobley gloss: class C at best. **SUPPORTS** the warrant for extending the interpreting role past persons, in the depersonalised-but-mind-like form only. Bears on A-R5.

**Sources:** C. S. Peirce to Victoria Lady Welby, 23 December 1908, in Charles S. Hardwick (ed.), *Semiotic and Significs* (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1977), 80-81 ; no DOI ; convergence-verified in Catherine Legg, "Making It Explicit and Clear," *Metaphilosophy* 39(1) (2008): 105-123, n. 10 ; DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9973.2008.00527.x. Paul Cobley, "Peirce in Contemporary Semiotics," in Tony Jappy (ed.), *The Bloomsbury Companion to Contemporary Peircean Semiotics* (2020), 31-72 ; DOI 10.5040/9781350076143.ch-001 ; paywalled.

**Evidence:** "I define a Sign as anything which is so determined by something else, called its Object, and so determines an effect upon a person, which effect I call its Interpretant, that the latter is thereby mediately determined by the former. My insertion of 'upon a person' is a sop to Cerberus, because I despair of making my own broader conception understood" (SS: 80-1).

*Correction applied, from lens 1:* "Peirce marks the requirement that a sign act upon a person as a concession rather than doctrine … and Cobley reads the sop as a refusal of 'a general anthropocentrism.'"

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "What the broader conception substitutes for 'person' is not nothing but a quasi-mind: signs require 'a Quasi-utterer and a Quasi-interpreter' (Monist 16.4, 1906, 523). [Cobley's gloss on Fisch and Eco: unverified, do not print without checking pp. 31-72.]"

## Finding 2 — In his own published statement of sign structure Peirce names the interpreter a "Quasi-mind," and makes the two quasi-minds a requirement of logic rather than of psychology (high confidence, 3-0 on the p. 504-5 half [C52], 2-1 on the p. 523 half [C53])

This is the strongest thing the run found, and it is a primary source in the public domain. All three lenses read the 1906 *Monist* original — which sidesteps the copyright problem with *Collected Papers* vol. 4 — and all three confirmed that Peirce writes "the Quasi-mind that is the Interpreter." One correction of detail is unanimous: the sentence begins at the foot of p. 504 and completes on p. 505, so cite 504-5.

The 2-1 on C53 turns on a modal operator, and the dissent is right about it. What Peirce declares "not merely a fact of human Psychology, but a necessity of Logic" is that every logical evolution of thought should be *dialogic* — not that the interpreter may be non-personal. Two further cautions the chapter should print rather than let a referee find. Peirce disowns the passage's rigour on the next page: "You may say that all this is loose talk; and I admit that, as it stands, it has a large infusion of arbitrariness." And the widening is bounded: the Quasi-utterer and Quasi-interpreter "are at one (i.e. are one mind) in the sign itself." Quasi-mind displaces the person, not the mind. That boundary is exactly where the camera argument has to do its work, and naming it early costs the chapter nothing. Class A. **SUPPORTS** the depersonalising of the interpreter's seat; **COMPLICATES** any use of it that skips the quasi-*mind*. Bears on A-R5.

**Sources:** Charles S. Peirce, "Prolegomena to an Apology for Pragmaticism," *The Monist* 16, no. 4 (October 1906): 492-546, at 504-5 and 523 ; no DOI ; Internet Archive scan, identifier `sim_monist_1906-10_16_4`. Conventionally CP 4.536 and CP 4.551 — **the CP paragraph mapping was not verified in this run.**

**Evidence:** "I have already noted that a Sign has an Object and an Interpretant, the latter being that which the Sign produces in the Quasi-mind that is the Interpreter by determining the latter to a feeling, to an exertion, or to a Sign, which determination is the Interpretant" (504-5). And: "Thought is not necessarily connected with a brain. It appears in the work of bees, of crystals, and throughout the purely physical world … Moreover, signs require at least two Quasi-minds; a Quasi-utterer and a Quasi-interpreter; and although these two are at one (i.e. are one mind) in the sign itself, they must nevertheless be distinct. In the Sign they are, so to say, welded. Accordingly, it is not merely a fact of human Psychology, but a necessity of Logic, that every logical evolution of thought should be dialogic" (523).

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "What he calls 'a necessity of Logic' is that every logical evolution of thought be dialogic; and he adds at once that the passage 'has a large infusion of arbitrariness'. The camera move stands inside this lineage only to the extent that the camera can count as a quasi-mind."

## Finding 3 — A Peircean communication theorist makes utterer and interpreter functional roles rather than agents, and the word that looked like an exclusion does not exclude (medium confidence, 2-1) [C16]

The claim went to verification carrying its own warning flag: check the word "nonmechanical" before relying on it. Two lenses checked, and the word is genuinely there. Then the third lens read the sentence structure and refuted the claim's use of it, correctly. Bergman's functionalist thesis — utterer and interpreter "do not necessarily refer to individual agents; they are rather whatever may function in a manner similar to an utterer and interpreter in dialogue" — is stated with no agent restriction whatever. "Nonmechanical" appears one sentence later, inside a doubly hedged corollary ("one could argue that virtually any nonmechanical agent …"), and what it qualifies is a *different* proposition: that a single agent occupies both roles at every stage. Bergman nowhere excludes mechanical agents from either role.

So the passage is friendlier to the chapter than the claim allowed, and the chapter should not manufacture an opponent out of a passing qualifier. One further point the first lens raised and the chapter should carry: Bergman's warrant for the non-individuality is Peirce's Platonic internal dialogue, "where different aspects of the self function as utterers and interpreters" — the non-individuality is intra-personal in the first instance. Provenance: the passage came from a licensed full-text index, not the printed page; pagination unconfirmed. Class B/C — **label second-hand as to locator.** **BOTH.** Bears on A-R5.

**Sources:** Mats Bergman, "Experience, Purpose, and the Value of Vagueness: On C. S. Peirce's Contribution to the Philosophy of Communication," *Communication Theory* 19(3) (2009): 248-277 ; DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2885.2009.01343.x ; paywalled, passage retrieved via full-text index.

**Evidence:** "The concepts of 'utterer' and 'interpreter' do not necessarily refer to individual agents; they are rather whatever may function in a manner similar to an utterer and interpreter in dialogue. Peirce sometimes emphasizes this by speaking of the 'quasi-utterer' and the 'quasi-interpreter,' and follows Plato in conceptualizing thought as an internal dialogue, where different aspects of the self function as utterers and interpreters. Building on this point of view, one could argue that virtually any nonmechanical agent involved in communication functions as utterer and interpreter at all stages of the process … In this sense, the utterer and the interpreter are analytical abstractions that designate different functions in communicational transactions."

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "The thesis is stated without restriction as to what kind of thing may fill a role. The word 'nonmechanical' enters only in a hedged corollary … which concerns whether a single agent holds both roles at once, not whether a machine may hold either. Bergman states no exclusion of mechanical agents."

## Finding 4 — Nöth names cameras exactly once, in the sign-producing list, and holds that a device in a communicative chain is a medium or an extension rather than a term that interprets (high confidence, 2-1 on C6, 3-0 on C23) [C6, C23]

These two claims are one finding: Nöth's counter-position, reached once in his own paper and once through Brier's long quotation of him. Both quotations are verbatim in both sources, and the structural point survives all six lens-readings. "Camera" occurs exactly once in "Semiotic Machines," in the list of "machines which produce signs," so the reading is not cherry-picked. And the distinction the chapter needs is on the page: "the mediation of signs by means of machines must be distinguished from the nature of sign processing within machines."

Three narrowings the chapter must absorb, since a referee will find each. Nöth asks "Are they semiotic machines?" of the whole list and then answers only for the copy machine and the pencil; he never adjudicates the camera. His framework is graded rather than binary — "the concept of quasi-sign thus suggests degrees of semioticity" — and he grants machines quasi-mind. And the ground he gives for denying the triad is a missing *object* relation, not a missing self, which is a different objection from the one the chapter is expecting to meet. The dissent on C6 rests on the evaluative superlative ("the field's standard authority") and on the seat-assignment metaphor, which Nöth explicitly refuses in favour of "a gradual continuum." Both charges are fair. Class A. **REFUTES** — this is the rival reading of the camera the chapter must answer. Bears on A-R5.

**Sources:** Winfried Nöth, "Semiotic Machines," §§1.3, 2.1, read at the Wayback capture `https://web.archive.org/web/20090716063214/http://www.library.utoronto.ca/see/SEED/Vol3-3/Winfried.htm` (host dead; the captured page carries no masthead, so the SEED volume/issue/year and the *Cybernetics & Human Knowing* 9(1), 2002 print record are both **unverified**, and Crossref does not index that journal). Søren Brier, "The Cybersemiotic Model of Communication: An Evolutionary View on the Threshold between Semiosis and Informational Exchange," *tripleC* 1, no. 1 (2003): 71-94, at 82-83 ; DOI 10.31269/triplec.v1i1.6 ; open access.

**Evidence:** §1.3: "For example, the mediation of signs by means of machines must be distinguished from the nature of sign processing within machines." §2.1: "Typewriters, copy machines, cameras, and tape recorders, e.g., are machines which produce signs. Are they semiotic machines? If semiosis is required, a copy machine can certainly not be called a semiotic machine although it may be said to produce signs. After all, a pencil is also involved in sign production, but it can hardly be considered to be the sufficient cause of an interpretant." Nöth quoted in Brier at 83: "What is missing for these signs to develop from dyadic to triadic signs is an object relationship. […] the messages produced by a computer in the interface of humans and machines are either messages conveyed by a human sender and mediated by the computer or they are quasi-signs resulting from an automatic and deterministic extension of human semiosis" (Nöth 2002: 8).

*Correction applied, from lens 3 on C6:* "Nöth lists cameras among the 'machines which produce signs' and then asks, without answering for the camera, 'Are they semiotic machines?' … His framework is a graded one — he replaces the machine/organism dualism with 'a gradual continuum' and grants that 'there is (quasi-)mind not only in the brain, but also in the machine', while holding this to be 'only a necessary, but not yet a sufficient condition of genuine semiosis' (§3.4)."

## Finding 5 — Nöth's own conclusion refuses a criterial threshold: no criterion of semiosis is wholly absent from machines, and the remaining difference is one of degree (high confidence, 3-0) [C7]

The concession inside the refutation, and unanimous. All three lenses read §5.3 and confirmed the block quotation word for word. Two lenses went further than the claim needed and found the sentences that follow: abduction is what a "truly creative 'reasoning machine'" would have to add to deduction, and "first steps to cross this semiotic threshold from quasi-semiosis to genuine semiosis have in fact been done in Artificial Intelligence research." Nöth even reaches a positive verdict on one case: "Such a missile, we must conclude, is hence a genuinely semiotic machine."

The lenses agree on where the claim is loose. "Refuses a hard threshold" sits in tension with Nöth's own retention of the word *threshold* for the crossing, and with his abstract, which tilts the other way: "it is in fact questionable whether machines produced by humans, can already be described as capable of triggering genuinely semiotic processes." The two are compatible — no threshold in principle, uncertainty about actual machines — but quoting §5.3 while the abstract hedges invites the charge of selective reading. Name the abstract and the tension disappears. One citation correction, unanimous: the capture that resolves is `www.library.utoronto.ca/see/SEED/Vol3-3/Winfried.htm`, not `see.library.utoronto.ca`. Class A. **SUPPORTS.** Bears on A-R5.

**Sources:** Winfried Nöth, "Semiotic Machines," §5.3 "Conclusion and the vision of poetic machines" ; `https://web.archive.org/web/20090716063214/http://www.library.utoronto.ca/see/SEED/Vol3-3/Winfried.htm`.

**Evidence:** "A result of our study has been that none of the criteria of semiosis is completely absent from the world of machines. On the one hand Peirce's concepts of semiosis and quasi-semiosis are so broad that not even matter and mind are separated by a semiotic threshold. On the other hand, the history of engineering has come up with so many types of intelligent machines that no single semiotic criterion can any longer be found to be completely absent from the world of machines. First steps to cross this semiotic threshold from quasi-semiosis to genuine semiosis have in fact been done in Artificial Intelligence research."

*Correction applied, from lens 1:* "Cite the capture that resolves: web.archive.org/web/20090716063214/http://www.library.utoronto.ca/see/SEED/Vol3-3/Winfried.htm."

## Finding 6 — Nöth elsewhere lets a printed word hold the office of interpreter, but the office he assigns it is the interpretant's (high confidence, 2-1) [C8]

All three lenses reached the publisher's full text and confirmed both quotations, including the embedded Peirce locator. The dissent is a seat-correction and it is the useful part of the finding. What Nöth puts in "the office of interpreter" is the interpret*ant* — the triad's third correlate, another sign, the printed word "man" — and he says so in the same breath: "an interpreter, alias interpretant." Peirce's own sentence is a simile ("it fulfills the office of an interpreter, *who* says that a foreigner says the same thing which he himself says"), not the seating of an artefact between two parties. The claim's phrase "a text interpreting one party to another" supplies an addressee the passage does not have: the learner needs the interpretant, and nobody is being interpreted *to* anyone.

The second lens supplies the reading that keeps the finding useful without over-reaching: the seat Nöth opens is the interpretant's, "which is exactly the seat Royce built his interpreter on." Note also the tension worth flagging in the chapter rather than hiding — this is the same Nöth who in "Semiotic Machines" denies machines the triadic object relation. Class A. **SUPPORTS**, at one remove. Bears on A-R5.

**Sources:** Winfried Nöth, "The Semiotics of Learning New Words," *Journal of Philosophy of Education* 48(3) (2014): 446-456 ; DOI 10.1111/1467-9752.12076.

**Evidence:** "Our learner of the word *homme* uses a dictionary because he or she needs an interpreter, alias interpretant, to make sense of the unknown French word. The role of the interpreter is assumed by the familiar word *man* found besides *homme* in the dictionary entry." Quoting Peirce: "Such a mediating representation may be termed an interpretant, because it fulfills the office of an interpreter, who says that a foreigner says the same thing which he himself says (CP 1.553 and W 2, pp. 53-4, 1867)." And, elsewhere in the same article: "whether a word is a word does not depend on any particular interpretation by some actual interpreter. Words have a life of their own."

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "What holds the office is the interpretant, the triad's third correlate; Nöth does not make the dictionary a party that interprets one person to another."

## Finding 7 — Nadin argues the computer is a semiotic engine on a claimed equivalence between automata and Peircean sign processes, and lodges it as a processor and self-interpreter of signs rather than a term between two parties (medium-high confidence, 2-1) [C17]

The most confident machine-side claim the run reached, and the citation needed repair. All three lenses confirmed the quotations verbatim; two read the author's deposited PDF at nadin.ws, whose running head gives "pp. 85-114," and the third read the published OA version and Crossref, which give pp. 57-75. **The published pagination is 57-75 and the DOI is 10.37693/pjos.2007.1.8815** — cite that, not the deposit's variant line. One lens reported nadin.ws returning 404 for the file, so the deposit may no longer be a reliable route.

Two substantive narrowings. Nadin does not prove the equivalence here; he refers it to Nadin 1977, 1978, 1981 and gives a summary of the proof. And his own strengthening runs through self-interpretation — "a semiotic engine is not pure and simple an engine, but one with a certain self-awareness … with the mechanism of self-interpretation embedded in the process" — while the validity of the machine's output is tied outside it: "always pragmatic, i.e., in reference to the human being's practical performance." That is a machine interpreting its own states, not a machine interpreting one party to another. The dissent additionally strikes "in the strict sense" (Nadin's term of art is "semiotic engine") and "the most confident claim available" (a superlative over an unsurveyed field). Class A. **BOTH.** Bears on A-R5.

**Sources:** Mihai Nadin, "Semiotic Machine," *Public Journal of Semiotics* 1, no. 1 (January 2007): 57-75 ; DOI 10.37693/pjos.2007.1.8815 ; open access (Lund).

**Evidence:** "In proving the equivalence between automata and sign processes (in Peirce's definition, since all other known definitions are particular cases), a methodological foundation for the entire discussion regarding the semiotic machine has been established (cf. Nadin 1977, 1978, 1981) … S = S(R, O, I, o, i), which is equivalent to A = A(X, Y, Q, α, β) … Every automaton is a generative semiotics. *Correction applied, from lens 3:* "Nadin argues that the computer is a semiotic engine, on the equivalence he claims to have proved … (*Public Journal of Semiotics* I(1), 2007, 57-75, at 65-66, DOI 10.37693/pjos.2007.1.8815) … it lodges the machine as processor and self-interpreter of signs, not as a term interpreting one party to another."

## Finding 8 — The Peircean intelligence-augmentation line puts the machine in the sign-vehicle and prosthesis role, and does not itself seat a machine as interpreter — but it records that Peirce left the question open (medium confidence, 2-1) [C9]

All three lenses read the 1998 Paideia paper in full and confirmed both quotations, including the CP 7.366 locator against Skagestad's own note 16. The unanimous correction is the universal negative. "Never in the interpreter's seat" is asserted over three works of which two were metadata-only, and it is falsified inside the one paper actually read: Skagestad reports that Peirce thought simple inference "could be observed not only in logic machines, but in any kind of machine, including blocks of wood," and states flatly that "the question whether future machines might be able to reason was explicitly left open by Peirce." The dissenting lens adds a taxonomic point worth heeding — Skagestad distinguishes "external sign vehicles, both 'hard' … and 'soft'" from "external tools for sign production," and files the inkstand under the latter — and one dating trap: Skagestad dates CP 7.366 to 1905 while Nöth dates the same passage 1902. Do not launder that dispute into the chapter's own citation. Class A for the 1998 paper; class C for the 1993 and 1996 articles, which are Crossref-verified and unread. **REFUTES**, with the door left ajar. Bears on A-R5.

## Finding 9 — Semiotic engineering puts designers, systems and users in one ontological category and makes the interface the designers' deputy — the utterer's chair by proxy, not the interpreter's (medium-high confidence, 2-1) [C10]

Two lenses reached the published Springer PDF and fixed the locators: the deputy sentence on p. 237, the metacommunication sentence on p. 238. Both corrected the claim's provenance note — the chapter is *not* paywalled, the publisher PDF carries real pagination, and neither lens could find the author version the claim cited at sedici.unlp.edu.ar. Cite 237 and 238. The third lens could not reach the text at all and refuted on that ground plus the superlative "the nearest live contemporary company."

The seat problem is where all three converge, and the chapter should lead with the fix rather than the metaphor. De Souza's deputy *speaks for* the absent designers — "spokesperson," "the interface speaks for designers at interaction time" — which is a proxy utterer, not a mediating interpreter; and she installs a symmetrical "user's deputy" on the other side. The strongest warrant here is not the deputy metaphor at all but the ontological-category sentence on p. 237, which puts systems on a par with designers and users as interlocutors. Lead with that and the deputy becomes the illustration rather than the argument. Class A. **SUPPORTS**, in the weakened form: company for the claim that an artefact can hold a term in a communicative structure, not for the claim that it can hold the interpreting term. Bears on A-R5.

**Sources:** Clarisse Sieckenius de Souza, "Semiotic Engineering — A New Paradigm for Designing Interactive Systems," in *The Past and Future of Information Systems: 1976-2006 and Beyond*, IFIP AICT 214 (Boston: Springer US, 2006), 231-242, at 237-238 ; DOI 10.1007/978-0-387-34732-5_21 ; ISBN 9780387346311 ; publisher PDF freely downloadable. Book-length statement: *The Semiotic Engineering of Human-Computer Interaction* (MIT Press, 2005) — **not read.**

**Evidence:** p. 237: "Semiotic engineering proposes to connect both ends by postulating that designers/developers, systems, and users, all belong to the same ontological category: they are all interlocutors whose conversations are inter-related … The system's interface speaks for designers at interaction time … To design a system thus amounts to designing a rational mechanic spokesperson that will tell users what sense (predicted or not) to make of the artifact." ## Finding 10 — Thesis 7 of the biosemiotic joint statement locates interpretive capacity in a self-propagating system and refuses an extrinsic interpreter — but the refusal is scoped, and the alternative it rules out is a homunculus, not a device (high confidence, 2-1) [C19]

And devices, machines and artefacts appear nowhere in the thesis, so the opposition is intrinsic-versus-extrinsic, with a homunculus as the contrast term. The thesis supplies no exclusion of manufactured things either way. Class A. **REFUTES**, but far more weakly than the claim asserted. Bears on A-R5.

## Finding 11 — Kull makes choice criterial for interpretation and consigns algorithmic, IF/THEN selection to the non-semiotic side (high confidence, 3-0) [C21]

## Finding 14 — Royce's own definitional sentence glosses only two of the three terms, and expressly admits a text into the object seat (high confidence, 2-1) [C47]

**The central absence.** No source in any of the four literatures seats a camera, or any manufactured device, in the interpreter's place between two parties. Four independent angle agents report this separately and in the same words. The only Peircean who names cameras in this exact debate — Nöth, §2.1 — puts them among "machines which produce signs," and denies that a sign-producing device is "the sufficient cause of an interpretant." On the chapter's own decisive test, the camera-as-interpreter claim has no company. The extension is the author's.

**The Peircean widening runs to life and to nature, not to artefacts.** Every expansive authority reached — Deely, Stjernfelt via Cobley, Nöth on the sunflower at CP 2.274, Peirce's own MS 318 chameleon-insects-plants, and Peirce's bees and crystals at *Monist* 16.4, 523 — widens toward organisms, crystals and the physical world. Searches for a Peircean who widens specifically to designed technical objects *in the interpreting role* returned only the machine-semiosis literature, which is either hedged (Nöth) or reframes the question as sign-processing rather than interpreting (Nadin, Skagestad).

**No Royce commentator calls the restriction to selves incidental.** Everything reached treats it as doctrinally load-bearing: Smith via Corrington, Corrington himself, Parker in the SEP, Kegley in the IEP. The absence of the opposite verdict is itself the finding. But note the converse absence, which is equally real and cuts the other way: **no Royce scholar was found who explicitly argues that a Roycean interpreter must be a self.** Searches across all 23 Crossref records in *Transactions of the Charles S. Peirce Society* matching "Royce interpretation," the open-access *EJPAP* (Fabbrichesi 2010, Pfeifer 2016, both read in full), and both reference entries turned up nobody pressing the question. The restriction is attested in Royce's own text (II, 140-42, 148, 210-13), not in scholarship about it.

**No film or media theorist cites Royce.** Scholar Gateway semantic search on Royce's triadic interpretation applied to cinema, film narration, media, photography and the camera returned 12 passages across 11 articles, none about film. Crossref and Internet Archive full-text search-inside returned nothing connecting *The Problem of Christianity*'s triad to the apparatus, the camera, the edit, or film narration. The chapter has no Roycean company in film studies and should say so.

**No sustained Peircean-interpretant account of the camera.** Deleuze maps Firstness, Secondness and Thirdness to affection-, action- and relation-images and uses "interpretation" and "dicisign," but the word *interpretant* does not occur anywhere in the Minnesota translation of *Cinema 1* — zero hits on a grep over the full text. Wollen's Peirce in *Signs and Meaning in the Cinema* is the icon/index/symbol trichotomy, not the triadic act. The Peircean lineage in film theory runs through the sign-type taxonomy.

**No "camera as interlocutor" formula in ethnographic film.** The exact phrase returns nothing in Internet Archive full-text. MacDougall's and Rouch's arguments about the camera mediating between filmed subjects and audience were not reached, and on structural grounds this is the likeliest remaining place to find a genuine party-to-party hit. **Recommended follow-up: David MacDougall, *Transcultural Cinema* (Princeton, 1998), and Jean Rouch on "shared anthropology."**

**Two corrections to the brief's own premises, both from the primary text.** (i) The Colorado Canyon passage is *not* subjunctive throughout: the governing sentence at II, 146 is indicative with modal hedges. But nor is it the indicative warrant a second claim took it for — Royce introduces the whole generalization through a counterfactual observer at II, 145 ("could, were he present, interpret to a possible future observer"), calls the result "an order of *possible* interpretation," and marks the generalization as his own departure from Peirce (II, 144). (ii) Royce does *not* require three distinct selves: "two or all three members may be wholly, or in part, identical" (II, 142). A web summary asserting the opposite is wrong and was discarded.

## How this lands in the chapter

Write the negative first, and write it flatly. Nobody has seated a camera in the interpreter's place — not in Peirce studies, not in machine semiosis, not in biosemiotics, not in film theory, not in media theory, and not in the two standard reference accounts of Royce. Four independent angles converged on that, and the convergence is worth more to the chapter than any single citation would have been. A section that opens by admitting it has no company reads as confidence; a section that hedges the same fact reads as evasion.

Then take the two things the run genuinely earned. Peirce depersonalised the interpreter's seat in print, in 1906, in the very lineage Royce credits on the page — "the Quasi-mind that is the Interpreter," and two quasi-minds as "a necessity of Logic." That is not a licence for the camera, because Peirce's quasi-mind is still a mind and is welded to the quasi-utterer in the sign, but it moves the burden: the chapter is not inventing an impersonal interpreter, it is asking whether a particular kind of impersonal thing can be one. And Royce leaves the *object* seat open to "the person or the meaning or the text" while naming only the addressee "the person" (II, 142). The asymmetry is his, not the chapter's.

Then price the extension honestly, and price it at the right place. The cost is not that Royce requires the interpreter to be a self in some bare sense — he allows two or all three members to be identical, and he writes "whatever or whoever that interpreter may be." The cost is at II, 213 and II, 207: the Community of Interpretation is composed of "many selves" whose three offices rotate at pleasure, and the three terms "appear, *in our explicitly human and social world*, as three distinct selves." A camera can hold the interpreter's chair for one turn. It cannot take the other two. State that, and the "extension is mine" flag stops being a disclaimer and becomes an argument.

Finally, meet the four objections in the order of their force. Kull's choice criterion is the sharpest, because it excludes a camera without appealing to minds at all — and the chapter's answer, if it has one, has to say what counts as an option handled non-sequentially. Nöth's is the most direct, because he names cameras and puts them among sign-*producers*; his own §5.3 supplies the reply, since he then refuses a criterial threshold and makes the difference one of degree. Metz's is the most awkward, because his depersonalised third term turns out to dissolve rather than depersonalise — the film folds back on itself and the addressee goes with the enunciator. And Hayles is not an objection at all but an embarrassment of a different kind: she has already put digital cameras on the cognizer side. On her account the camera cognizes, and what she withholds is exactly the register Royce's triad operates in. That is the sentence the chapter should end its literature section on, and it should quote it rather than paraphrase it.

Its title is the chapter's question and its subtitle is the chapter's method. Obtain it before the section is drafted; do not cite it, and do not characterise it, until someone has read it.
