# Deep research: Reich's New Property reception and the private extension (Packet 5)

Run date 2026-08-12. Four search angles: (1) Reich's own text and the mid-century reception (Van Alstyne, Monaghan, Mashaw, Merrill, Verkuil, Super); (2) the platform-governance and platform-property literatures (Klonick, Van Loo, Citron, Cohen, Kadri, Guggenberger, Rahman, Pasquale); (3) the empirical and regulatory record on deactivation (Rosenblat & Stark, Calo & Rosenblat, Dubal, the RDU/ALC survey, P2B, DSA, the Platform Work Directive, Seattle SMC 8.40); (4) genealogy and objections (Pound, Jones, Peñalver, the Simmel-third literature, numerus clausus). Eighty-five claims went to three-lens adversarial verification. Fifty-eight survived; twenty-one of those at 3-0, thirty-seven at 2-1. Twenty-seven were refuted — fourteen unanimously, thirteen by two lenses of three. The dossier below carries thirty-nine findings, several merging two or three claims that said the same thing.

Gap rows covered: A-C1, A-C2, A-C3, A-C4, A-C5, A-C6, A-C7, A-C8, B-C1, B-C2, B-C3, B-C4.

**The run's central question came back negative, and the reader should go to the Absences section before writing a word.** The chapter's hinge — that Reich *named* a grantor-power form, with the naming of an unreviewable withholding of reasons at 783 — does not survive contact with the page. Reich's 783 sentence sits inside his prescriptive Part V, is written entirely in the deontic, and is preceded by his own concession that the law developed without procedure. Both of the reception's most careful readers (Van Alstyne in 1977, Super in 2013) pin exactly that passage and classify it as advocacy. Separately, and just as consequential: nine platform-governance and platform-property articles were searched in full text for "Reich," "new property" and "largess," and every one returned zero. The literature the chapter is entering arrived at the grant structure without Reich. That is a result, not a gap — but it changes what the chapter can claim to be doing.

## Finding 1 — Reich's page-751 "grantor power" sentence describes the direction of procedural advantage, doubly hedged, and carries less than the chapter's A-C1 asks of it (high, 3-0 on C1 and C71; 2-1 on C29) [C1, C29, C71]

All three lenses retrieved the Yale Law School repository scan of 73 Yale L.J. 733 independently, page-mapped it against four supplied anchors, and confirmed the sentence verbatim at 751, opening Part II.C.1, "Procedures: in general." What they disagreed about is how much the sentence can bear. Lens 3 on C29 refused the framing outright: the sentence is hedged twice over ("in varying degrees," "tend to augment," "is likely to be"), it describes an asymmetry that procedures *produce* rather than enumerating any power the grantor holds, and it says nothing whatever about withholding reasons. That dissent is the finding. C29 and C71 read the same sentence in opposite directions, and C71 has the better of it — a point one lens made explicitly: the chapter cannot have both.

One correction the chapter must absorb rather than resist. Lens 3 on C1 noted that the sentence is not *purely* descriptive of procedure: it asserts that the short-cuts "augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient," so it *is* a statement about grantor power, effected procedurally. Saying it is not a statement of substantive powers over price, evaluation, removal or reasons is accurate. Saying it is not a statement of a grantor's powers would not be.

Reich uses the word "grantor" exactly once in fifty-five pages. A regex over the full text returns two hits in the family: "grantor" at 751 and "grantee" at 780, the latter inside a block quotation of the unconstitutional-conditions rule from *United States v. Chicago, M., St. P. & P.R.R.* Two lenses ran that count themselves rather than accepting it. COMPLICATES. Bears on A-C1 and A-C4.

**Sources:** Charles A. Reich, "The New Property," *The Yale Law Journal* 73, no. 5 (April 1964): 733–787, at 751 ; DOI 10.2307/794645 ; openyls.law.yale.edu, bitstream 013f54f6-ad84-45bb-94ff-59bff2d3c46b

**Evidence:** "The granting, regulation, and revocation of government largess is carried on by procedures which, in varying degrees, represent short-cuts that tend to augment the power of the grantor at the expense of the recipient. In the first place, the tribunal is likely to be an arm of the granting agency rather than independent and impartial." (751)

*Correction applied, from lens 3:* "Reich's page-751 sentence opens his treatment of procedure (II.C.1, 'Procedures: in general') and says that the procedures by which largess is granted, regulated and revoked are short-cuts that augment the grantor's power at the recipient's expense — power asserted through procedural design, not a taxonomy of substantive powers over price, evaluation, removal or reasons."

*Correction applied, from lens 3 (on C29):* "The chapter's 751 pin is correct, and the sentence there is descriptive rather than deontic … What it states as a form is the direction of procedural advantage, hedged twice over — not an enumerated grantor power, and not a power to withhold reasons, which appears in the article only at 783 and only as a demand."

## Finding 2 — The pin for "the grantor decides disputes in its own forum" is 751, the claim there is hedged, and the illustration is government contracts (high, 3-0) [C2]

Three lenses confirmed the quotation character for character, including the mid-sentence page break: 751 ends "…may turn out to be the government contracting" and 752 opens "officer, himself a party to the dispute, followed by a series of contract appeals boards likewise composed of government contracting officials." The bracketed "[officer]" in the chapter's version is correct and honestly marked. No lens dissented. The one thing to carry forward is Reich's own word — "likely" — and the fact that the forum he describes is an *arm of the granting agency*, not a private tribunal. That distinction is what sank the refuted C28, which glossed feature (3) at 770 as "a private forum for disputes"; the word "private" is nowhere in Reich, and in a chapter arguing the transposition to private doors it is precisely the word that must not be smuggled in. SUPPORTS. Bears on A-C4.

**Evidence:** "In the first place, the tribunal is likely to be an arm of the granting agency rather than independent and impartial. (751–52)

## Finding 3 — The legal form Reich names is feudal tenure over conditionally held wealth, recapitulated in eight features at 770 — offered as an analogy, and predicated of the state throughout (high, 3-0 on C4 and C64; 2-1 on C3) [C3, C4, C64]

Reich's own verb at 769 is "resembles." The philosophy of *Flemming v. Nestor*, *Barsky*, *In re Anastaplo* and *Cohen v. Hurley* "resembles the philosophy of feudal tenure"; the section heading is "The New Feudalism"; and at 770 he introduces the eight-feature list as "recapitulating *some* of the chief features of government largess" for the purpose of "the comparison to the general outlines of the feudal system." All three lenses read that as a label supplied by analogy rather than a doctrinal classification, and all three flagged that the chapter's phrase "the legal FORM" runs harder than the text.

The dissent on C3 is worth printing in full because it is arithmetical, not interpretive. Lens 3 refused the claim that 770 pins three of the chapter's four A-C4 propositions. On A-C4's own terms (writes the terms / changes them / own forum / owes no procedure), feature 3 pins the own-forum proposition and feature 5 pins the changes-them proposition — two, not three. Feature 6 is forfeiture, which belongs to A-C2's "remove," not to A-C4 at all. And 770 pins nothing for "owes no procedure," since Reich's argument at 783–85 is that procedure *should* be owed. A second lens independently reached the same count and added the point that matters most for the chapter: Reich writes "at the will of *the state*," not "at the grantor's will." The substitution is precisely the extension under test, not something 770 supplies.

C64's framing — that "Reich named the form" is defensible only if the form is stated as feudal tenure over conditionally held wealth, and not as "grant plus grantor powers" — went 3-0 and is the version the chapter should print. BOTH. Bears on A-C3 and A-C4.

**Sources:** Reich, "The New Property," at 769–770 (Part III.D, "The New Feudalism") ; DOI 10.2307/794645 ; openyls.law.yale.edu

**Evidence:** "The philosophy of Flemming v. Nestor, of Barsky, In Re Anastaplo, and Cohen v. Hurley, resembles the philosophy of feudal tenure. Wealth is not 'owned,' or 'vested' in the holders. Instead, it is held conditionally, the conditions being ones which seek to ensure the fulfillment of obligations imposed by the state." (769)

"The comparison to the general outlines of the feudal system may best be seen by recapitulating some of the chief features of government largess. … (3) the administration of the system has given rise to special laws and special tribunals, outside the ordinary structure of government; (4) the right to possess and use government largess is bound up with the recipient's legal status … hence the new wealth is not readily transferable; (5) individuals hold the wealth conditionally rather than absolutely … the obligations may be changed or increased at the will of the state; (6) for breach of condition the wealth may be forfeited or escheated back to the government; (7) the sovereign power is shared with large private interests; (8) the object of the whole system is to enforce 'the public interest' … by means of the distribution and use of wealth in such a way as to create and maintain dependence." (770)

*Correction applied, from lens 3 (on C3):* "Reich's nearest thing to a consolidated statement is the eight-item recap at 770, which he offers as 'recapitulating some of the chief features of government largess' for a feudal analogy. It supplies exact pins for two of A-C4's four propositions — special tribunals outside the ordinary structure of government (feature 3) and obligations that 'may be changed or increased at the will of the state' (feature 5) — plus forfeiture (feature 6), which pins the removal power rather than anything in A-C4. It supplies no pin for 'the counterparty writes the terms' and none for 'owes no procedure.'"

*Correction applied, from lens 3 (on C4):* "Reich does name the form of the holding … an analogy supplying the label, not a doctrinal classification of largess as tenure."

## Finding 4 — Reich's fourfold scheme at 740 is a taxonomy of judicial theories he reports in order to attack, not a taxonomy of powers — and it contains no pricing and no evaluation (high, 3-0 on both) [C5, C72]

Three lenses confirmed four italic run-in headings on 740 in this order — "Right vs. privilege," "The gratuity principle," "The whole and the parts," "Internal management" — followed verbatim by "Quite often these four theories are blurred in a single statement of judicial attitude." The reported-speech register is unmistakable in Reich's own grammar: "has often been considered," "Hence it is said that," "Under this theory." He attacks the doctrine directly at 787.

Two refinements, both from lenses that voted to keep the claim. Lens 1 caught an over-read: "Right vs. privilege" is a classificatory distinction governing how much protection attaches, not itself a theory that licenses grantor power; only the other three do that work. Lens 3 narrowed the superlative in C72: 740 sets out four theories, and while the gratuity principle is the nearest to a *withholding* power, "the whole and the parts" is at least as close for the power to dictate terms, and note 33's quotation from *Perkins v. Lukens Steel* ("the unrestricted power … to fix the terms and conditions") is closer still to a power over terms and price — a doctrine Reich reports in order to reject.

This finding is also what defeated the refuted C61, which claimed 740 contains "nothing corresponding to price, evaluate, remove, withhold." The page says in terms that "the state can withhold, grant, or revoke the largess at its pleasure." Two of four are on the page in Reich's own transcription of the doctrine. Only price and evaluation are genuinely absent. COMPLICATES. Bears on A-C1, A-C2 and A-C3.

**Sources:** Reich, "The New Property," at 739–741 and 740 n.31 (citing *Lynch v. United States*, 292 U.S. 571, 577 (1934)) ; DOI 10.2307/794645 ; openyls.law.yale.edu

**Evidence:** "The gratuity principle. Government largess has often been considered a 'gratuity' furnished by the state. Hence it is said that the state can withhold, grant, or revoke the largess at its pleasure. Under this theory, government is considered to be in somewhat the same position as a private giver. … Quite often these four theories are blurred in a single statement of judicial attitude." (740)

"It is time to see that the 'privilege' or 'gratuity' concept, as applied to wealth dispensed by government, is not much different from the absolute right of ownership that private capital once invoked to justify arbitrary power over employees and the public." (787)

*Correction applied, from lens 1:* "Reich's own fourfold scheme at 740 is a taxonomy of four traditional legal concepts that courts have used to limit individual rights in largess … three of which license expansive government power. It is not a taxonomy of four powers, and it includes neither pricing nor evaluation."

## Finding 5 — The livelihood locus at 741 reports which types of largess courts have in fact protected; it is not a description of grantor power (high, 3-0 on both) [C7, C85]

Both claims quote the same page and both went 3-0. C85 is the better-quoted version, because it carries the countervailing sentence that C7 elides: courts "have been reluctant to grant individual rights in those types of largess which seem to be exercises of the managerial functions of government, such as subsidies and government contracts." The chapter should print C85's pair, not C7's ellipsis, and lens 3 flagged two things about the C7 version worth honouring — the ellipsis silently spans a paragraph break and the subheading "Occupational licenses," so the "Doctors, lawyers, real estate brokers, and taxi drivers" sentence states the rule for that class rather than for largess at large; and "the criterion courts applied" is firmer than Reich's "most readily granted protection to those types which are intimately bound up with." He reports a tendency and a countervailing reluctance, not a stated test.

This page also does quiet damage to a flat "owes no procedure" reading, and lens 3 on C73 used it to refute one leg of that claim: Reich's picture is uneven protection, not its absence. COMPLICATES. Bears on A-C2 and A-C4.

**Sources:** Reich, "The New Property," at 741 (Part II.A, "Individual Rights In Largess") ; DOI 10.2307/794645 ; openyls.law.yale.edu

**Evidence:** "These sentiments are often voiced in the law of government largess, but individual interests have grown up nevertheless. The most common forms of protection are procedural… Development has varied mainly according to the particular type of wealth involved. The courts have most readily granted protection to those types which are intimately bound up with the individual's freedom to earn a living. *Correction applied, from lens 3 (on C7):* "At 741 Reich describes the pattern of judicial protection, not a grantor power … the 'Doctors, lawyers, real estate brokers, and taxi drivers' sentence sits under the subheading 'Occupational licenses' and states the rule for that class."

## Finding 6 — The 783 passage is a demand, not a description: it sits in the prescriptive Part V under "Procedural Safeguards," runs entirely in the deontic, and follows Reich's own concession that the law developed without procedure (high, 3-0 on C8; 2-1 on C67) [C8, C67]

This is the hinge, and on Reich's own text it goes against the chapter. Three lenses independently extracted the article's heading structure and confirmed it: Part V, "Toward Individual Stakes in the Commonwealth," begins at 778; A. Constitutional Limits (779); B. Substantive Limits (782); C. Procedural Safeguards (783); D. From Largess to Right (785). Every operative verb after the concession is "should" — "should be subject," "should be open," "should no longer be tolerated," "Nor should." One lens called it "the strongest claim in the set on the hinge question."

C67 pushed further and drew a dissent. Its word-count evidence is exact — "undisclosed" occurs precisely once in fifty-five pages, and a wider regex for withheld-grounds language returns hits only at 736, 747, 759, 760, 782 and 783, of which only 783 concerns withheld grounds for a denial. The defensible statement is narrower and still fatal to A-C1 in its strong form: Reich describes decision-making on unstated grounds, but he nowhere states a grantor's *power* to withhold reasons as a feature of the form. BOTH. Bears on A-C1 and A-C5.

## Finding 11 — The positivist trap is the standing objection to any entitlement theory, and three readers state it: Mashaw names it, Merrill generalises it, Monaghan states its endpoint as a reductio (high, 3-0 on C19; 2-1 on C17 and C18) [C17, C18, C19]

Take these together, because the chapter will meet them together. Mashaw's "two jaws" close on the threshold characterisation of the interest and on its later valuation. Merrill widens the diagnosis past the bitter-with-the-sweet quarrel: *Roth*'s method "effectively ceded the domain of constitutional property to governmental actors over which the Court, in its capacity as constitutional interpreter, had no control." Monaghan spells out where the logic ends — by clear statement a state could deny the underlying right and then owe only the procedures it chose to specify, so that "the 'right-privilege' distinction is back in full bloom."

Three corrections, each of which would cost the chapter a referee if unabsorbed.

First, the trap's jaws are *judicial*, not grantor-side. Lens 3 on C17 refused the claim's own summary on that ground, and it is correct on the quoted text: both jaws are things a court does. The grantor-defines/grantor-defines-away point belongs to the *Roth*/*Bishop* line, and needs the separate passage at 891, which should be quoted as the aside it is. That lens also could not retrieve Mashaw at all and classed the claim D; the other two lenses retrieved the article from the Yale repository (HeinOnline page stamps confirming 888 and 891) and verified both quotations verbatim, so the finding stands at class A with a named non-retrieval.

Second, Merrill hedges — "seemed to flow inexorably" — and the hedge is load-bearing, because he goes on to say the Court quashed the thesis. Two lenses caught the chapter dropping it. And the method Merrill indicts is the *Court's*, not Reich's; deploying this passage against Reich would misattribute it.

Third, Monaghan states his endpoint in order to destroy it. The very next sentence is "Bishop is wrongly reasoned, or at least wrongly phrased." Cited without that, Monaghan becomes authority for a proposition he denies. His subject is also "a state," not "the grantor" — substituting the generic grantor smuggles the private-door extension into a passage about state action. REFUTES. Bears on A-C1 and A-C4.

Thomas W. Merrill, "The Landscape of Constitutional Property," *Virginia Law Review* 86, no. 5 (2000): 885–999, at 923 ; DOI 10.2307/1073818. **Evidence:** "Analysis in terms of dignitary values holds out a prospect for avoiding the 'positivist trap' that plagues contemporary due process decisions. The trap has two jaws. It may spring shut either on the threshold question of the legal characterization of the interest 'held' by the complaining party (i.e., is it a 'life,' 'liberty,' or 'property' interest?), or later, when the court comes to assess the 'value' of that interest vis-a-vis governmental interests in efficiency, efficacy, or the like." ## How this lands in the chapter

The hinge has to move. Section 4 cannot say that Reich *named* the grantor's power to act without reasons, because he demanded its abolition — in the subjunctive, in a prescriptive part, after conceding that the law had grown up without procedure. Two of his most careful readers pin the same two pages and file them as conjecture. The recovery is not a retreat but a better sentence: Reich described a structure of conditional holding and named it by analogy (feudal tenure, 769–770), and he *demanded* procedure against a state of affairs he found procedureless (783). The chapter's claim then becomes that the demand went unmet at private doors — which is true, checkable, and stronger than a misattributed naming.

The four powers are the chapter's own, and should be announced as such in the first paragraph that uses them. That announcement is cheap and it forecloses the two objections a referee will otherwise raise: that a reader who knows Reich's 740 passage will think the chapter has misread it, and that Cohen already ran a four-part Hohfeldian taxonomy of platform entitlements in 2017. Meet Cohen by name, concede the overlap on entry-control, and note that her powers of interdiction run *against* platforms rather than from them.

The private-doors extension is the chapter's own move, and Reich half-licensed it twice — once in a scope-limiting sentence at 786, and once, much more strongly, at 302 of the 1990 retrospective. Cite 302. Then own the step, because Peñalver is right that the population dependent on private counterparties drops out of the 1964 article, and Van Alstyne's overstates/understates verdict, though addressed to the public sector, states the general risk of building on a property metaphor.

The boundary section writes itself and should not be softened. In the EU a business user has been owed reasons since July 2020; a hosting service has owed a content-triggered statement since February 2024; and from December 2026 a platform worker's deactivation must be taken by a human being with reasons on the day and a substantiated reply within a fortnight. In Seattle a network company has owed notice, records and an internal challenge since January 2025. Every one of these carries carve-outs the dossier prints, and the chapter should print them too — the P2B repeat-infringement exemption, the DSA's content trigger, Article 11(5)'s disapplication to business users, and the Seattle enforcement gap running to mid-2027. Stated with its limits, "owes no procedure" is a claim of US constitutional law about non-business users at non-EU doors, and it is still worth making.

Finally, the negative result deserves a paragraph of its own rather than a footnote. Nine platform-law articles and the field's most comprehensive treatment of deplatforming cite Reich zero times. Pasquale reaches a private-sovereign structure through occasional feudal imagery and never names him. Guggenberger runs platform property through Calabresi and Melamed. Rahman runs it through public utility. Cohen runs it through Hohfeld. The chapter is not joining a Reichian conversation about platforms; it is proposing one. That is a better thing to be doing, and it costs nothing to say so.
