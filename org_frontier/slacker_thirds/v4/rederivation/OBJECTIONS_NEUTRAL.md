# Design constraints, stripped of any prior draft's vocabulary

Drawn from review objections against an earlier chapter on Richard Linklater's *Slacker* (1991)
— a chapter this document does not describe. Stated as requirements any new design must satisfy,
not criticism of a design you cannot see. Some were "satisfied" only by renaming something; that
does not count. A reviewer's "fatal" means failing it is fatal.

---

## 1. A sorting procedure has to actually sort

If the design classifies coordinating third parties into two or more kinds via some procedure —
a test, questions, a checklist — that procedure must produce, applied honestly, the verdict the
prose itself asserts for a shared set of paradigm cases: a real historical city, an informal
access norm (a door policy), a film camera, a ride-hailing platform, and an online creative-work
submission page. It must not disagree with the prose on any case, and must not return the same
verdict for every instance of representation — film, novel, broadcast, painting — since a
procedure answering that way everywhere discriminates nothing. Failure mode: a question like
"could this have happened without the thing in question?" is vacuous for any mediating device by
definition, so it misclassifies every representational case alike. Satisfies it: different
questions sorting different kinds — some separating cases where an in-reach environment does the
work, others where a specific pairing-in-sequence does — with an account of why they point
opposite ways. The trap: redefining the procedure's job as "locating" a difference already
settled by definition, rather than classifying, without changing what it returns on hard cases.

## 2. A two-kind distinction has to work at both ends, not just the middle

If the design distinguishes two kinds of coordinating third, the distinction must do real
explanatory work across its whole range, not only at the interesting middle case. It cannot, at
one end, relabel a concept already established in prior scholarship (a city's role in chance
encounters); at the other end it cannot be inert, if the interesting facts there (pricing a
transaction, evaluating or removing someone) don't actually depend on which kind of third it is.
These capacities look like they belong to one kind, but are properties of market power,
governance, and law — a market square can charge rent and have someone removed; a door can
select and exclude — so they cross-cut any two-kind sorting. Satisfies it: treating "which kind"
and "what governing capacities it holds" as separate claims, acknowledging non-selecting
environments can hold governing capacities too. The trap: leaving this as an implicit hedge
rather than a stated concession.

## 3. Attributed powers must follow from the definition, not the examples

If particular powers are attributed to one kind of third as following from its definition, those
powers must be entailed by the defining criterion, not merely common among the examples
illustrating it. "Chooses which two parties come together" does not entail "can set a price" or
"can expel a party": those are separable facts about market position, contract, and law that
things which don't select pairs at all can also hold. A payoff claim — "here is what this kind
can do to you that the other can't" — cannot rest on capacities the classification doesn't
predict. Satisfies it: an explicit concession, inside the argument, that the extra capacities
aren't entailed by the defining criterion, with a counterexample that doesn't choose pairs yet
still prices a transaction and can exclude someone. The trap: proposing the concession in a side
note and never writing it into the running text.

## 4. Which city — unanswered in every prior round

If the design grounds its argument in a real historical place at a specific moment (documented
population, economic, market facts), it must decide, explicitly, whether those facts are
evidence that coordination happened in the real place, or background conditions for a
constructed, edited depiction assembled by a fictional work. These are different claims; using
real-world data to support a claim about mechanism inside fiction needs naming.

Raised once, in an early review round, never resolved, reopened, accepted, or declined
afterward — unlike every other objection here, which recurs somewhere in the record. Treat this
as genuinely open ground. Satisfies it: naming the slide between the real place and its
fictional version openly, and conceding that if the coordination claim depends on the
constructed version, the argument concerns how the fiction is assembled, not how the real city
functioned. The trap: using vivid, well-sourced detail about the real place as if it established
coordination, when it may only establish conditions the fiction drew on.

## 5. What a "learned skill that doesn't transfer" section is for — a live question, not a defect

If a design argues (a) that some way of reading a medium is a learned skill, acquired through
exposure, and then (b) that the skill probably doesn't transfer to the competence the argument
cares about, it must decide, deliberately, what that section accomplishes — not show its own
premise is irrelevant and move on as though something still followed from it. Two real options
exist: narrow to a claim the section can support (one work trained its audience to notice
something narrower than the opening promise), or turn it into a critical finding in its own
right (the competence is one of following an arrangement someone else made, not of contesting
it, which darkens the ending). Choose on purpose — don't keep the broader promise while the
section's own evidence points narrower or opposite. The trap: restating the shrug in more
confident language, or letting a broad-transfer claim stand once the evidence has undercut it.

## 6. A coordination claim needs a stated falsifier

If the design claims some entity — place, medium, platform — "coordinates" the people passing
through it, that claim needs a falsifier: a case with the relevant features (density, shared
presence, legibility) where coordination demonstrably does not occur, plus a reason the case
under discussion falls on the other side. Without one, "coordinates" risks restating a fact true
of any populated, legible environment. Satisfies it: one falsifying case plus one specific thing
the claim buys beyond existing accounts of density and agglomeration. The trap: conceding the
real contribution is placing an existing idea in new vocabulary — relocation, not mechanism —
while still presenting it as explaining something new.

## 7. Engage the closest prior art directly, don't just narrow around it

The novelty claim must directly engage the scholarship closest to making the same move: an
established debate in the sociology of third parties (German-language in origin) that already
names a concrete-versus-abstract distinction among them, and platform/media-studies work
deliberately treating matching people and directing their attention as one unified function,
plus further work finding the two coexist within individual real platforms rather than sorting
onto separate ones. Satisfies it: citing and engaging both. The trap: narrowing an unverifiable
"nobody has done this" claim into a smaller, checkable one, and treating that as if it also
discharged the duty to cite anticipating or complicating work.

## 8. Decide whether withholding a reason is its own capability

If the design enumerates a fixed list of capabilities a coordinating third can hold (pricing,
evaluating, removing a participant), and later leans on one not on that list — declining to
explain a decision — as central evidence, it must decide whether that capability is distinct or
already covered, and revise the list to match. Satisfies it: an explicit yes-or-no in the
running text, the list revised accordingly, and if yes, an account of where a boundary case (a
device that also never explains an individual choice, like a cut in a film) sits relative to it.
The trap: fixing a sentence's grammar — what a pronoun refers to — without declaring whether the
capability belongs on the list.

## 9. "Closure" claims need a dated fact, not a softer title

If the design argues a distribution channel became gatekept or closed over time — rather than
superseded by a different channel — it needs a dated, verified fact showing tightened access
terms (e.g., a shift from open to invitation-only submission). Retitling a section to avoid
asserting closure, while keeping evidence that only supports a weaker replacement story, does
not satisfy this. Absent the fact, make the replacement claim explicitly and address the
counter-evidence — a middle industry tier refilling, overall growth in works reaching audiences.
Satisfies it: the dated fact, or an argued switch to replacement framing that confronts the
counter-evidence. The trap: softening a title so it no longer technically asserts closure while
surrounding claims still lean on closure being true.

## Already settled — restated neutrally, do not re-solve

**Internal consistency of platform evidence.** An early claim that a matching platform directly
reads both parties at the moment of matching was reconciled with a weaker later one: the
strongest evidence for close reading arrives after a match (pricing, rating, removal), not while
forming it.

**A framing device coordinates its own audience too.** That a film camera doesn't just transmit
but actively coordinates what its audience encounters and when was conceded directly, moved
earlier into the classification discussion, and paid off: coordinating an audience and
coordinating people who must live with being paired are different jobs sharing little beyond
both being a form of choosing.

**Scope the novelty claim to what's checkable.** An unverifiable "nobody has done this" claim
was narrowed to something checkable — a small, named set of reviews don't cite a particular
tradition. Independent of constraint 7: narrowing a claim's scope doesn't substitute for
engaging traditions that anticipate it.
