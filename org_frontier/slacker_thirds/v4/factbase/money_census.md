# The money census — how *Slacker*'s people get money

A second census of the same transcript, run on the same protocol as the seam census: two
coders, blind sheets, disagreements published rather than reconciled, kappa computed from
row-level codes. Its purpose is to put evidence under a historical claim that was otherwise
an assertion — that 1990 Austin coordinated its inhabitants without coordinating their work.

**A prediction was recorded before the count so the count could refute it.** It did.

## The prediction, and its refutation

**Predicted, before the count:** the only people working in this film are the ones staffing
its coordinating positions — the taxi driver, the café counter, the doorman — while
everyone else pitches, trades, or refuses work.

**The coordinating half holds.** Every coordinating position in the film is staffed and
audibly working: the taxi (1), the café counter (11), the bar counter (25), the door (28,
30), the venue staff clearing the room at closing (31). No such position is shown
unattended. The one apparent exception is a boundary artifact — at segment 29 the cover is
reported by a patron rather than by staff, and the door's own voice arrives one line later,
in segment 30.

**The exclusivity half fails, five times over.** People are working who coordinate nothing:
the T-shirt and magazine seller ("Can I sell you a shirt?… Buy this magazine"), the
newspaper vendor with a "Two-for-one special," the children selling Cokes at "Half price,"
the store security supervisor ("One of my employees saw you put something in the bag that
you haven't paid for"), who also hands off a shift, and the two police officers on duty at
the accident. Three separate segments of street vending cannot be absorbed into "staffing a
coordinating position," and the prediction survives only by using two escape hatches at
once: refusing to count self-employed vending as work *and* stretching "coordinating
position" to cover police and loss prevention. Under either reading alone it fails.

The prediction's second clause also overreaches. Only two segments refuse work, twelve or
thirteen show no relation to paid work at all, and eight only discuss it.

## The finding the count produced instead, which is better than the prediction

**Nobody in this film looks for a job.** Seeking is zero across all thirty-five segments, in
both codings. The only job search anywhere in the transcript is narrated on a postcard, in
the third person, about a man who has already left town: "So now he's looking for a job that
doesn't involve much work." The one direct question about getting one is deferred to "the
true call."

And the work that does appear divides cleanly in two, with nothing between: staffing a
position that coordinates other people, or selling something hand to hand in the street.
There is no workplace in this film beyond one store and a few counters, and no employer is
named except "the security in the store."

That is the evidence §3 needs, and it is stronger than what was predicted. A city can
organize encounter without organizing employment, and this one does. The claim no longer
rests on the absence of work — which was false — but on the absence of anyone looking for
it, which both coders confirm across every segment.

## Boundary artifacts, flagged not hidden

Both coders independently flagged the same seam the earlier census flagged: line 1404,
"Five-dollar cover," sits in segment 30 by the ledger's ranges while completing segment 29's
stamp trick. Moving that one line flips segment 30 to none/none and segment 29 to
performing/priced-access. Coder 1 flagged a second: segment 25's only performing evidence is
a drinks order at lines 1187–1193, inside 25's range but inside the bar scene the ledger
describes under 26.

The ledger also dates the transcript's last line at 1518; the file has 1517 lines.

## Coder notes worth carrying

The most consequential single coding decision is whether self-employed street vending counts
as performing paid work. It moves three of the ten performing codes. Both coders counted it,
and both said so.

Coder 1 corrects the scene ledger on segment 11: the ledger calls the change-asker a
panhandler, but the transcript has him say he has a dollar and wants change for it — and the
"Get a job" retort lands on him.
