# Money census — *Slacker* (1991), coder 1

Independent coding of all 35 segments on two dimensions, WORK and EXCHANGE, against the fixed
scheme. Sources: `research/slacker_transcript.md` (subtitle-derived, 1,518 lines, no speaker
names, no stage directions) and `v4/factbase/scene_ledger.md` (segment numbering and line
ranges). No chapter draft, no `v4/rederivation/` file, and no prior census was opened before or
during this count.

**Quoting convention.** All evidence is verbatim. ` / ` marks a line break in the transcript;
`- ` marks a speaker change as the subtitle file marks it. The transcript's own ellipses,
spellings, and run-together words are reproduced as they stand.

**Scope limit.** Only dialogue was available. No claim below rests on what anyone is visibly
doing. Where a code turns on knowing who is speaking, the Note says so.

### Every EQUIVOCAL

- **Segment 12, WORK.** An unidentified questioner conducts a professionally shaped interrogation

- **Segment 2, EXCHANGE.** A card pressed on an accident scene with "call me" is either a

### Does the prediction hold?

The prediction: *the only people working in this film are the ones staffing its coordinating
positions — the taxi driver, the café counter, the doorman — while everyone else pitches, trades,
or refuses work.*

**It holds only partly. Its second half is confirmed and stronger than stated; its first half —
the exclusivity claim — fails.**

What holds. Every coordinating position the prediction names is audibly working, without
exception. The taxi driver in segment 1 (by the passenger's words, since the driver never speaks),
the café counter in segment 11 and the bar counter at the tail of segment 25, the door in
segments 28 and 30, and the venue staff closing the room in segment 31. None of the film's
coordinating positions appears unstaffed. The one place a coordinating position is discussed
without anybody staffing it speaking is segment 29, where a patron reports the cover — "Uh, I
think it's five bucks" — and the group then routes around the door with a licked hand stamp; the
door worker's voice arrives in the next segment ("Five-dollar cover"). That is a gap in the
dialogue, not an unstaffed post.

The second half holds and then some. Fourteen segments carry an exchange attempt — six sales,
five solicitations, three named prices — against **zero** segments coded SEEKING. Nobody in
*Slacker* looks for, applies for, or arranges a job for themselves. The two near misses both cut
the other way: the film's only job search is narrated on a postcard about an absent housemate
("So now he's looking for a job that doesn't involve much work," segment 7), and the only time
anyone is asked what it would take to get one, the answer defers it indefinitely ("I'll get a job...
when I hear the true call," segment 20). Even the injunction to seek work — "You've got a strong
back. Get a job." (segment 11) — is aimed at a man who was asking for change for a dollar, not
for a handout.

What fails. Five working parties are audible who staff no coordinating position:

1. **Officers Bozzio and Love (segment 2)** — "Officer Bozzio and Love with the police
   department," on duty at an accident. Public employment in an announced role.
2. **The T-shirt seller (segment 9)** — "Can I sell you a shirt? You know? / And it's like, look
   at this magazine. Buy this magazine."
3. **The newspaper vendor (segment 11)** — "- Paper? - No, thank you. / Two-for-one special." He
   works the same street as the café counter and coordinates nothing.
4. **The children selling Cokes (segment 14)** — "- Hey, you want a Coke? - Half price," with
   their own stock to haul: "Hey, come on, man. This shit's getting heavy."
5. **The store security supervisor (segment 21)** — "Uh, I'm with the security in the store. /
   One of my employees saw you put something in the bag that you haven't paid / for," preceded by
   a shift handoff, "- Hey, would you take over? - Okay, I got it."

Segment 21 is the case that will not go away. He announces his employer, claims subordinates,
and hands off a post mid-shift, all in words. Nothing about a department store's loss-prevention
desk is a coordinating position in the sense the prediction uses — it regulates who may leave
with goods, not who may pass. Segment 2 is nearly as hard: police at an emergency are waged
public employees, and if "coordinating position" is stretched wide enough to absorb them it
stops distinguishing anything.

The prediction survives only if two escape hatches are used at once: refusing to count
self-employed street vending as work (which removes 9, 11, and 14) *and* reading "coordinating
position" broadly enough to absorb the police and the store detective (which removes 2 and 21).
Under either reading alone it fails. Under the narrow reading of "coordinating position" that the
prediction's own three examples imply, it fails five times over.

One further correction to the shape of the claim. The prediction implies that work in this film
is concentrated in the positions that manage passage. The count shows something more specific:
work in *Slacker* is concentrated in the positions that manage *access to something priced* —
the door, the counter, the store's goods, the cab's seat — and the three named prices in the film
(three dollars, five dollars, a five-dollar cover) all buy entry to the same club on the same
night. Selling and soliciting, by contrast, are spread across eleven segments and eleven
unrelated parties.

## Method note

**What counted as paid work.** PERFORMING was coded only where an audible line shows an activity
being done for money, or shows a speaker in an announced paid role. That admits four kinds of
evidence: an announced employer or office ("Officer Bozzio and Love with the police department";
"I'm with the security in the store"), an order taken or a price quoted in the role's own voice
("What do you need?"; "Three dollars"), a sale attempted in the moment ("Can I sell you a shirt?";
"Half price"), and, in segment 1 alone, a paid service named by its customer ("not taking a cab at
all"). Activity alone never sufficed.

**The ambiguous case: self-employed vending.** Segments 9, 11, and 14 were coded PERFORMING
because a person is pitching goods for money in the moment, which is income work whether or not
anyone employs them. The scheme's three examples of PERFORMING — the taxi, the counter, the door
— are all staffed positions, so a coder who reads PERFORMING as waged employment would code these
three as DISCUSSING or NONE and code only their EXCHANGE cells. This is the single decision most
likely to differ between coders, and it moves three of the ten PERFORMING codes. It does not move
the verdict, because segments 2 and 21 are waged employment on any reading.

**Burglary, and why it is not work.** Segment 23 shows a completed burglary and is coded NONE.
The line drawn: vending is an audible attempt to obtain money for goods; the burglary yields no
money, attempts no sale, and produces an object the thieves did not want ("It's a fuckin' book").
By the same rule, segment 24's standing order for stolen televisions is coded on the EXCHANGE
dimension as SOLICITING and not as anybody's job, since no payment is ever named for it.

**Unpaid labor that looks like work.** Four cases were coded NONE or DISCUSSING rather than
PERFORMING because no pay is named: the dictation taken in segment 4 (a play-acted secretarial
relation), the engine work in segment 18 (on cars the dialogue marks as the men's own), the
coffee Delia brings her father in segment 22 (counter service performed at home by a daughter),
and the staged ritual in segment 15 (assigned parts, a designated reader, no employer).

**People who talk about jobs they no longer hold.** The film has fewer of these than its
reputation suggests. Nobody in the transcript names a specific job they once held and have since
lost. What the film has instead is declarations of present non-employment — "Still unemployed"
(8), "I graduated a couple of years back. Just pretty much hanging out" (17), "See, I'm a drifter
from Texas" (12) — and one man who claims a profession he shows no sign of practising ("I'm a
medical doctor. / I own a mansion and a yacht," 11), and one who claims a war he did not fight,
debunked on the phone by his own daughter (22). The rule applied: a bare declaration of not
working was coded DISCUSSING when the speaker names their work status, and REFUSING only where
the speaker articulates a position against working (segments 4 and 20). "Still unemployed" is
therefore DISCUSSING, not REFUSING; "To hell with the kind of work you have to do to earn a
living" is REFUSING. Segment 12's "drifter" is coded NONE on that clause, since the word states
a condition without a position.

**Where the ledger's boundaries made the coding arbitrary.**

- **Segments 25 and 26.** The ledger places the bar scene under 26 but ends 25 at line 1195, so
  the drinks order at 1187–1193 — "Yeah, so what do you guys need?" — falls inside 25. That one
  line is the entire basis for segment 25's PERFORMING code. Move the boundary eight lines and 25
  becomes NONE and 26 becomes PERFORMING. This was the most arbitrary cell in the census.
- **Segments 29 and 30.** The cover charge is discussed in 29 and named by the door in 30, because
  the ledger's ranges split at line 1403/1404, one line before "Five-dollar cover." Segment 30 —
  the PixelVision party — consequently carries the door worker's only line.
- **Overlapping ranges.** The ledger's own ranges overlap in three places, and the shared lines
  were coded in both segments: 308–318 (segments 6 and 7, the postcard invitation), 809–815
  (segments 17 and 18, the "poppin' freeze plugs" garage talk that bleeds under the bookstore
  pitch), and 848–851 (segments 18 and 19, the lift into town, which supplies the SOLICITING code
  in both).
- **Segment 34** runs to line 1518, one past the inventory's stated end, as the ledger notes; the
  last line is coded here.
- **Segment 35** has no dialogue at all, so both its codes record the absence of evidence rather
  than a coded observation.

**Speaker attribution.** The transcript has no speaker names. Six codes depend on inferring who
is speaking, and each is flagged in its row: segment 1 (the driver never speaks), segment 2 (who
gives the card), segment 12 (who conducts the interrogation), segment 28 (who answers "Three
dollars"), segment 30 (who says "Five-dollar cover"), and segment 31 (who clears the room). In
every one of these the inference rests on what the line does — quote a price, check a list, order
a room emptied — rather than on any image.
