# Handoff census — coder 2

Independent coding of all 34 segment-to-segment transitions in *Slacker* (1991), against
`research/slacker_transcript.md` (subtitle-derived, no speaker names) and the 35-segment
boundaries in `v4/factbase/scene_ledger.md`. Two dimensions, coded independently. No image,
shot, or continuity evidence is used anywhere — only what the transcript records as audible.
Line numbers are the transcript file's own.

### Where the two dimensions disagree interestingly

- **1→2.** The only ECHO with agency NONE. The viewer hears a rhyme ("someone getting run over" / "ran over his mother") that no character makes, notices, or acts on. The film's connective work here is entirely the film's.
- **17→18.** Also ECHO + NONE, and by a different route: the next segment's dialogue is audible *before* the cut, so the link is maximal while the agency is nil. Nobody hands the film to the garage; the garage is simply already sounding.
- **4→5.** SPOKEN-BRIDGE + INCIDENTAL, the scheme's own example, and the film's cleanest instance of it: the bridge exists precisely because the link was not addressed to the person who used it.
- **7→8 and 16→17.** SPOKEN-BRIDGE + INCIDENTAL of a second kind. A character announces where he is going and the film follows him, but the encounter waiting there is produced by nobody — a stranger recognizes him, or approaches him over a book. The spoken line routes the camera, not the meeting.
- **28→29.** ECHO + INCIDENTAL sitting one line apart from a deliberate act that is not coded: the doorman's refusal is intentional and does put the group outside, but he intends no meeting, so the strongest act at the seam produces the transition without producing the encounter.
- **30→31.** SPOKEN-BRIDGE + DELIBERATE where the two codes rest on different people. The forward-pointing line ("catch that show") is spoken by one character about himself; the deliberate handoff is a camera passed by another.
- **No transition is NONE + DELIBERATE.** Every deliberate act in the film that produces a next encounter is also spoken aloud — there is no silent handoff in the dialogue record. The converse is common: nine of the fifteen NONE-on-both rows contain a deliberate act (an offer, an invitation, a stated destination) that the film declines to follow, which is why the two dimensions track each other closely while the *content* of the acts diverges.

## Method note

**What I treated as the seam.** The boundary between the last transcript line the ledger assigns
to segment *n* and the first line it assigns to segment *n+1*. Evidence is quoted from either
side, and the row records how far from the boundary it sits when that distance matters. Where a
segment's own range contains a line the ledger calls its "handoff" (25→26 is the clearest case),
I coded the actual boundary, not the ledger's description of it.

**Overlapping ranges.** Three pairs overlap by design: 6/7 at 308–318, 17/18 at 809–815, 18/19 at
848–851. For these there is no seam line, only a seam zone, and the bridging line belongs to both
segments. I coded from inside the overlap and said so in each row. The overlap does not by itself
create an audible link — 17→18 is coded ECHO because a topic sounds on both sides, not because
the numbers overlap.

**The rule I applied for ECHO.** The scheme allows "a word, phrase, or topic repeated or answered
across the seam." Applied without limit, thematic affinity would swallow most of the film, which
returns constantly to conspiracy, withdrawal, media, and departure. I required either a shared
word or phrase used in the same sense, or a named topic that is unmistakably the same referent.
Under this rule 1→2 counts (a run-over body is a run-over body) and 2→3 does not ("touch" in two
incompatible senses), and 33→34 does not (two monologues with no addressee is a shared form, not
a shared topic). A coder who counted thematic affinity would return a much higher ECHO count; a
coder who required lexical repetition near the seam would code 1→2 as NONE and return five.

**The rule I applied for agency.** DELIBERATE requires that a character intend the next encounter
— an offer, an invitation, an address, an object handed over. INCIDENTAL covers a character
carrying over into the next segment where contact happens without a handoff: overhearing,
approaching a stranger, walking out of one scene into another. NONE covers a cut to people no
character act connects to. Self-routing without a handoff ("I'm gonna go look at some books") is
coded INCIDENTAL, since the scheme's DELIBERATE examples are all transfers between characters.
This decision alone moves 7→8, 16→17, and 22→23.

**Where the ledger's boundaries made the coding arbitrary.**

- **5→6.** The ledger claims a verbatim repeat across this cut that its own ranges cannot produce.
  Coded EQUIVOCAL; not renumbered.
- **7→8, 12→13, 21→22, 28→29.** In all four the boundary falls inside what the transcript records
  as one continuous exchange, so the bridging or answering line lands on the later side of a seam
  the dialogue does not mark. Coded as the ledger defines them.
- **25→26.** The bar-service and card-drawing lines at 1187–1195 are audibly located in segment
  26's bar but assigned to segment 25. Any code here is an artifact of where that line is drawn.
- **11→12.** Line 550 belongs to no identifiable speaker; the ledger itself flags whether it
  belongs to the location at all.
- **30/31.** The ledger assigns the camera heckle at 1436 to segment 31 while describing it as the
  bridge out of segment 30. I coded it as a line on the later side answering back.

**Line numbering.** The ledger's line numbers match the transcript file exactly at every anchor I
checked (1133, 1436, 1497, and the segment openings). One exception: the ledger describes "the
transcript's final line, 1518" as "I see it. I'm gonna solve all these goddamn problems," but that
line is 1517 in the file and 1518 is blank. The discrepancy affects nothing above.

**Subtitle-derived wording.** Three codes depend on exact wording that subtitles may have altered
and should not be argued from until checked against the film: 19→20 (the verbatim repeat of "Hey,
you got a cigarette?" across the cut, which could be a duplication artifact), 29→30 ("I got a
