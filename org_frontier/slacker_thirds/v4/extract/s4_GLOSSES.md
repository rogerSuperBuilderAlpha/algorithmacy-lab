## Finding 2 — Berg and the "ordering axis"

- Lens 3's reading of why the "declined an ordering axis" claim fails: "Berg did not decline an ordering axis; his axis is temporal scrambling, and the chapter's 'ordered walk' — the sequence in which the camera visits people — is not a variable in his scheme at all." (Finding 2, lines 78–80)

## Finding 3 — Berg and the two-graphs move

- Verdict: Berg supports the chapter's story-world-graph reading (characters "come into contact with each other") but cannot support draft_v0's "no edges" claim, because his own sentence makes Linklater — not the camera, not nobody — the agent of the linking. (Finding 3, lines 128–143)
- Verdict: "He lists the film under a definition his own example arguably fails" — i.e., Berg's general Daisy Chain definition requires each story "presented fully, in turn," which *Slacker*'s vignettes are read as not meeting. (Finding 3, lines 142–143)
- Lens 1's added reading: Berg's placement of *Slacker* alongside Ophuls's *La Ronde* (built of successive diegetic encounters) "strengthens rather than weakens the reading that his chaining runs through contact between characters." (Finding 3, lines 163–165)

## Finding 4 — Bordwell 1985

- Verdict on the project's own draft: "The project note undersells Bordwell, and the shortfall lands on a load-bearing sentence." (Finding 4, lines 173–174)
- Recommendation: the function-list sentence ("it sometimes suppresses information, it often restricts our knowledge, it generates curiosity, it creates a tone") is characterized as "the chapter's best single piece of evidence that narration is what selects." Repeated later: "It is the best evidence in the section that narration is what selects." (Finding 4, line 178; "How this lands," lines 937–938)
- Judgment: reading Bordwell's p. 53 positive definition of narration as "the warrant for leading with the ordered walk rather than the star" is called "a drafting preference the sentence does not itself license" — i.e., the chapter may want to make that move, but Bordwell's text does not itself justify it. (Finding 4, lines 231–232)

## Finding 5 — the rival-accounts map

- Framing judgment: because Chatman's cinematic narrator is "already depersonalised" (a composite of communicating devices, explicitly said not to be a human being), the dispute between Chatman and Bordwell is reframed as being about "whether an impersonal system counts as an *agent*, not about whether the selecting is systematic." (Finding 5, lines 236–238)
- Judgment: the field is read as not being two camps of equal size — "little debate" among film scholars generally (narrator view predominant, Bordwell the exception) versus "very lively" debate among philosophers of film. The chapter is characterized as "leaning on the film-studies minority." (Finding 5, lines 239–241; "How this lands," line 944)
- Recommendation: "what those skeptics grant is narration by the actual filmmakers, not by any in-film agent — which is the fallback the chapter needs and cannot get from Bordwell." This routes the "fallback to real authors" move to Currie, Kania, and Thomson-Jones rather than to Bordwell. (Finding 5, lines 242–243)
- Verdict against adopting "presenter"/"present" as a neutral term: "Adopting 'presenter' as the hinge smuggles Chatman's conclusion into a sentence meant to stand between them," because Chatman introduces "to present" specifically to argue that presenting entails an agent, and a presenter is by that logic a sender — which is exactly what Bordwell denies exists. (Finding 5, lines 245–250)
- Judgment: "Bordwell as the standing exception" (full stop) is called "an over-reading produced by truncating Slugan's endnote"; the endnote's second half narrows this to film scholars specifically, not scholarship on narration generally. (Finding 5, lines 252–253)

## Finding 6 — Poulaki

- Verdict: because Poulaki "demotes the diegetic encounter in favour of informational linkage," the chapter's existing rebuttal ("nobody in *Slacker* meets twice") is judged to "aim at a weaker opponent than she is." (Finding 6, lines 313–314)

(Finding 6, lines 320–322)
- Quality assessment: lenses 1 and 2 are recorded as calling this "the cleanest-sourced claim in the set" — a comparative judgment about verification quality, not a fact about Poulaki's argument. (Finding 6, lines 325–326)

## Finding 10 — Münsterberg — THE HIGH-RISK GLOSS

**This is the exact interpretive move the task brief warns about ("an ally, not a rival").** Treat everything below as argument, not fact, and re-derive it from the primary Münsterberg quotations in CLAIMS (S4-083–S4-087) before using it anywhere downstream.

- Headline verdict, stated in the finding's own title: "Münsterberg 1916 is an ally of the training thesis, not the rival the claim proposed." (Finding 10 title, line 548)
- Supporting interpretation: "His claim is that the technique [the close-up] reproduces the *form* of an attentive act, not that the spectator controls what is shown." (Finding 10, lines 559–560)
- Restated verdict: "Chapter IV is one of the earliest statements of the training thesis, not a rival to it." (Finding 10, lines 560–561)
- Absence-judgment: "If the chapter wants a genuine rival to the training thesis, this run did not find one." (Finding 10, line 565; restated in "How this lands," lines 975–976: "This run found no genuine rival to the training thesis.")
- Directive built on the verdict: "He is an ally. Section 6 should recruit him; section 4 should stop looking for its rival there." ("How this lands," lines 974–975)
- Also struck as a misuse: reading the p. 146 sentence "True art has always been selection, but never selection of the beautiful elements in outer reality" as a "selector epigraph" — the dossier's own correction states this sentence is about an artist's choice of subject matter and "does not bear on who selects for the spectator." (Finding 10, lines 589–591) — this is itself a corrective gloss, included here because it is still an interpretive judgment about applicability, not a fact about the sentence's content.
