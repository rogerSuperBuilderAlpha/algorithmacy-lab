# Learning to Read the Machine: A Three-Wave Panel of Algorithmacy in a Developer Cohort

A three-wave panel of a sixteen-week developer cohort that coordinates through platforms it builds and a peer-review gate, developing a scale for algorithmacy as a lived competence and tracking whether it grows. The lab's first human-subjects measurement.

*Proposal and pre-registered protocol — front end through method, with the instrument as fielded. Data
collection opens Fall 2026; no data is in hand at the time of writing.*

---

**Abstract (108 words)**

Algorithmacy is the competence a worker uses to coordinate through an opaque mediating system:
reconstructing a hidden counterpart's wants from outcomes, compressing intent into the few signals the
system accepts, and tracking rule changes the system makes without announcement. The construct is drawn
from the lab's catalog of coordination priors and has never been measured on real workers. This study
fields a three-wave panel on a sixteen-week developer cohort whose members coordinate through platforms
they build and submit work through a peer-review gate. It develops and validates an Algorithmacy
Competence Scale, tests whether the competence grows over the program, and relates it to task
interdependence, perceived system authority, autonomy, psychological ownership, and perceived
substitutability. It is the lab's first human-subjects measurement.

---

## Fit

This question needs a longitudinal survey because it is about change in a competence over time and about
the relations among constructs as a person lives through a coordination arrangement, which a one-shot
model cannot show and a cross-sectional snapshot cannot separate from stable individual differences.
A panel that measures the same people before, during, and after a structured program of coordination is
the design the question calls for. The contribution follows: a measured construct where the lab has had
only a modeled one, and the first evidence about whether algorithmacy behaves as the catalog's account
of it predicts.

## The setting and why it fits the construct

The setting is the Hult Cohort Program, Fall 2026: a sixteen-week developer cohort in which participants
build production software and submit each project as a merged pull request. The program has the features
the construct needs. Participants coordinate through mediating systems — the project-management,
internal-communications, and public-showcase platforms the cohort itself builds and then runs on — so
the mediating system is real, used daily, and changes under them. Work is gated: a submission is
reviewed by peers who file a written review and then cast a private vote, and the gate decides what
advances. Coordination is dense and interdependent, and it leaves a complete record on GitHub. The
arrangement is the kind the catalog's peer-review and gate priors describe, and its participants are
workers reading a system they cannot fully see, which is what algorithmacy names.

The setting also bounds the claim. The cohort is small, self-selected, and technical, and a competence
measured there is a competence in that population. The study reports descriptive and within-person
change as its primary results and treats between-person inferential tests as exploratory, for reasons
the analysis plan states.

## The construct and the prior it is read against

Algorithmacy is defined in the lab's methods guide as a lived competence with three parts: a worker
reconstructs a hidden counterpart's wants from outcomes; compresses real intent into the few signals the
system accepts; and tracks rule changes the system makes without announcement (see
[`../../qualitative/METHODS.md`](../../qualitative/METHODS.md), method 8). The catalog's structural
account sets the prior the survey reads against. Three expectations follow from it, and each is held open
to being wrong.

First, the three parts should cohere. If algorithmacy is one competence, its three facets should load on
a common factor while remaining distinguishable, and the scale should hold its meaning across waves. A
finding that the facets do not cohere, or that one drops out, is a finding about the construct.

Second, the competence should grow under sustained coordination through a mediating system. Sixteen weeks
of reading a changing platform, inferring what reviewers want, and compressing work into what the gate
accepts is the kind of practice the construct says builds the competence. Flat or declining trajectories
would weigh against that account.

Third, the competence should track the conditions the catalog says make a mediating arrangement bind.
Where a participant reports the system commits a determination rather than conveying a signal, and where
task interdependence is high, the catalog expects the coordinating work — and the competence it
draws on — to be heavier. A null or reversed relation is informative against the prior.

## Research questions

**RQ1 (measurement).** Do the three named facets of algorithmacy — counterpart inference, signal
compression, and rule-change tracking — form a coherent, reliable, and longitudinally invariant scale?

**RQ2 (development).** Does algorithmacy competence change over a sixteen-week program of mediated
coordination, and in which direction?

**RQ3 (nomological relations).** How does algorithmacy competence relate to perceived task
interdependence, perceived system authority (commit versus convey), job autonomy, psychological
ownership over the built system, and perceived substitutability?

The hypotheses that operationalize these questions, their nulls, and their decision rules are
pre-registered in [`PRE_REGISTRATION.md`](PRE_REGISTRATION.md).

## Expected contributions

To the lab, the study turns a modeled construct into a measured one and supplies the first human-subjects
data on the side of the validation gap the computational arm cannot reach. A validated Algorithmacy
Competence Scale is an instrument the qualitative and field arms can carry into other settings, and a
growth result is the first evidence about whether the competence the catalog names behaves as named.

To the literature on algorithmic management, the study offers a worker-side competence measure. That
literature has documented the control a platform exerts and the information asymmetry it imposes
(Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016), and it has measured workers' fairness
and trust responses to algorithmic decisions (Lee, 2018). It has paid less attention to the skill
workers develop to coordinate through such systems, and has not measured it as a competence that grows.
A scale for that skill, validated and tracked over time, is the opening this study works in.

To practice, a measure of whether and how this competence develops in a training setting speaks to how
programs that put people to work through opaque systems might be designed, and to what they are teaching
beyond their stated curriculum.

## Literature review

Four conversations bear on this study, and reading them together locates what a measured construct can
add.

*Algorithmic management and the worker's side of it.* A body of work establishes that software systems
now perform functions of management — directing, evaluating, and disciplining workers — and that this
shifts control onto terrain workers contest through the system rather than with a manager (Kellogg et
al., 2020). The platform holds an information asymmetry: it sees both sides of a coordination and the
worker sees only outputs, so the worker infers the rules from results (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016).
Workers' responses to algorithmic decisions have been measured on dimensions of perceived fairness,
trust, and emotional reaction (Lee, 2018), and the management of work on labor platforms has been
characterized as a regime of tracking and nudging that workers learn to anticipate (Möhlmann, Zalmanson,
Henfridsson, & Gregory, 2021). This conversation establishes that workers coordinate through opaque
systems and learn to do so. It has described that learning rather than measured it as a competence with
a structure that can be scored and tracked, which is what this study builds.

*Sensemaking and reading a hidden counterpart.* Reconstructing what a system or a counterpart wants from
the traces it leaves is a sensemaking problem: acting into an ambiguous situation and reading the
feedback to revise an account of it (Weick, 1995). The first facet of algorithmacy — inferring a hidden
counterpart's wants from outcomes — is sensemaking under a particular constraint, where the counterpart
is partly machine and gives only outcomes. The sensemaking literature supplies the cognitive process the
facet draws on. It has not been brought to the case of a worker reading an algorithmic mediator as a
measured, developing skill.

*Task interdependence and coordination.* How tightly parties depend on one another to get work done is a
long-studied feature of coordination, and reciprocal interdependence — where each party's work feeds the
others' — has been distinguished from pooled and sequential forms and measured by self-report (Pearce &
Gregersen, 1991; Van der Vegt, Emans, & Van de Vliert, 2001), with consequences for how groups manage
their joint work (Wageman, 1995). The catalog's structural claim is that reciprocal coupling is what
makes a mediated arrangement irreducible, while one-way dependence factors. This conversation supplies a
validated measure of the interdependence the catalog says should matter, and lets the study test whether
the construct that the model treats as the source of irreducibility relates, in workers' reports, to the
competence of coordinating through it.

*Ownership, autonomy, and the worker's standing in the arrangement.* Two further constructs locate the
worker in the arrangement. Psychological ownership — the felt state that a target is "mine" — develops
through control over a target, intimate knowledge of it, and investment of self in it (Pierce, Kostova,
& Dirks, 2001; Van Dyne & Pierce, 2004), and a participant who builds and runs a platform has the
conditions for it. Job autonomy — discretion over methods, scheduling, and criteria — is a basic feature
of how work is experienced and is measured by established scales (Breaugh, 1985). The catalog's
substitutability prior adds a third: a worker one of several interchangeable copies can supply is
non-pivotal, and the arrangement factors. These constructs frame the worker's position as owner,
agent, and pivot or replacement, and the study measures each to place algorithmacy among them.

*The opening.* The algorithmic-management literature shows that workers learn to coordinate through
opaque systems but has not measured that learning as a structured, developing competence. The
sensemaking, interdependence, ownership, and autonomy literatures supply the processes and the
surrounding constructs but have not been assembled around a worker reading an algorithmic mediator. The
lab supplies a precise account of the competence and a catalog of structural priors about when a
mediated arrangement binds, all of it in-silico. This study enters that opening: it measures the
competence the lab names, on real workers, over time, and against the constructs the surrounding
literatures and the catalog say should matter.

## Method

The study is a pre-registered, three-wave longitudinal panel with an embedded scale-development and
validation component. The instrument, the hypotheses, the scoring, and the analysis plan are committed
to the repository before any response is collected.

*Design and timing.* Three survey waves track the program's structure. Wave 1 is a baseline, fielded
before the first working week, when participants have enrolled but not begun project work. Wave 2 is
fielded at week 4, after the first build project and its peer-review-and-vote week, when participants
have coordinated through the gate once. Wave 3 is fielded at the end of the sixteen-week session, after
the full sequence of projects, reviews, and votes. The wave spacing maps onto the program's own milestones
so that change is measured across phases of real coordination, not arbitrary intervals.

*Participants and setting.* The population is the enrolled Fall 2026 cohort. All enrolled participants
are eligible; participation is voluntary and is not a condition of enrollment or assessment. The cohort
is small (expected tens, not hundreds), self-selected into a competitive program, and technically
skilled, and the analysis plan treats the resulting sample size as a feasibility constraint rather than
a basis for well-powered between-person inference.

*Measures.* The instrument combines a purpose-built scale with established measures. The full item text,
response scales, reverse keys, and sources are in [`codebook.md`](codebook.md), and the instrument as
administered, wave by wave, is in [`instruments/`](instruments/).

- **Algorithmacy Competence Scale (purpose-built).** Three facets, each written to the lab's definition:
  counterpart inference, signal compression, and rule-change tracking. Items are generated from the
  construct definition following standard scale-development practice (Hinkin, 1998; Clark & Watson,
  1995), reviewed for content, and fielded at all three waves so the structure and its invariance can be
  examined.
- **Perceived task interdependence**, adapted from Pearce and Gregersen (1991) and Van der Vegt et al.
  (2001), with reciprocal interdependence the focal subscale.
- **Perceived system authority (commit versus convey)**, items written to the catalog's central
  distinction — whether the mediating system commits a determination the parties must heed or conveys a
  signal they act on alone — anchored in the algorithmic-decision-perception literature (Lee, 2018).
- **Job autonomy**, from Breaugh's (1985) work-autonomy scales (method, scheduling, and criteria
  autonomy).
- **Psychological ownership** over the built platform, adapted from Van Dyne and Pierce (2004).
- **Transactive memory system**, from Lewis (2003), measuring specialization, credibility, and
  coordination in the cohort.
- **Perceived substitutability (purpose-built, exploratory)**, short items on how replaceable a
  participant feels in the cohort's coordination, written to the catalog's substitutability prior.
- **General self-efficacy**, from Chen, Gully, and Eden (2001), as a covariate and discriminant check.
- **Algorithmic competency**, twelve items adapted from Zhou, Lei, Liu, Huang, and Hou (2025), at Wave 2
  only, as the discriminant test against the one published rival measure of competency in
  algorithmically mediated work. Their scale was built on Chinese ride-hailing and food-delivery
  workers, so the items keep their dimensions and verbs and change only the gig-specific referents;
  every substitution is recorded beside the original in the codebook. Algorithmacy claims an axis their
  items never touch — coordination *with a human counterpart* through the system, where all twelve of
  theirs are worker-to-system — and H4 is where that claim meets data.
- **Sense of belonging** to the cohort, adapted from Walton and Cohen (2007), as a covariate.
- **Demographics and background** at Wave 1, and program-experience and open-response items at Wave 3.

*Procedure.* Surveys are administered on the cohort platform the participants already use. A participant
who is enrolled and signed in is shown the open wave during its window, gives informed consent before the
first wave, and completes the items. Responses are linked across waves by a one-way hash of the
participant's GitHub handle, so the panel can be assembled without storing the handle beside the
responses. A wave that closes is no longer accepted. Reminders are sent during each window. The platform
implementation, the storage schema, and the consent flow are described in the program repository's site
documentation; this directory holds the instrument and the design.

*Analysis.* The measurement model is examined first: factor structure of the Algorithmacy Competence
Scale (exploratory at Wave 1, confirmatory at Waves 2 and 3), reliability by McDonald's ω, and
longitudinal measurement invariance (configural, metric, scalar) before any mean comparison across waves
(Meredith, 1993; Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). Change is modeled with latent growth or multilevel models
with waves nested in persons (Bollen & Curran, 2006). Nomological relations are estimated within and
between persons. Missing data are handled by full-information maximum likelihood under a
missing-at-random assumption, with attrition examined and reported (Enders, 2010). The full model
specifications, the decision rules, the small-sample stance, and the common-method-bias safeguards
(Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003) are in [`analysis_plan.md`](analysis_plan.md).

## Ethics and human-subjects protection

The study is conducted under the Bentley University Institutional Review Board (IRB#1, FWA00007335),
which is the board of record; the Hult Cohort Program is the field site. The board determined the
protocol (registered title "Algorithmacy: Validating a measure of communication competency in AI-mediated
work," IRB# 260511078) exempt under 45 CFR 46.102(e)(2)(ii) on May 11, 2026, on the basis that disclosure
of responses outside the research would not place participants at risk of liability or harm to their
financial standing, employability, educational advancement, or reputation. Participation is voluntary,
may be withdrawn at any time without penalty, and has no bearing on a participant's standing, assessment,
or place in the cohort. Informed consent is obtained before the first wave; the consent form is in
[`instruments/consent.md`](instruments/consent.md). Responses are confidential, linked across waves only
by a one-way hash, stored separately from identifying records, and reported in aggregate or de-identified
form. Only de-identified, aggregated, or board-approved material is committed to this public repository.
Because the exemption is tied to the documents the board reviewed, a substantial change to the consent,
the instrument, or the design requires a Research Progress & Review Form to the IRB before fielding.

## Limitations and what would weigh against the account

The sample is small, self-selected, and technical, so estimates are imprecise and external validity is
narrow; the study is honest that its between-person tests are underpowered and treats them as
exploratory. Self-report measures one thing — how participants experience and describe the arrangement —
and a participant's report that the system decides is not a measurement of irreducibility on a model. The
purpose-built scales are new, and their structure is a hypothesis the data may not support. Three waves
fix the shape of any growth curve to a line or a single bend, no more. Common method variance is a risk
where constructs are measured by the same self-report at the same time, and the analysis plan states the
procedural and statistical safeguards against it. A flat algorithmacy trajectory, a scale that does not
cohere or does not hold its meaning across waves, or a null relation to interdependence and perceived
system authority would each weigh against the catalog's account of the construct, and each is reported as
found.

The study measures self-report and stops there. Linking these measures to the structural Φ verdict or to
the behavioral cross-recurrence measures the lab computes on the same cohort's GitHub record is a
separate study, noted here as a path rather than a claim.
