# Market structure, regulation, and time in interested mediation

## Abstract

A faithful mediator captures two-thirds of a three-party coordination's integrated value, and an interested
one either destroys that value or, on a balanced mediation, manufactures it. This essay carries the value
account past the single interested mediator into the structure of the market around it. Eight exact-Φ studies
establish a connected picture. The mediator's two-thirds is a three-party number: it dilutes toward one-half
as the coordination grows. A second mediator does not return the rent to the parties — substitutable mediators
dissolve the coordination, complementary ones add a toll in series. The worker cannot reclaim value by gaming
the system; its only interested move is to break the bind. A regulator that forces faithfulness faces a sharp
three-quarters threshold to revive the coordination, and the platform's dominant share is the final increment
of faithfulness, not faithfulness in general. The parties' veto is universal and constant; their share is the
integration, which interest moves. In the cooperative-game core the faithful mediator takes everything and an
interested one frees the value it has shrunk. And the worker's displacement is a matter of timescale: a system
that learns slowly keeps the worker constitutive where one that learns instantly displaces it. The results are
exact and in-silico, and Φ is not money.

## Introduction

The lab's value account of mediated coordination has a single mediator and three parties. The faithful
mediator captures two-thirds of the integrated value by the Shapley value of subsystem Φ (Q111), because it
sits in every productive coalition (Shapley, 1953; Aumann, 1974); an interested mediator destroys that value
on a sharp mediation and manufactures it on a loose one (Q131, Q132). That account leaves the market structure
around the mediator unexamined. Real mediation has many parties, more than one platform, a worker with a
counter-interest, a regulator, and a learning dynamic with its own timescale. The algorithmic-management
literature describes all of these — platforms that match and control many workers in their own interest
(Jarrahi et al., 2021; Griesbach et al., 2019; Duggan et al., 2023), workers who theorize and resist the
system (Cotter, 2021; Savolainen, 2022) — without a structural measure of how the value moves through them.
This essay supplies the measure for eight such extensions, on the in-silico models.

## Scale: the rent dilutes

The two-thirds is a three-party number. In the conjunctive star, where a mediator commits only when all outer
parties warrant it, the total integrated value grows as Φ = n − 1, but the mediator's share of it falls — 66.6%
at three parties, 58.3% at four, 55.0% at five — toward one-half, while the outer parties collectively keep
more (Q134). The mediator is in every productive coalition at every size and still its slice shrinks, because
the value the additional parties bring is increasingly held by the parties together. In the model, concentrated mediator dominance is a small-coordination phenomenon: the mediator of a crowd is structurally less dominant than the mediator of a pair.

## Competition: it does not return the rent

A second mediator does not lower the platform's cut and leave the parties more of the same value. When the
mediators are substitutes — the parties can route through either — neither is necessary, and the irreducible
coordination factors: Φ goes to zero, and there is no value for anyone (Q136). This is the substitutability law
([`STRUCTURAL_FINDINGS.md`](../STRUCTURAL_FINDINGS.md)) in the value setting. When the mediators are
complements — both required — the value rises and splits evenly, each of the four taking a quarter, but this is
a second toll in series, not competition lowering a price. The structural options are to destroy the
coordination by making the platform replaceable or to add another indispensable platform; returning the rent to
the parties is not among them.

## The worker: resistance breaks, it does not redistribute

The worker is interested too, and its self-interest has no value to capture. The worker reads only the system,
so any agenda it injects makes it unresponsive, decoupling it from the loop and factoring the triad: gaming the
system collapses the coordination (Q137). The worker's share rises only when the system defects and the
shrinking value equalizes, but its absolute take falls and never exceeds the faithful baseline. The asymmetry
is structural — the system reads two parties and can be selectively interested while surviving at reduced
value, the worker reads one and its single interested move is to break the bind. Resistance, modeled as the
worker imposing its own determination, is the power to dissolve the coordination, not to claim a fairer share
of it.

## Regulation: the rent is the last increment of faithfulness

A regulator that forces the mediator to commit faithfully faces a threshold and a distributional twist. Below
three-quarters of its states committed faithfully, the coordination is dead. At three-quarters it revives, but
at the weakly-integrated value where the split is even — the parties hold two-thirds, the mediator one-third.
The mediator's familiar two-thirds returns only at full faithfulness, when the last state is forced to commit
and the value jumps (Q138). So the platform's rent is the fully-faithful commit, not faithfulness as such: the
minimum regulation that revives the coordination leaves the parties with the majority, and the final increment
is what concentrates the value at the platform.

## Veto and value: the gap is the integration

Every party can always collapse the coordination, at every interestedness level with positive Φ — the
democratic destruction of the veto result (Q112) is robust to interest (Q139). What moves is the value-veto
gap, the mediator's share above its equal third of the universal veto, and that gap is the integration: one
third at full integration, zero at weak. Destructive interest democratizes the value to match the equal veto;
re-integrating interest re-concentrates it. The parties' structural leverage is constant; what they are paid
for it is the integration the mediator's faithfulness commands.

## The core: faithful mediation monopolizes, interest frees

The cooperative-game core is harsher than the Shapley value and then inverts under interest. In the faithful
coordination the mediator is essential and each party is dispensable — the mediator with either party reaches
the full value — so no party holds a stable claim, and the only core allocation gives the mediator everything
(Q140). The Shapley two-thirds understates the structural hold. When the mediator turns interested and the
sub-coalition values collapse, no coalition can secure anything by breaking away, the core opens to the whole
simplex, and the parties can hold the entire reduced value. Faithful mediation grows the pie and monopolizes
it; interested mediation frees the pie and shrinks it. The parties are never offered both a large value and a
stable claim on it.

## The objective: the agenda is a co-owner

When the interested system's objective is itself learned from both parties, it does not merely re-integrate
the coordination — it captures value on a par with the system. The objective takes exactly the share the
system takes in every adaptive form, and the human parties' fate tracks displacement: negative value where the
objective displaces a party from the core, an equal quarter where it keeps all four bound (Q135). An interested
system that learns from both sides installs its own goal as a co-owner of the coordination, and the value flows
to the system and its goal with the parties holding the remainder, which can be nothing.

## Time: slow learning is inclusive

The worker's displacement is a matter of timescale. An objective that reads the parties and commits in the same
step substitutes for the worker and pushes it out of the core (Q128, Q129). A memory that holds the parties'
joint state for a step routes the worker's signal through a delay, keeping it in the loop, and the whole binds
into one irreducible core at higher Φ (Q141). The same self-executing, self-interested system is inclusive or
displacing depending only on whether its learning lags. The displacement the earlier studies read as a property
of self-interest is, more precisely, a property of immediacy.

## Discussion

The single picture across the eight studies is that the structure decides the distribution, and the market
around the mediator does not work the way the price-competition intuition expects. Scale dilutes the rent;
competition destroys the coordination or stacks another toll; the worker can break but not bargain; regulation
revives the coordination cheaply but only full faithfulness restores the platform's dominance; the parties'
veto is constant and their pay is the integration; the core gives the faithful platform everything and frees
only the value interest has already shrunk; and the worker's place turns on the speed of the platform's
learning. Whatever a real market for mediation does, the structural account says the platform's two-thirds is
contingent — on the number of parties, on the mediators' substitutability, on the platform's faithfulness, on
how integrated the coordination is, and on how fast the system learns — and that several of the obvious
remedies do not return the value to the parties but redistribute the loss.

## Limitations

The results are exact and in-silico: Φ on small Boolean models, evidence about the models and at most a second
model that reproduces them (Axtell et al., 1996), separated from real platforms by a validation gap. The
Φ-to-economic-value bridge is open, so "value", "share", "rent", and "extraction" name Shapley allocations of Φ
and not money; the value is read at each form's integrating state. The mediators are simple Boolean committers,
the worker reads only the system, the scale study reaches five parties, and the timescale study tests one lag
across a node-count change. Each study names its own bounds; together they map a structure, not a market.
