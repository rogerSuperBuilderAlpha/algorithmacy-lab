# q180 — Individuation and the Party Boundary

A coded account of a coordination names parties before it measures anything. The coder decides
that a system is one party or two, that a matcher and a pricer are separate roles or one role
playing two parts. Phi is computed on whatever node set the coding produces. The boundary choice
comes first, and this study asks whether it changes the verdict.

It does. Across a synthetic palette of seven accounts, splitting one coded party into two sub-nodes
flips the dyadic/triadic verdict for 19 of 42 cases, a flip rate of 0.452. The pre-registered line
was 20 percent and the null was 5 percent. Individuation is not a free choice for the instrument.

The split operator comes in two modes. A function-changing split severs the party's contribution
by clamping its output. Every triadic account flips to dyadic under it, because clamping removes
the integration the verdict reads. A re-aggregable split keeps the party's joint input-output
function: both sub-nodes carry the original rule and downstream reads them through AND, which
returns the unchanged value. The split merges back to the base TPM exactly, checked numerically for
every party of every account.

A re-aggregable split should leave the verdict alone. It mostly does, holding the verdict in 11 of
15 triadic cases. It flips the other 4. The flips are the or_triad family and one xor_triad case.
Splitting an OR or XOR hub into two AND-aggregated copies changes which states the system reaches,
and the dropped state was the one carrying the integration. Function-preserving individuation can
still move the verdict by changing the reachable state space. This is a real effect of the coding
operator, and the instrument exposes it.

The rule_to_phi confidence interval was built to flag this. A panel of coders reads each split,
most taking the canonical reading and a seeded minority mis-reading the case as the other mode.
The per-coder Phi readings are propagated to a CI. When the CI crosses 0 the account cannot be told
apart from dyadic. For function-changing splits the CI crosses 0 in 13 of 15 cases; for
re-aggregable splits, 5 of 15. The CI separates the two modes by direction and degree.

The separation does not clear the bar fixed in H2. The hypothesis asked for the function-changing
CI to cross 0 in more than 90 percent of cases, and the rate is 0.867. Two function-changing splits
leave residual Phi on a node other than the clamped party, enough to hold their CI above 0. H2 is
refuted on its threshold. The construct works in the intended direction and the gap to the
threshold is two cases, but the number is the number.

The result for the field program is that the count of coded parties is a measurement decision, not
a neutral preliminary. Two coders who agree on every rule but disagree on how many nodes the
account has can produce different verdicts, and the CI registers that disagreement as Phi spread.
The instrument does not hide the boundary choice; it prices it.

## Scope and validation gap

All accounts are synthetic coded rule sets. No worker is measured, and no real coordination is
coded here. The flip rates and CI behavior are properties of the split operator and the panel
construct on this palette. The validation gap is the move from synthetic rule sets to coded
accounts of observed coordination, which this study does not make.
