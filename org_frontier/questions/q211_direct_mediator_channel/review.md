# Q211 — Does a direct mediator-to-mediator channel merge two triads into one core, where a shared counterpart could not? · Stage 1 review

**Question.** When two complete conjunctive triads are joined only by a direct channel between their two
mediators, does the major complex merge into one core that spans both triads, in the way a single shared
counterpart did not?

**Agenda id.** Follow-on from #16 (q210), the complementary link the q210 discussion named.

## Prior probes that bear on this

| probe | finding | how it relates |
|---|---|---|
| #364 (q210) | two triads sharing one counterpart do not merge; the major complex stays one local triad at Φ=2.0 under every bridge rule | the negative result this question turns around: the shared link was a peripheral counterpart, and it was too weak |
| #73 (multi-role) | a single node carrying two roles can pull two cores together or hold them apart | shows merger is possible in principle through the right shared structure |
| single-triad reference | the conjunctive triad reads triadic at Φ=2.0 | the per-triad baseline each core is measured against |

## The gap

q210 established that two triads sharing one peripheral party — a counterpart that both mediators feed —
never merge: the indirect mediator-counterpart-mediator path does not integrate the two coordinations, and
the maximal complex stays one local triad at the single-triad value. The open question, named in the q210
discussion, is whether the merger fails because the link is *indirect* or because a single shared party is
simply too weak. The direct test replaces the shared counterpart with a channel between the two mediators
themselves: each mediator reads the other directly. If a direct mediator-mediator channel merges what a
shared counterpart could not, the obstacle in q210 was the indirectness of the path, not the principle that
two triads can fuse. If it also fails to merge, two self-contained triads resist fusion through any single
binary link.
